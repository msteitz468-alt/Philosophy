# Sartre vs Freud — Lucidity vs the Unconscious
> Both seek to understand the human from subjectivity and hold that the human gives itself its own form — yet they split irreconcilably over whether the mind harbours an *unconscious* or is wholly *lucid*.

## The Tension

Sartre's lifelong need to distance himself from Freud, Welten notes, betrays how much the two share: both understand the human from *subjectivity* (against objectifying psychology), and both hold that the human *makes itself* (Sartre's "project"/"original choice" answers Freud's developmental self-formation). The break is over a single thesis.

### Position A — Freud: the unconscious
The mind is split into conscious and unconscious. Neuroses trace back to *repressed* childhood memories; the lucid mind must be understood *from* the unconscious and from libidinal drives. Therapy is possible because the unconscious, though withdrawn from awareness, is accessible. Sexuality is grounded in a largely unconscious *libido*.

### Position B — Sartre: total lucidity
*Being and Nothingness* Part One ch. 2 is now the primary text. Consciousness is translucent: its being is consciousness of being. Freud's id/ego/**censor** tries to restore a "lie without a liar" (BN 51). The censor must know what it represses in order to repress it, and so "is in bad faith" (BN 53). Steckel's "frigid" cases are conscious distraction, not buried complexes. Welten's slogan — "a consciousness that is unconscious is like water that is not wet" — is this argument's popular form. What Freud calls repression, Sartre re-describes as [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)|bad faith]]: one consciousness, metastable, deciding not to demand too much evidence. His "**existential psychoanalysis**" (Part Four) recovers a free **original choice**, not a hidden past (see [[Concepts/Existential Psychoanalysis and the Original Choice (Sartre)]]). Childhood is never a *cause*. Sexuality is intentional incarnation, not libido (see [[Concepts/Concrete Relations with Others (Sartre)]]).

### Position C — Foucault: a third pole (1976)
*The History of Sexuality* I does not adjudicate lucidity vs the unconscious. It relocates Freud. The repressive hypothesis already treats Freud's couch as a cautious first light and denounces "Freud's conformism, the normalizing functions of psychoanalysis" (Hurley p. 5). By the end of the book Freud is a *relay* of the [[Concepts/Deployment of Sexuality (Foucault)|deployment of sexuality]], not the breaker of a millennial silence: "the good genius of Freud" placed sex "at one of the critical points marked out for it since the eighteenth century by the strategies of knowledge and power" and gave "a new impetus to the secular injunction to study sex and transform it into discourse" (p. 159). Psychoanalysis is both a theory of "the essential interrelatedness of the law and desire" and a technique for relieving pathogenic taboo (p. 129) — and, against *dégénérescence*/racism, a "retro-version" that grounds sexuality in alliance, taboo, and the Sovereign-Father (p. 150). Reich's antirepressive struggle is "a tactical shift and reversal in the great deployment of sexuality," not an exit (p. 131). The question is not whether the unconscious is real but whether "repression" is the right name for how power works. See [[Concepts/Repressive Hypothesis (Foucault)]].

## Why It Matters
The whole edifice of Sartrean freedom depends on denying the unconscious: if there were a domain inaccessible to lucid choice, responsibility would no longer be total, and bad faith would collapse back into ordinary repression. Conversely, Freud's clinical practice depends on the unconscious being real. The disagreement is thus not a detail but a fork in twentieth-century theories of mind, self-knowledge, and responsibility. Foucault's third pole does not dissolve that fork; it asks whether both sides still share a juridico-discursive picture of power (law, taboo, liberation-or-trap) that Volume 1 drops.
