# Quentin Skinner

> Regius Professor of Modern History at Cambridge and the leading theorist of the **Cambridge School**: the history of political thought must recover not what texts say but what their authors were *doing* in saying it. "There are no perennial problems in philosophy. There are only individual answers to individual questions."

## Biography and Historical Context

Quentin Skinner (b. 1940) was Regius Professor of Modern History at the University of Cambridge. *Visions of Politics* (2002) collects his work in three volumes; Volume 1, *Regarding Method*, gathers the methodological essays, including the revised version of his 1969 manifesto "Meaning and understanding in the history of ideas." Volumes 2 and 3 (*Renaissance Virtues*, *Hobbes and Civil Science*) are the applications.

He describes his own position as writing "as a practising historian" while joining the sceptical camp in three post-empiricist assaults on "the world of facts": from epistemology, from the theory of meaning, and from post-modern accounts of rhetoric and power. The opening epigraph is Gradgrind's "Facts alone are wanted in life."

## Core Contributions

### Seeing things their way
The programme in one phrase. The historian's aim is "to grasp their concepts, to follow their distinctions, to appreciate their beliefs and, so far as possible, to see things their way." This is not mystical entry into another mind but ordinary historical technique — recovering the conventions available on a given occasion.

### The mythologies
See [[Concepts/Mythologies in the History of Ideas (Skinner)]]. The catalogue of errors generated by reading canonical texts for "dateless wisdom": the mythologies of **doctrines**, **coherence**, and **prolepsis**, plus two forms of **parochialism**. What results are "not histories but more appropriately mythologies."

### Illocutionary force
See [[Concepts/Illocutionary Force and Historical Meaning (Skinner)]]. From [[Thinkers/J.L. Austin]]: "whenever we use language for purposes of communication, we are always doing something as well as saying something." Recovering meaning requires separating what a text means from what its author meant *in* writing it, and distinguishing **motives** (antecedent, contingent, genuinely outside the work) from **intentions in** writing (a feature of the work itself).

### Bracketing truth
See [[Concepts/Explaining Beliefs Without Truth (Skinner)]]. "The concept of truth is irrelevant to the enterprise of explaining beliefs." What matters is whether it was *rational* for that agent to hold the belief true, by the standards then prevailing.

### Legitimation and the innovating ideologist
See [[Concepts/Innovating Ideologist and Legitimation (Skinner)]]. Even where professed principles are not an agent's motives, they constrain what he can do, because he must be able to legitimise it: "any course of action will be inhibited to the degree that it cannot be legitimised." Hence political vocabulary is "not an epiphenomenon… but one of the determinants of their behaviour."

### Rhetorical redescription
See [[Concepts/Rhetorical Redescription and Paradiastole (Skinner)]]. The later Skinner's central technique, recovered from Quintilian and Cicero: redescribing an action with a "neighbouring" term — prodigality as liberality, recklessness as courage.

### Words are not concepts
See [[Concepts/Cultural Lexicon - Words vs Concepts (Skinner)]]. Against Raymond Williams's *Keywords*: "what we need to know is not what words they use but rather what concepts they possess," and "a history of a word is by no means the same as a history of a concept."

## Self-Corrections in the Retrospect

Chapter 10 is unusually candid, and the vault should record what Skinner himself revises:

- He now regards chapters 8–9 as "an advance (or at least a change of mind)" over chapter 4, where he had wrongly assumed that morals and politics possess "distinct and relatively stable vocabularies."
- In the early 1970s he assumed "for every evaluative term there will at any one time be a standard and accepted meaning and use," and pictured the innovating ideologist as manipulating it by sleights of hand. After immersion in the classical rhetoricians he holds that "it makes little sense to speak of evaluative terms as having accepted denotations," and that "**all attempts to legislate about the 'correct' use of normative terms must be regarded as equally ideological in character**."
- He concedes Palonen's charge that he neglects Time as internal to the meaning of concepts — "I do indeed neglect this possibility, but only because I cannot make sense of it."
- He denies he ever aimed to discredit **Begriffsgeschichte**, saying he simply had "no knowledge of Koselleck's research programme" in the 1960s–70s. He accepts Palonen's placement of his own work within Koselleck's larger one: "Koselleck is interested in nothing less than the entire process of conceptual change: I am chiefly interested in one of the techniques by which it takes place."

## Relations to Other Vault Thinkers

- **[[Thinkers/Arthur O. Lovejoy]]** — the principal antagonist. "There cannot be a history of unit ideas as such, but only a history of the various uses to which they have been put by different agents at different times"; "there is nothing lying beneath or behind such uses."
- **[[Thinkers/Herbert Butterfield]]** — the mythology of prolepsis restates the Whig-interpretation critique in the vocabulary of intention.
- **[[Thinkers/J.L. Austin]]** — the enabling apparatus, though corrected: Skinner individuates illocutionary *acts* by the agent's intentions where Austin wavered toward conventions.
- **[[Thinkers/Ludwig Wittgenstein]]** — meaning as use; "words are also deeds" is quoted as an epigraph to the programme. See [[Concepts/Language-Games (Wittgenstein)]].
- **[[Thinkers/W.V.O. Quine]]** — credited in the Introduction with undermining the positivist picture of "unvarnished news," and endorsed on holism and the indeterminacy of translation.
- **[[Thinkers/Michel Foucault]]** — an unexpected ally: endorsed on the endless revisability of classificatory schemes, and quoted approvingly — "the history which bears and determines us has the form of a war." But Skinner criticises the "death of the author" as "exaggerated," since the author cannot be dispensed with: language is a resource as well as a constraint.
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Kuhn]]** — the "priority of paradigms" is borrowed (with Gombrich) to explain why the historian's own mental set distorts; Kuhn is defended as "widely, if mistakenly" read as a relativist.
- **[[Thinkers/Ian Hacking]]** — endorsed twice: "styles of reasoning" as an alternative to demanding translatability, and the account of how new descriptions create new possibilities for action.
- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]]** — Skinner declares allegiance to a Nietzsche–Weber tradition in which concepts give only changing perspectives, and reads *Genealogy* I as an unrecognised exercise in *paradiastole*.
- **[[Thinkers/Karl Popper]]** — criticised for prolepsis (Plato as totalitarian party-politician), though Skinner ends chapter 6 defending "the open society" by name.
