# Richard Rorty
> One of the most influential late 20th-century American philosophers, known for his critique of the representationalist tradition and his promotion of neopragmatism and therapeutic philosophy.

## Biography and Historical Context
Richard Rorty (1931–2007) began his career in analytic philosophy, editing the pathbreaking anthology *The Linguistic Turn* (1967). However, he gradually broke with the analytic mainstream, culminating in his landmark work *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979). He argued that the Cartesian-Lockean-Kantian tradition of treating mind and language as a "mirror of nature" (representing external reality) was a historical mistake. Rorty integrated the insights of John Dewey, Martin Heidegger, and Ludwig Wittgenstein to advocate for a post-epistemological neopragmatism, viewing language as a tool for social cooperation rather than an instrument of representation.

## Core Contributions

### The Critique of Representationalism
Rorty argues that the primary task of modern philosophy should be to abandon representationalist epistemology. Instead of seeking to match words to things or anchor beliefs to mind-independent reality, we should evaluate our beliefs and linguistic vocabularies by their practical utility in human life ("social practice").

### Therapeutic vs. Pragmatic Wittgensteinianism
In his contribution to *Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations: A Critical Guide* (2010), Rorty distinguishes two ways of reading the later Wittgenstein:
- **Therapeutic Wittgensteinianism**: The "resolute" reading (associated with James Conant and Cora Diamond) that views Wittgenstein's metaphilosophical goal as purely therapeutic, aiming to show that philosophical questions and metaphysical theories are plain, ungrammatical nonsense.
- **Pragmatic Wittgensteinianism**: The reading (associated with Donald Davidson and Rorty himself) that treats Wittgenstein as replacing the "picture theory" of meaning with a positive, naturalistic social-practice theory of meaning, showing how language works as a set of tools.

Rorty argues that while therapeutic readings capture Wittgenstein's own self-image, pragmatic readings are philosophically more useful because they help replace the "spectator theory of knowledge" with a pragmatist account of language.

### Introduction to Sellars's *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1997)

Rorty wrote the Introduction to the Harvard edition of [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|EPM]], and it is the vault's clearest statement of his historical thesis about analytic philosophy.

He identifies **three seminal works** in the shift from logical-empiricist to "post-positivistic" analytic philosophy — a shift that "began around 1950 and was complete by around 1970": [[Sources/Two Dogmas of Empiricism - W.V.O. Quine (1951)|Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"]] (1951), Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), and Sellars's EPM (1956). Of the three, EPM "is the least known and discussed," which Rorty calls "a pity," since "Sellars's attack on 'the Myth of the Given' … [was] a decisive move in turning analytic philosophy away from the foundationalist motives of the logical empiricists."

His division of labor between the first two is often quoted: "Whereas Quine's 'Two Dogmas' had helped destroy the distinction between analytic and synthetic truths, 'Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind' helped destroy the empiricist form of foundationalism by attacking the distinction between what is 'given to the mind' and what is 'added by the mind.'"

Rorty reads the essay as raising "doubts about the very idea of 'epistemology' — about the reality of the problems which philosophers had discussed under that heading," and connects it directly to his own *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979), where he "urged that giving up foundationalism might cause us to abandon the idea that we needed a 'theory of knowledge.'" He describes Sellars's project as an attempt "to usher analytic philosophy out of its Humean and into its Kantian phase," and the essay's closing sections as pushing it toward "its Hegelian stage." He also stresses the American pragmatist lineage Sellars rarely acknowledged — Peirce's "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" (1868) and Dewey's polemics against givenness.

On the substance he emphasizes [[Concepts/Space of Reasons (Sellars)|the space of reasons]] — "knowledge is inseparable from a social practice — the practice of justifying one's assertions to one's fellow-humans. It is not presupposed by this practice, but comes into being along with it" — and [[Concepts/Psychological Nominalism (Sellars)|psychological nominalism]], noting that Sellars, "like the later Wittgenstein but unlike Kant, identified the possession of a concept with the mastery of the use of a word," so that "mastery of a language is prerequisite of conscious experience." He borrows [[Thinkers/Robert Brandom|Brandom's]] contrast between **representationalist** philosophers ("like Descartes and Locke") and **inferentialist** ones ("like Leibniz, Kant, Frege, the later Wittgenstein, and Sellars").

Rorty closes by proposing that the Sellars–Brandom "social practice" approach "might help reconnect that philosophical tradition with the so-called 'Continental' tradition," imagining "a future in which Sellars and Habermas, Davidson and Gadamer, Putnam and Derrida, Rawls and Foucault, are seen as fellow-travelers on the same journey, fellow-citizens of what Michael Oakeshott called a *civitas peregrina*." He records his own quip to Sellars: "If a man chooses to bind the spirit of Hegel in the fetters of Carnap, how shall he find readers?"

## Reception in *The Embodied Mind*

[[Sources/The Embodied Mind - Varela Thompson and Rosch (2016)]] is an anti-foundational ally that refuses Rorty's "continuing the conversation of the West" as provincial. They take Heidegger's "planetary thinking" instead and bring Madhyamaka resources to groundlessness. See [[Concepts/Groundlessness and the Middle Way (Varela)]].
