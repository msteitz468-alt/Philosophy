# Thomas Hobbes

> English philosopher (1588–1679) widely regarded as one of the founders of modern political philosophy. Developed a thoroughly materialist metaphysics and an influential theory of the social contract, arguing that peace and social order can only be maintained through the establishment of an absolute sovereign power.

## Key Contributions

**Materialist Human Nature**:
- Rejected substance dualism in favor of a mechanistic, materialist view of the world. Argued that humans are completely physical systems governed by motion, appetite, and aversion.
- Described human life in its natural state—the **State of Nature**—as a state of perpetual war of all against all, where life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." See [[Concepts/State of Nature (Hobbes)]].

**The Social Contract and Sovereign Power**:
- Proposed that to escape the state of nature, individuals rationally choose to enter into a **Social Contract**, collectively transferring their natural right to self-preservation to a centralized political authority (the **Leviathan**).
- Held that the sovereign's power must be absolute, indivisible, and unlimited to prevent the collapse of society back into civil war. See [[Concepts/Social Contract (Hobbes)]].

## Relevance to Vault

- **The Realist political axis**: Hobbes represents the foundational realist view of political power, contrasting sharply with the constitutionalism of John Locke and the checks-and-balances design of [Publius](file:///home/mark/Documents/Philosophy/wiki/Thinkers/Publius%20(Hamilton,%20Madison,%20and%20Jay).md). See [[Contradictions/Hobbesian Absolutism vs. Lockean Constitutionalism]].
- **Materialism vs. Teleology**: Hobbes' mechanical materialism aligns with Democritus and Lucretius, rejecting Aristotelian final causes and Hegelian spiritual teleology.
- **Necessity and Freedom**: Hobbes' view of choice as caused by physical appetites parallels Spinoza's necessitarianism but contrasts with Kant's transcendental freedom.

## Versus Constitutional Economics (2026-06-29 Ingest)

James M. Buchanan and Gordon Tullock's *The Calculus of Consent* (1962) directly engages and modifies Hobbes's social contract theory:
- **Constitutional Contract vs. Leviathan**: Both Hobbes and the public choice team ground political authority in a rational social contract designed by self-interested individuals to escape anarchy (external costs). However, Hobbes concludes that individuals must surrender all power to an absolute, undivided sovereign. Buchanan and Tullock demonstrate that rational individuals can instead design a constitution with qualified majorities, bicameralism, and veto points that protects them from both private anarchy and public exploitation, avoiding the necessity of an absolute Leviathan.
- **Methodological Alignment**: Buchanan and Tullock embrace Hobbes's mechanistic, self-interested view of human nature (methodological individualism) but use it to construct a constitutional democracy rather than an autocracy.

See [[Thinkers/James M. Buchanan]], [[Thinkers/Gordon Tullock]], and [[Concepts/Constitutional Political Economy (Buchanan-Tullock)]].

## Versus Foucault's Micro-physics (2026-08-12 Ingest)

*Discipline and Punish* does not cite Hobbes. Adjacent material: punish as *merum imperium* / war on enemies; crime as personal affront to the prince; the condemned as inverted king's body. Do not equate Foucault's scaffold-sovereign with the Leviathan — Hobbes's sovereign is instituted by covenant; Foucault's is a ceremony of dissymmetry. The load-bearing clash is later and explicit (IV.3, effect 3): the theory of the contract answers how a new *foundation* was given to the right to punish; Foucault asks the reverse — how people were made to *tolerate* being punished. The contract is a "fiction of a juridical subject giving to others the power to exercise over him the right that he himself possesses over them"; the carceral continuum is "the technical and real, immediately material counterpart of that chimerical granting." Discipline's reference is "not to the primal social contract, but to permanent coercions… not to the general will but to automatic docility." See [[Thinkers/Michel Foucault]], [[Concepts/Sovereign Power and the Spectacle of the Scaffold (Foucault)]], [[Concepts/The Delinquent and the Carceral Continuum (Foucault)]].

*The History of Sexuality* I names Hobbes (Hurley p. 135): is the sovereign's right of life and death "the transfer to the prince of the natural right possessed by every individual to defend his life even if this meant the death of others," or a right proper to the sovereign as a new juridical being (Pufendorf)? Left open. Either way the right is dissymmetrical — take life or let live — and is *succeeded* by bio-power's foster life or disallow it (p. 138). Hobbes is the named ancestor of the *old* right, not of the new one. See [[Concepts/Biopolitics and Governmentality (Foucault)]].

## Versus Nozick (2026-08-12 Ingest)

[[Thinkers/Robert Nozick]] refuses the Hobbesian worst-case as the comparison class: investigate the *best realistic anarchy* (people generally satisfy moral constraints), not the war of all against all. The state that emerges is a night-watchman [[Concepts/Minimal State and Ultraminimal State (Nozick)|minimal state]], filtered by an [[Concepts/Invisible-Hand Explanation of the State (Nozick)|invisible hand]], not instituted by covenant, and it has no special rights. Third pole on [[Contradictions/Hobbesian Absolutism vs. Lockean Constitutionalism]].

## Versus Berlin (2026-08-12)

[[Thinkers/Isaiah Berlin]] (reception: Baum/Nichols 2013) names Hobbes in the lineage of purely **negative** freedom — liberty as absence of external impediment — and follows him in holding that such liberty is compatible with autocracy. MacGilvray (same volume) puts Hobbes on the **market** side of a republican/market contrast Berlin’s dual cannot capture. See [[Concepts/Negative and Positive Liberty (Berlin)]].
