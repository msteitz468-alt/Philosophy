# J.L. Austin

> Author of *How to Do Things with Words* and originator of **speech-act theory**. In the vault he enters through [[Thinkers/Quentin Skinner|Skinner]], who makes Austin's distinction between the meaning of an utterance and its **force** the engine of a whole method for the history of ideas.

## Biography and Historical Context

J. L. Austin (1911–1960) was White's Professor of Moral Philosophy at Oxford and a central figure in post-war ordinary language philosophy. *How to Do Things with Words* was published posthumously in 1962 from his 1955 William James Lectures.

## Core Contributions as They Appear in the Vault

### The dimension of force
Austin's decisive move was to separate two dimensions of any serious utterance: its **meaning** (sense and reference) and its **force**. Skinner's formulation of the consequence: "anyone issuing a serious utterance will always be doing something as well as saying something, and doing it in virtue of saying what is said."

### Illocutionary and perlocutionary
- **Illocutionary** — what we do *in* saying something: warning, promising, ridiculing, repudiating, conceding.
- **Perlocutionary** — what we bring about *by* saying it: alarming someone, inducing sadness, persuading.

The standard example, which Skinner adapts from Strawson: a policeman says "The ice over there is very thin." The meaning is transparent; the illocutionary act is a *warning*; the perlocutionary effect may be that the skater stops.

### Uptake
An utterance's force must be taken up to have been performed. Austin, quoted by Skinner: "I cannot be said to have warned an audience unless it hears what I say and takes what I say in a certain sense." Skinner glosses that gaining uptake of illocutionary force "will be equivalent to understanding what the speaker was *doing* in issuing it."

## Where Skinner Corrects Him

Two amendments matter, and both are Skinner's, not Austin's:

1. **Intentions rather than conventions.** Austin wavered toward individuating illocutionary acts by the conventions governing them. Skinner individuates them by the **agent's intentions**, expanding Austin via Strawson, Searle, Schiffer and Holdcroft. On Skinner's account force is "a resource of language" while acts are identified by intentions — which is why utterances routinely carry force their authors never intended.
2. **No unintentional illocutionary acts.** Skinner denies these exist: one may warn someone unintentionally, but then no illocutionary *act* of warning has been performed. "It is rather that I disbelieve in its existence."

Skinner also declines Austin's "bringing it about that p" analysis of illocutionary acts (with Davidson, against Graham): to warn is simply to advert to the fact that someone is in danger, not to make some new state of affairs true.
