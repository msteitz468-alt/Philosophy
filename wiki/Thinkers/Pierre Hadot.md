# Pierre Hadot
> French historian of ancient philosophy (1922–2010) who argued that Greco-Roman *philosophia* was a way of life — a conversion of existence practiced through spiritual exercises — and that modern university philosophy, reduced to theoretical discourse, has forgotten this.

## Life and Posts (as this source presents them)

Most of Hadot's career was at the École pratique des Hautes Études and the Collège de France. Appointed directeur d'études of the EPHE fifth section in 1964, he occupied a chair in Latin Patristics (Ambrose, Augustine). In 1972, as his work turned to non-Christian thought, the chair was retitled "Theologies and Mysticisms of Hellenistic Greece and the End of Antiquity"; he lectured on [[Thinkers/Plotinus]] and [[Thinkers/Marcus Aurelius]]. In February 1983 he assumed the chair of the History of Hellenistic and Roman Thought at the Collège de France.

He published translations and commentaries on Marius Victorinus, [[Thinkers/Porphyry]], Ambrose, Plotinus, and Marcus. The English collection *Philosophy as a Way of Life* (Blackwell 1995, ed. Arnold I. Davidson, trans. Michael Chase) expands the 1987 second edition of *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique*. The Marcus essay in that volume is rewritten after *La Citadelle intérieure* (1992). Davidson reports that Michel Foucault first mentioned Hadot to him in 1982.

The 1992 interview that closes the English volume is first-person: Newman’s distinction of real vs notional assent; a passage through Thomism, then Bergson, existentialism, and Marxism; twenty years on Victorinus that were “unchosen”; Wittgenstein’s language-games as forms of life; from about 1970, Stoicism and Epicureanism preferred over Plotinian mysticism as what “could nourish the spiritual life” now. Last lecture: “In the last analysis, we can scarcely talk about what is most important” — aligned with *Tractatus* 6.522.

## Method

Ancient works cannot be read as contemporary treatises. Meaning is fixed by school setting, the nature of *philosophia*, literary genre, rhetorical rules, dogmatic imperatives, and oral teaching. “Literary structure and conceptual structure must never be separated.” Once the “hidden form” of a text is seen, details that seemed arbitrary become necessary. Writing is an aid to memory; true education is oral; works are meant **to form, not inform**.

From the mid-4th century BC to the late 16th century, philosophy is largely **exegesis of authoritative texts**. Forced accommodation of an “authentic” author is fidelity, not fraud. The historian’s name for the productive result is the [[Concepts/Creative Mistakes and Philosophical Exegesis (Hadot)|creative mistake]] (*contresens*).

## Central Thesis

*Philosophia* is a **choice of a form of life**, to which discourse then gives justifications. For the Stoics (and, Hadot says, most schools), logic, physics, and ethics are parts of *discourse about* philosophy, not of philosophy itself. Philosophy is the unitary act of *living* logic, physics, and ethics. See [[Concepts/Philosophy as a Way of Life (Hadot)]] and [[Concepts/Spiritual Exercises (Hadot)]].

The *Meditations* of Marcus are *hypomnemata* structured on Epictetus’s three *topoi* (desire, action, assent) as lived physics, ethics, and logic. See [[Concepts/The Three Disciplines - Perception, Action, and Will (Hadot)]]. Foucault recovered the ancient art of living but narrowed it to an aesthetics of the self; Hadot’s objection is that interiorization is a going-beyond-oneself, a universalization. See [[Contradictions/Hadot vs Foucault - Care of the Self vs Cosmic Universalization]].

## Modern Recoveries and the Contemporary Proposal

Causes of the theoretical reduction: the philosopher’s “perpetual danger” of shutting himself in concepts; the medieval split (exercises absorbed into Christian spirituality; philosophy as *ancilla theologiae*); the university as professors training professors. “In modern university philosophy, philosophy is obviously no longer a way of life or form of life — unless it be the form of life of a professor of philosophy.”

Recoveries Hadot names: Erasmus, Montaigne, Descartes, Spinoza, Shaftesbury, Kant, Goethe, Nietzsche, [[Thinkers/Søren Kierkegaard]], Wittgenstein, Thoreau. Kierkegaard now has a primary: *Fear and Trembling* confirms the Hadot figure (pseudonym as *prosopon*; deceive into the truth; the Individual) from inside one aesthetic work. Johannes's infinite resignation is a spiritual exercise one can discipline oneself into; the second movement, he says, philosophy cannot account for. Detached from “outmoded elements,” there remain universal Stoic, Epicurean, Platonic, Aristotelian, Cynic, and Pyrrhonian *attitudes*. One need not choose exclusively; Stoicism and Epicureanism are “two opposite but inseparable poles.” The historian must still ask: “What is it to philosophize?”
