# Karl Marx

> German philosopher, economist, political theorist, and sociologist (1818–1883). Developed the framework of historical materialism and the critique of capitalism, arguing that human societies progress through class struggles and that the economic base of a society determines its social and political superstructure.

## Key Contributions

**Historical Materialism**:
- Formulated the theory that the material conditions of a society's mode of production (its economic base) shape its political, legal, and ideological structures (its superstructure).
- Taught that history is not driven by the self-unfolding of ideas (as Hegel argued), but by the contradictions and conflicts within the material/economic sphere. See [[Concepts/Historical Materialism (Marx)]].

**Class Struggle**:
- Asserted that "the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." Under capitalism, this conflict is polarized between the bourgeoisie (owners of the means of production) and the proletariat (wage laborers).
- Envisioned that class contradictions would inevitably lead to a proletarian revolution and the establishment of a classless, communist society. See [[Concepts/Class Struggle (Marx)]].

## Relevance to Vault

- **The Inversion of Hegel**: Marx adopted Hegel's dialectical method but rejected his idealism, swapping *Geist* for material economic relations. This is the classic idealist-materialist division. See [[Contradictions/Dialectical Idealism vs. Historical Materialism]].
- **Alienation and Labor**: Marx's analysis of how capitalism alienates the worker from the product of their labor builds directly on Hegel's [Master-Slave Dialectic](file:///home/mark/Documents/Philosophy/wiki/Concepts/Master-Slave%20Dialectic%20(Hegel).md).
- **Critique of Liberalism**: Marx challenges the individual rights and contract theories of Locke and Mill, viewing them as ideological justifications for bourgeois property relations.

## Versus Mancur Olson (2026-06-29 Ingest)

Mancur Olson's *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982) presents a direct challenge to Marx's theory of class action:
- **The Paradox of Class Action**: Marx assumes that the working class (the proletariat) will naturally act collectively to overthrow capitalism once they overcome "false consciousness." Olson argues this is a logical error because classes are large groups, and individuals within them face strong incentives to free-ride. An individual proletarian incurs high personal risks by participating in revolution, while hoping others will fight and secure the classless society — meaning large-scale class revolution requires selective incentives or coercion to overcome free-riding.
- **The "Internal Contradiction" of Stability**: Both Marx and Olson identify an "internal contradiction" in stable societies. For Marx, it is the dialectical contradiction between the forces and relations of production leading to class struggle. For Olson, it is the non-dialectical conflict between the advantages of peace/stability and the gradual accumulation of rent-seeking distributional coalitions that ultimately undermine economic growth.

See [[Thinkers/Mancur Olson]] and [[Concepts/Collective Action and Free Riding (Olson)]].

## Versus Nozick (2026-08-12 Ingest)

[[Thinkers/Robert Nozick]] Ch. 8 is a compressed attack on *value theory*, not on class struggle or historical materialism (those are barely treated). Once the labor theory of value “crumbles,” exploitation reduces to *nonaccess* to means of production; if workers are not forced to deal with capitalists (an expandable public sector), wage labor is not exploitation. “Socially necessary” labor time is defined by the market, so LTV collapses into competitive exchange. Residual “exploitation” is “people’s lack of understanding of economics.” Worker-controlled firms may be started voluntarily; seizing profits to float them is unjustified. The [[Concepts/Framework for Utopia (Nozick)|utopian framework]] allows communes to grow by adhesion, not by imposed pattern. See [[Concepts/Entitlement Theory (Nozick)]].

## Versus Constitutional Economics (2026-06-29 Ingest)

Buchanan and Tullock's public choice framework reinterprets Marx's critique of the capitalist state:
- **The Exploitative State**: Marx viewed the state as the executive committee of the ruling class, structured to exploit the proletariat. Buchanan and Tullock model this exploitation using decision-making rules: if the constitutional rules allow collective choices to be made by less-than-unanimous voting thresholds (like simple majority), winning coalitions will naturally use the state's coercive tax-spending powers to redistribute income to themselves, imposing external costs on the minority. Exploitation is thus a consequence of non-unanimous voting rules, not class dialectics.
- **Economics of the State**: Both Marx and the public choice theorists apply economic models to political institutions, but public choice rejects Marx's historical materialism in favor of methodological individualism, focusing on the utility calculations of individuals rather than classes.

See [[Thinkers/James M. Buchanan]], [[Thinkers/Gordon Tullock]], and [[Concepts/Constitutional Political Economy (Buchanan-Tullock)]].

## Versus Foucault's Political Anatomy (2026-08-12 Ingest)

*Discipline and Punish* quotes *Capital* I on cooperation and military tactics and accepts that the body is invested as labour-power. Foucault adds a *prior* operation: "If economic exploitation separates the force and the product of labour, … disciplinary coercion establishes in the body the constricting link between an increased aptitude and an increased domination." At large-scale industry one finds, "beneath the division of the production process, the individualizing fragmentation of labour power." Part Two also splits ancien-régime tolerated illegalities by class: the poor are forced into an *illegality of property* (theft); the bourgeoisie reserves the *illegality of rights* (fraud, tax evasion). Part Four cites *The Eighteenth Brumaire* for the political use of delinquents (1848 / Louis Napoleon). Power is not reduced to class; it is a micro-physics of bodies. See [[Thinkers/Michel Foucault]], [[Concepts/Political Technology of the Body (Foucault)]], [[Concepts/The Delinquent and the Carceral Continuum (Foucault)]].

## Versus Berlin (2026-08-12)

[[Thinkers/Isaiah Berlin]] (reception: Baum/Nichols 2013) treats “social and economic freedom” as claptrap — “not a type of freedom, but an independent value altogether—substantive equality” (TC, 172–173). Tully restores enabling capacities as *constituent* of negative liberty; Gould retrieves **effective freedom**. Crowder: Berlin still builds a welfare-state case *from* negative liberty (IN, 38–39) and only refuses the *name*. See [[Concepts/Liberty versus the Conditions of Liberty (Berlin)]].

*The Crooked Timber of Humanity* (now ingested) adds Berlin’s own cuts: Marx historicises conflict but keeps “true history” / a happy ending ([[Concepts/Utopianism and the Pursuit of the Ideal (Berlin)]]); nationalism as bourgeois **false consciousness** is the miss ([[Concepts/Bent Twig Nationalism (Berlin)]]); Maistre is a tough-minded successor in *cast of mind*, not doctrine ([[Thinkers/Joseph de Maistre]]).

## Versus Foucault's Bio-power (2026-08-12, *History of Sexuality* I)

Volume 1 first *refuses* the Marxist-adjacent energetics of the [[Concepts/Repressive Hypothesis (Foucault)|repressive hypothesis]]: repression dated to coincide with capitalism because sex is "incompatible with a general and intensive work imperative" (Hurley p. 6) is the story's political guarantee, not Foucault's explanation. IV.4: "we must shift our analysis away from the problem of 'labor capacity' and doubtless abandon the diffuse energetics that underlies the theme of a sexuality repressed for economic reasons" (p. 114) — including the *Spätkapitalismus* / "hyper-repressive desublimation" two-phase story. Sexuality is "originally, historically bourgeois," a self-affirmation of body and blood, not first a device for castrating the proletariat into labor.

Part Five then *re-joins* capitalism on different terms. Marx is not named; capitalism is. "This bio-power was without question an indispensable element in the development of capitalism; the latter would not have been possible without the controlled insertion of bodies into the machinery of production and the adjustment of the phenomena of population to economic processes" (pp. 140–41). "The adjustment of the accumulation of men to that of capital" was made possible in part by bio-power. Complementary to the 1975 claim (discipline links aptitude to domination *inside* the body): 1976 adds the population pole. See [[Concepts/Biopolitics and Governmentality (Foucault)]], [[Concepts/Deployment of Sexuality (Foucault)]].
