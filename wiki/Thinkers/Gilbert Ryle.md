# Gilbert Ryle

> Author of *The Concept of Mind* (1949) and the polemic against "the ghost in the machine." In the vault he enters as the foil for [[Thinkers/Wilfrid Sellars]]: the philosopher whose dissolution of inner episodes Sellars wants to *avoid* while still accepting Wittgenstein's case against self-authenticating experience.

> [!note] Sourcing
> This page is built **only** from what [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|EPM]] and Rorty's Introduction say about Ryle. *The Concept of Mind* is not itself in the vault. Treat the characterizations below as Sellars's and Rorty's, and check them against Ryle's text before relying on them.

## Biography and Historical Context

Gilbert Ryle (1900–1976) was an Oxford philosopher and editor of *Mind*. *The Concept of Mind* (1949) appeared shortly before Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953). Rorty notes the consequence for the period: the two together "had made Wittgensteinian opposition to the idea of 'a private language,' and to that of 'entities capable of being known by only one person,' seem inseparable from Ryle's polemic against 'the ghost in the machine.'" Prying those apart is one of the tasks of EPM.

## Contributions as They Appear in EPM

### Achievement words
Sellars adopts and amends Ryle's point that *seeing that* something is the case is an **achievement**, and "see" an "achievement word." Sellars's amendment: "I prefer to call it a 'so it is' or 'just so' word, for the root idea is that of **truth**." The underlying insight — that "S sees that *p*" does not merely describe an experience but *endorses* a claim — is what [[Concepts/The Logic of Looks (Sellars)|Sellars's account of 'looks']] is built on.

### The Rylean language
Sellars names the starting point of the [[Concepts/Myth of Jones (Sellars)|Myth of Jones]] after him: "a **Rylean language**, a language of which the fundamental descriptive vocabulary speaks of public properties of public objects located in Space and enduring through Time." Sellars is careful that it is Rylean in a second sense too — logically rich, with quantification, subjunctive conditionals, vagueness and open texture — so that the myth does not cheat by starting from an impoverished base.

### The position Sellars rejects
Sellars considers, and finds "impossibly crude and inadequate as an account of even the simplest concept," an account of thinking as a "mongrel categorical-hypothetical" — a verbal episode as the manifestation of associations of word-object and word-word types. His verdict is that a Rylean framework of dispositions to overt verbal behavior cannot by itself capture the **intentionality** of thoughts, which is why Jones must add semantical resources and then postulate inner episodes.

The upshot, in Rorty's formulation: Sellars's "account of inner episodes as having originally been postulated, rather than [observed]" explains "why we can be naturalists without being behaviorists, why we can accept Wittgenstein's doubts about … 'self-authenticating non-verbal episodes' without sharing Ryle's doubts about the existence of such mental entities as thoughts and sense-impressions."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Sellars against Ryle on inner episodes: Sellars retains genuine inner episodes with genuine (fallible, defeasible) privileged access. He agrees with Ryle that they are not the Cartesian self-intimating theatre, and agrees with Wittgenstein that no non-verbal episode is self-authenticating — but denies that either point licenses eliminating them. See [[Concepts/Myth of Jones (Sellars)]].
- > [!warning] Note the distinction Sellars insists on (§53): *methodological* behaviorism is a thesis about how to build new concepts and "does not deny that there is such a thing as introspection"; *philosophical* or *analytical* behaviorism is a thesis about the analysis of common-sense mentalistic concepts. Sellars endorses a liberalized version of the first and rejects the second.
