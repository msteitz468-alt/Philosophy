# Space of Reasons (Sellars)

> "In characterizing an episode or a state as that of *knowing*, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the logical space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says" (§36) — the single most quoted sentence in [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|EPM]], and the hinge on which the regress objection to Sellars's anti-foundationalism turns.

## The Claim

The sentence arrives as the answer to a specific objection. Sellars has argued ([[Concepts/Myth of the Given (Sellars)]]) that a report expresses observational knowledge only if the reporter *knows* that reports of that kind are reliable indicators. The obvious reply is that this generates a vicious regress: observational knowledge presupposes general knowledge of the form "X is a reliable symptom of Y," which presupposes prior observational knowledge, and so on.

Sellars answers that the objection "rests on too simple, indeed a radically mistaken, conception of what one is saying about Jones when one says that he *knows* that *p*. It is not just that the objection supposes that knowing is an episode; for clearly there are episodes which we can correctly characterize as knowings, in particular, *observings*."

> The essential point is that in characterizing an episode or a state as that of *knowing*, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the logical space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says.

Because attributing knowledge is a *normative placement* rather than a *description of an episode*, the required general knowledge need not have been possessed at the earlier time. All that follows is that "no tokening by S now of 'This is green' could count as 'expressing observational knowledge' unless it were correct to say of S that he now knows the appropriate fact of the form X is a reliable symptom of Y." And "while the correctness of this statement requires that Jones could now cite prior particular facts… it requires only that it is correct to say that Jones now knows… it does not require that it be correct to say that Jones knew these particular facts at the time these facts did obtain. **And the regress disappears.**"

The developmental corollary: Jones's present ability to give inductive reasons "is built on a long history of acquiring and manifesting verbal habits in perceptual situations, and, in particular, the occurrence of verbal episodes, e.g. 'This is green,' which is superficially like those which are later properly said to express observational knowledge" — but "it does not require that any episode in this prior time be characterizeable as expressing knowledge."

## The Wider Thesis: Epistemic Facts Are Not Reducible

The space-of-reasons remark is a specific application of a general claim Sellars flags early (§5) and calls "a central theme in a later stage of my argument":

> Now the idea that epistemic facts can be analyzed without remainder — even "in principle" — into non-epistemic facts, whether phenomenal or behavioral, public or private, with no matter how lavish a sprinkling of subjunctives and hypotheticals is, I believe, a radical mistake — a mistake of a piece with the so-called "**naturalistic fallacy**" in ethics.

The comparison to ethics is deliberate and load-bearing: *knows*, like *good*, is a normative term, and the attempt to analyze it into descriptive vocabulary loses precisely what makes it the term it is. This is why classical sense-datum analyses of sensing "did so in *non-epistemic* terms," and why that project was doomed from the start.

Rorty's gloss: "In other words, knowledge is inseparable from a social practice — the practice of justifying one's assertions to one's fellow-humans. It is not presupposed by this practice, but comes into being along with it." And: since the rationality of empirical knowledge "is not a matter of conformity to ahistorical standards (which epistemologists might hope to codify), but rather of successful participation in a cooperative human enterprise."

## Why It Matters

The distinction between the *causal* order (what brings a state about) and the *normative* order (what justifies it) is what allows Sellars to be a thoroughgoing naturalist about the *occurrence* of perceptual episodes while denying that any such episode can, simply by occurring, confer justification. A sensation may be a necessary causal condition of a knowing without being a knowing — exactly the branch of the [[Arguments/Inconsistent Triad in Sense-Datum Theories (Sellars)|inconsistent triad]] the sense-datum theorist could not afford to take.

It also explains the shape of the positive story. Because knowing is a status conferred within a practice, and practices are public and learned, the [[Concepts/Psychological Nominalism (Sellars)|linguistic]] character of awareness follows rather than being an extra assumption.

## Reception and Downstream Use

- **[[Thinkers/John McDowell]]** — *Mind and World* (1994) takes the space of reasons as its organizing opposition: the "space of reasons" (rational justification) versus the "space of nature" (empirical causes). McDowell's worry is that if experience is entirely outside the space of reasons, empirical belief has no rational constraint from the world; his answer is that experience is *already conceptual*, so the world can bear on belief rationally without any Given.
- **[[Thinkers/Robert Brandom]]** — builds an entire inferentialist semantics on the priority of the normative: to be a concept-user is to be a participant in a game of giving and asking for reasons. Rorty notes Brandom "begins, so to speak, where Sellars's essay leaves off," and makes good on Sellars's "promissory notes."
- **[[Thinkers/Richard Rorty]]** — reads the essay as raising "doubts about the very idea of 'epistemology'" and, in *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979), as licensing the abandonment of the notion that we need a "theory of knowledge" at all.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Contrast with [[Concepts/Naturalized Epistemology and the Web of Belief (Quine)]]. Quine's naturalism runs the other way: epistemology becomes a chapter of empirical psychology, continuous with science. Sellars's naturalistic-fallacy claim says exactly that the epistemic *cannot* be analyzed without remainder into the descriptive. Both are anti-foundationalist; they part company on whether normativity survives naturalization. This is a genuine and unresolved fork in the vault's post-positivist cluster.
- > [!warning] Sits uneasily with [[Concepts/No Authoritative Sources of Knowledge (Popper)]] only in emphasis, not in verdict: Popper too denies an ultimate court of appeal, but he explains scientific rationality by the *logic of testing* rather than by the normative structure of knowledge-attribution.
- Open: how far the naturalistic-fallacy analogy really carries. Sellars asserts it here and defers the argument; readers who reject the ethical analogy have room to resist the epistemological conclusion.
