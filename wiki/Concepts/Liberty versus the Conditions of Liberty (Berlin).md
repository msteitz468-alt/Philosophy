# Liberty versus the Conditions of Liberty (Berlin)

> Berlin’s most fought-over corollary of negative liberty: **liberty is one thing, the conditions of its exercise another**. A locked legal door is unfreedom; an unlocked door you are too poor, ignorant, or feeble to walk through is still liberty — just a liberty that is “nothing to you.” Reception source, not a Berlin primary.

## Summary

The 1969 Introduction states the split cleanly: “Liberty is one thing and the conditions for its exercise are another” (IN, 45). The 1958 lecture already has the slogan this volume repeats: “Everything is what it is: liberty is liberty, not equality or fairness or justice or culture, or human happiness or a quiet conscience” (TC, 172). Collapse the distinction, Berlin says, and you blur liberty into every other political good — the first step on the positive-liberty slide.

He is not claiming that conditions do not matter. “Without adequate conditions for the use of freedom, what is the value of freedom?” (TC, 171). His answer: the *value* of a liberty can be nil while the liberty is not “annihilated.” “If a man is too poor or too ignorant or too feeble to make use of his legal rights, the liberty that these rights confer upon him is nothing to him, but it is not thereby annihilated” (IN, 45).

This page exists because almost every essay in the 2013 volume attacks, defends, or relocates this split. It is the hinge between [[Concepts/Negative and Positive Liberty (Berlin)]] and [[Concepts/Value Pluralism (Berlin)]].

## Berlin’s Argument (as quoted)

1. **Human interference, not mere incapacity.** People “lack political liberty or freedom only if [they] are prevented from attaining a goal by human beings. Mere incapacity to attain a goal is not a lack of political freedom” (TC, 169).
2. **Poverty is theory-laden.** Lack of money is unfreedom only if “other human beings have made arrangements whereby I am, whereas others are not, prevented from having enough money” (TC, 170). That depends on “a particular social and economic theory about the causes of my poverty.”
3. **Trade-offs are real losses.** “To avoid glaring inequality or widespread misery, I am ready to sacrifice some, or all of my freedom… but it is freedom I am giving up.” If I do not “materially increase the individual liberty of others, an absolute loss of liberty occurs.” There may be gains “in justice or in happiness or in peace, but the loss remains” (TC, 172).
4. **Education as the test case.** Compulsory schooling restricts some parents’ liberty to determine their children’s education, for the sake of giving more children “opportunity for free choice.” Those opportunities are, for Berlin, *conditions* of freedom, not freedom (IN, 46).
5. **1969 welfare-state concession.** “The case for social legislation or planning, for the Welfare State and socialism, can be constructed with as much validity from consideration of the claims of negative liberty as from those of its positive brother” (IN, 38–39). “Freedom for the wolves has often meant death to the sheep” (IN, 38). Crowder: he still refuses the *name* “effective liberty” (Green’s “positive power”).

A further, less official, concession the critics seize: even TC 171 speaks of a “minimum development of… natural faculties.” Nichols: that is already a concealed positive value inside the negative concept.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

### Berlin / Crowder (keep the name, spend the value)

Crowder: Tully missed the point. Berlin *agrees* that poverty and ignorance undermine liberty’s value and *supports* redistribution. What he will not do is call capacity “liberty,” because that is how liberty swallows equality and the slide begins. Negative liberty remains one good among others.

### Tully / Gould / MacCallum (reported) — the split is the error

- **Tully:** if negative liberty is “the area within which the subject… should be left to do or be what he is able to do or be” (TC, 168), then the **abilities** to act in that area (literacy, health, mobility, workplace voice, recognition) are *constituent*, not preconditions. Ordinary language is not confused when it calls them liberties. Berlin’s own poor-man example already equates lack of means with legal prohibition (TC, 169–170), then dodges into “social theory.”
- **Gould:** separating liberty from its conditions is “a fundamental error.” Poverty and exploitation are human constraints. **Effective freedom** = choice + enabling/constraining conditions + self-transformation. Justice = **equal positive freedom** (equal rights to the conditions of self-development).
- **MacCallum (via the editors):** we invite misunderstanding if we say people are *free* to do what social and economic arrangements prevent, merely because no statute forbids it.
- **McFarlane (early reply, cited):** without “the conditions necessary for its effective use,” the freedom is “a mockery, save for a favored few.”

### Dimova-Cookson (split for one concept, fuse for the other)

For *negative* liberty the split is required — otherwise liberty is no longer uncensored choice. For *social positive* liberty, the *association* of exercise with justice and conditions *is* the concept (Green, Rousseau). Two levels, not one confusion.

### Christman (background, not meaning)

Social supports may be needed for **procedural autonomy** to be possible. They are not what autonomy *means*. Making proper social relations constitutive turns autonomy perfectionist and fails Berlin’s challenge.

### Hirschmann / Coole / Nichols / Baum

- **Hirschmann:** Berlin counts “actual doors,” not preferences — so a conditioned slave who loves her chains is unfree. Then he will not let *gender* (as structure, not a discrete agent) count as a closed door.
- **Coole:** once power is only *coercion* (deliberate interference), structural-economic “oppression” and micro-power drop out of unfreedom.
- **Nichols:** “minimum faculties” + meaningful options = a concealed positive notion. The conditions-talk *shields* the liberal chooser from subjectification.
- **Baum (Taylor, Marcuse):** extent of freedom is control over one’s life, not the number of options; *what* is chosen matters. Consumer “do our own thing” is unequally distributed. Ecology: some conditions of future freedom require limiting present options.

### Liberal despotism (the inversion)

The editors and Tully turn the split back on Berlin. If negative liberty has institutional *conditions* (rule of law, property, contract), may one impose those conditions coercively in liberty’s name? McKinley’s “benevolent assimilation” of the Philippines is the historical exhibit. Tully: TC 175–176’s “liberal-minded despots” and “severe discipline” for peoples “not ready” is the same inversion Berlin condemns in Rousseau, now under a negative banner. Crowder: that is a misread of the conditions-passage and of later nationalism essays; Berlin does not license forcing the Third World free from outside.

## Vault Adjacencies

- **[[Concepts/Harm Principle (Mill)]]:** Mill also splits self-regarding liberty from other goods, but he will not call a starved option “liberty” in the same way — development of faculties is inside his utilitarian defense. Compulsory education is a case both discuss.
- **[[Concepts/Side Constraints (Nozick)]] / [[Concepts/Entitlement Theory (Nozick)]]:** Nozick radicalizes the split: need is not a liberty-claim at all; patterned transfers *are* unfreedom. Berlin’s 1969 wolves/sheep and welfare-state sentences refuse that hardening.
- **[[Concepts/State of Nature, Property, and Revolution (Locke)]]:** property as a condition of using negative liberty is the liberal background Tully and the editors say Berlin both needs and will not theorize.
- **[[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]:** “economic slavery” is the name Berlin treats as claptrap unless a “specific arrangement” can be shown (TC, 170). Tully/Gould treat that showing as ordinary, not a special Marxist theory.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Tully vs Crowder: constituent liberty vs valuable conditions. Same 1958/1969 sentences.
- > [!warning] If conditions can be imposed in liberty’s name, negative liberty has its own “forced to be free.” See [[Contradictions/Berlin vs Rousseau - Negative Liberty vs Forced to Be Free]] (liberal-despotism horn).
- Editors’ further point (disability, technology): there is no firm line between “mere incapacity” and alterable social arrangements (no ramp, no Braille). Berlin does not have this argument.
- Reception, not primary.
