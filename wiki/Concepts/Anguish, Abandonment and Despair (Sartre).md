# Anguish, Abandonment and Despair (Sartre)
> The three technical names Sartre gives, in the 1945/46 lecture, to the experience of being a legislator without God, without a human nature, and without hope beyond one's own action.

## Summary

In *Existentialism is a Humanism* the triad is offered as a clarification, "perhaps a little grandiloquent," of what follows once [[Concepts/Existence Precedes Essence (Sartre)|existence precedes essence]]. The three terms answer the charge of quietism: none of them is a mood that stops action. Anguish is the condition of action; abandonment is the situation in which one must invent; despair is the discipline of counting only on what one can do.

**Anguish.** "When a man commits himself to anything, fully realising that he is not only choosing what he will be, but is thereby at the same time a legislator deciding for the whole of mankind — in such a moment a man cannot escape from the sense of complete and profound responsibility." Those who show no anxiety are disguising it or fleeing it. The shrug "everyone does not do so" is self-deception: the lie already implies the universal value it denies. Sartre names this Kierkegaard's "anguish of Abraham": an angel commands the sacrifice, but "if a voice speaks to me, it is still I myself who must decide whether the voice is or is not that of an angel." There will be no sign that I am Abraham, yet "I also am obliged at every instant to perform actions which are examples." The military leader who interprets a general order and sends men to their death knows this anguish; "it does not prevent their acting, on the contrary it is the very condition of their action."

**Abandonment.** "A favorite word of Heidegger" — here it means only that God does not exist and that the consequences must be drawn to the end. Sartre opposes the French "radicalism" of ~1880, which discarded God as a costly hypothesis and then re-inscribed honesty, progress, and humanity as *a priori* values "in an intelligible heaven." "There can no longer be any good *a priori*, since there is no infinite and perfect consciousness to think it." Dostoevsky's "If God did not exist, everything would be permitted" is "the starting point." No human nature to explain the act; no values or commands to legitimise it. "We are left alone, without excuse." Hence: "man is condemned to be free. Condemned, because he did not create himself, yet is nevertheless at liberty, and from the moment that he is thrown into this world he is responsible for everything he does." Passion is not a torrent that excuses; signs must be interpreted; "every man, without any support or help whatever, is condemned at every instant to invent man." Ponge: "Man is the future of man" — true only if the future is not already laid up in Heaven.

The **pupil** is the worked example. Father a would-be collaborator, elder brother killed in 1940, mother living only for him; he must choose between staying and joining the Free French. Christian charity, Kant's "never regard another as a means," and "trust in our instincts" all fail to decide: both roads are charitable, both treat someone as end and someone as means, and the strength of a feeling is known only by the deed that defines it (Gide: play-acting and vital sentiment are hardly distinguishable). To choose an adviser is already to choose the advice. Sartre's only reply: "You are free, therefore choose, that is to say, invent." The Jesuit who read a run of failures as a vocation still chose that reading; he might have become a carpenter or a revolutionary.

**Despair.** "We limit ourselves to a reliance upon that which is within our wills, or within the sum of the probabilities which render our action feasible." One counts on the train running; one does not count on a prevenient design that will adapt the world to one's will. Descartes's "Conquer yourself rather than the world" means, "at bottom, the same — that we should act without hope." Against Marxists who demand reliance on comrades in China and Russia and on those who will finish the revolution after one's death: one may count on a party whose movements one knows, as one counts on the tram; one cannot count on human nature or on the goodness of unknown men. "I do not know where the Russian revolution will lead." Tomorrow men may establish Fascism, "and so much the worse for us." "In reality, things will be such as men have decided they shall be." This is not quietism ("let others do what I cannot do"): "one need not hope in order to undertake one's work."

## Key Claims / Positions

- Anguish = responsibility of legislating for mankind; condition of action, not a bar to it.
- Abandonment = God gone, no *a priori* good, no excuse; condemned to be free; invent.
- Despair = count only on one's will and the probabilities internal to the action; act without hope.
- Feeling, signs, advisers, and abstract ethics (Christian, Kantian) do not decide the concrete case.
- The triad is the lecture's public vocabulary. BN's *angoisse* is a different, prior analysis.

### From *Being and Nothingness*, Part One ch. 1 (2026-08-12)

BN anguish is not legislating for mankind. It is "the mode in which freedom is conscious of itself" (BN 29). Fear is of something in the world; anguish is anguish before myself. **Vertigo**: "I am afraid not of falling over the precipice, but of throwing myself over" (BN 29). The **gambler** whose resolution not to gamble is "me in the mode of not-being": nothing in the past resolution *is* me now. Motives are *for* consciousness, hence ineffective. "I emerge alone and in anguish … Nothing can ensure me against myself … without justification and without excuse" (BN 39). Everyday action, values-as-things, psychological determinism, and Bergson's "profound self" are flight. Flight from anguish *is* anguish; that structure is already [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)|bad faith]].

Abandonment and despair are not a BN triad. "Condemned to be free" appears in the Temporality chapter ("To be free is to be condemned to be free," BN 129) and will be unpacked as situation and responsibility in Part Four. The lecture's Abraham / commander / pupil examples are a public ethics of the legislator; the treatise's cliff and gambler are the ontology of a nothingness between me and my past.

Keep both: lecture anguish is toward other men; BN anguish is before myself. Whether they are one phenomenon is not settled by either source.

### How This Compares to Other Thinkers in the Wiki

- **vs [[Concepts/Anxiety (Angst) (Heidegger)]]**: Heidegger's *Angst* is objectless world-collapse that individualizes Dasein before death. Sartre's *angoisse* in the lecture is the anguish of the *legislator* — Abraham, the commander — and is explicitly "towards other men who are concerned." Welten's BN gloss (vertigo at the cliff: nothing prevents me from throwing myself in) sits between them.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Søren Kierkegaard]]**: Sartre takes Abraham as the model of undecidable authority ("if a voice speaks to me, it is still I myself who must decide whether the voice is or is not that of an angel") and drops the theological frame. Johannes *de silentio* already has Abraham believing God exists; the trial is not the voice's identity but whether he can believe he will get Isaac back *for this life* while drawing the knife. Sartre's legislator (and his commander interpreting a general order) is closer to Johannes's **tragic hero** — someone who still has the universal — than to Abraham, who does nothing for the universal and cannot speak. See [[Sources/Fear and Trembling - Kierkegaard (Hannay 1985)]], [[Concepts/Teleological Suspension of the Ethical (Kierkegaard)]].
- **vs [[Thinkers/Kant]]**: the pupil is a worked counter-example to the claim that the Formula of Humanity decides the case. See [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)]].
- **vs [[Thinkers/Descartes]]**: "Conquer yourself rather than the world" is enlisted as despair — act without hope — and so touches [[Concepts/Provisional Morality (Descartes)]].
- **vs [[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: one may join a party without illusion; one may not treat the future revolution as a fact of human nature.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The lecture's anguish (universal legislator) is closer to a Kantian publicity test than to Heidegger's objectless *Angst* or to Welten's vertigo-of-freedom. Whether these are one phenomenon under three descriptions is not settled by this source.
- "Fascism will then be the truth of man" if men so decide: the same absence of human nature that grounds freedom also removes any essence that could *falsify* a Fascist future.
