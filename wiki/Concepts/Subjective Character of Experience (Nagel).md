# Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)

> An organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to *be* that organism — something it is like *for* the organism. That "what it is like" is the subjective character of experience, and it is what current reductions of the mental leave out.

## Summary

This is the load-bearing concept of "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974). Nagel introduces it as the mark of consciousness, not as a theory of how consciousness works. The English idiom is slightly misleading: "what it is like" does not mean "what (in our experience) it resembles," but "how it is for the subject himself."

Two constraints make the concept do philosophical work:

1. **It is not captured by functional, causal, or intentional analysis.** Those ascriptions are compatible with the absence of experience — they could hold of robots or automata that "behaved like people though they experienced nothing." Nagel does not deny that conscious states cause behavior or admit functional characterization; he denies that this exhausts them. (A footnote allows that perhaps nothing complex enough to behave like a person *would* lack experience — but that, if true, cannot be read off the concept of experience.)
2. **It is essentially tied to a point of view**, and the point of view is a *type*, not a private inner object. One person can know or say of another what the quality of the other's experience is, but only if they are similar enough to adopt that point of view. Privacy and incorrigibility are the wrong glosses: we are not incorrigible about experience, and animals without language have experience without beliefs about it.

The bat is an existence proof that there can be facts here whose specific character we cannot conceive. Most bats (Microchiroptera) perceive by sonar. We may ascribe general types — three-dimensional forward perception, pain, fear, hunger, lust — but not the specific subjective character of echolocation. Imagination only yields what it would be like for *us* to behave as bats. The same structure holds between humans (congenitally deaf-blind) and would hold for Martians trying to conceive us. Nagel infers a realism about **facts beyond the reach of human concepts**.

This is why physical reduction, which elsewhere is a move toward greater objectivity (abandoning a species-specific appearance), cannot be applied to experience in the same way. Lightning has an objective character not exhausted by its look; a blind Martian can still investigate it. "What would be left of what it was like to be a bat if one removed the viewpoint of the bat?" If experience has no objective nature apart from the point of view from which it is lived, a physiologist is not observing the same phenomenon "from another point of view" when looking at a brain.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: adopts Nagel's phrase as the definition of phenomenal consciousness and builds the [[Concepts/Hard Problem of Consciousness (Chalmers)]] and the [[Concepts/Phenomenal and Psychological Concepts of Mind (Chalmers)|phenomenal/psychological split]] on it. Chalmers's [[Arguments/Zombie Argument (Chalmers)]] is the 1974 robot-without-experience remark turned into a conceivability argument against logical supervenience.
- **[[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]]**: denies that a determinate, theory-independent "what it's like" has been established. [[Concepts/Heterophenomenology (Dennett)]] studies reports without granting intrinsic phenomenal properties; [[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]] treats the four-feature qualia package (intrinsic, ineffable, private, directly apprehensible) as either dispositional or incoherent. Nagel's actual view is a poorer fit for that package than Jackson's: he rejects privacy and incorrigibility, and he does not need a Cartesian Theater.
- **[[Thinkers/Frank Jackson]]** (1982 §III, now primary): indebted to this concept and then *distinguishes* his [[Arguments/Knowledge Argument (Jackson)|knowledge argument]] from it. He is not complaining that we fail to know what it is like to *be* Fred (*de se*, point of view, imaginative extrapolation). He is complaining of a **property of the experience**. Physicalism "makes no special claims about the imaginative or extrapolative powers of human beings." Nagel's leftover is a point of view; Jackson's leftover is a fact.
- **[[Thinkers/Andy Clark]]**: calls the puzzle a "trick of the mind" generated by simplified, self-opaque self-models ([[Concepts/Predictive Processing and the Hard Problem (Clark)]]). That is Dennett's diagnosis with a predictive-processing mechanism.
- **[[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]]**: the first-personal givenness of experience is the regional essence of Consciousness, irreducible to Nature. Nagel supplies the analytic slogan; Husserl supplies the ontological machinery ([[Contradictions/Husserl vs Naturalism — Phenomenal Consciousness and the Hard Problem]]).
- **[[Thinkers/John Searle]]**: agrees that something essential is missing from purely formal description; locates it in biological causal powers rather than in a point of view.
- **Jefferson / Turing**: the [[Concepts/Argument from Consciousness (Jefferson)]] is an earlier, less precise form of the same demand, aimed at machines. Turing answers it with the Imitation Game; Nagel would say the Game tests the functional remainder and leaves the subjective character untouched.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Whether the "what it's like" is a datum or a Theater-generated intuition is the vault's central phenomenology dispute — [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]].
- [[Thinkers/Anil Seth]] adopts Nagel's 1974 definition as the mark of consciousness ("any phenomenology counts") and then treats it as a *real-problem* explanandum, not a bar to mechanistic explanation. The bat appears in *Being You* ch. 12 only as echolocating *Umwelt*; Nagel is not named there. See [[Concepts/Animal Consciousness and the Beast Machine (Seth)]].
- Nagel himself asks the deeper question his critics often skip: does it even make sense to ask what experiences are *really* like, as opposed to how they appear to the subject? Without an answer, the hypothesis that a physical description captures their nature cannot be understood.
