# Intellectual Conscience (Nietzsche)
> The desire for certainty as one's inmost craving — and the contempt for living by valuations one has never given reasons for.

## Summary

*The Gay Science* §2 names it. Kaufmann calls the section one of the most important in the book. "The great majority of people lacks an *intellectual conscience*." They handle their scales, calling this good and that evil, and do not blush when told the weights are underweight. They "do not consider it contemptible to believe this or that and to live accordingly, without first having given themselves an account of the final and most certain reasons pro and con, and without even troubling themselves about such reasons afterward." Gifted men and noble women still belong to this majority.

What separates "higher human beings" from lower is not goodheartedness, refinement, or genius, but whether one "account[s] the desire for certainty as his inmost craving and deepest distress." Pious hatred of reason at least betrays a *bad* intellectual conscience. What is contemptible is "to stand in the midst of this *rerum concordia discors* and of this whole marvelous uncertainty and rich ambiguity of existence without questioning, without trembling with the craving and the rapture of such questioning."

***Dawn* (1881) already uses the name.** §149: conforming in practice (baptism, army, church wedding) while reserving “intellectual liberty” “lull[s] to sleep the intellectual conscience” and re-sanctions the custom; little unconventional *actions* matter more than opinions. §298: someone knows how it was done — “his intellectual conscience.” §370: “Make it a rule never to withhold or conceal from yourself anything that may be thought against your own thoughts.” The 1886 Preface's remaining “thou shalt” and “men of *this* conscience” belong to the same campaign. GS §2 is still the *definition*; Dawn is the earlier *practice*. See [[Sources/The Dawn of Day - Nietzsche (Kennedy, Collection 2013)]], [[Concepts/Faith in Morals (Nietzsche)]].

This is not yet the later suspicion of the will to truth. It *is* that will as a demand on persons: do not live by unexamined valuations. Book V then turns the same honesty against itself. **§344**: science rests on a faith — "Nothing is needed more than truth" — and "even we godless anti-metaphysicians still take our fire… from the flame… that truth is divine." **§335** ("Long live physics!"): conscience-judgments have a prehistory; honesty now compels us to become *physicists* of the lawful and necessary in order to be creators. See [[Concepts/Death of God (Nietzsche)]], [[Concepts/Ascetic Ideal (Nietzsche)]].

**Johnston *Genealogy* III.24** names the last idealists as "the only ones in whom intellectual conscience lives" and still denies they are free spirits: "They are not free spirits… for they still believe in the truth." **III.27** calls the same thing "intellectual cleanliness at any price" — Christian truthfulness become scientific conscience, which first kills God and then must ask what the will to truth *means*. The 1882 demand and the 1887 suspicion are one honesty at two dates. See [[Sources/On the Genealogy of Morals - Nietzsche (Johnston)]].

## Key Claims

- Intellectual conscience is rare; its absence is the default, including among the gifted.
- The test is whether one has given *final reasons pro and con* for what one lives by — not whether one is kind, refined, or brilliant.
- The surrounding "rich ambiguity of existence" makes the refusal to question contemptible.
- 1887 complication: the same honesty, made unconditional, is still piety toward a dead God (§344).
- **BGE §227 / §230 (1886, between GS 1882 and Book V 1887).** Honesty is “the virtue of which we cannot rid ourselves, we free spirits” — labor at it, do not let it become vanity or boredom. Knowing is “a kind of cruelty of the intellectual conscience and taste” against the spirit’s will to appearance. Task: “translate man back again into nature.” Same honesty, now as leftover virtue and as cruelty, not yet §344’s suspicion that the will to truth is a will to death.
- ***The Antichrist* (1888).** Preface: integrity to the verge of hardness; never ask whether truth is profit or fatality. §12: Kantian “practical reason” dresses a dearth of intellectual conscience. §47: faith vetoes science. §§50–54: “Faith makes blessed: *therefore*, it lies”; “Faith means the will to avoid knowing what is true”; philology as *ephexis* in interpretation; blood is the worst testimony to truth. The 1882 demand now has a named enemy: priestly faith. See [[Sources/The Antichrist - Nietzsche (Mencken, Collection 2013)]].

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]]** — §2 is the free-spirit demand; §344 is the free-spirit's self-critique. *Genealogy* will call this honesty the last remnant of the ascetic ideal.
- **[[Concepts/Philosophy as Involuntary and Unconscious Autobiography (Nietzsche)]]** — BGE §6: every philosophy is a confession. Intellectual conscience is the demand to make that confession *conscious* and to give reasons.
- **[[Thinkers/Socrates]]** — Hadot's figure already lives by giving an account of one's life (*Laches*). Nietzsche's §2 is a modern, anti-herd version of that demand; §340 then accuses the dying Socrates of a hidden verdict against life.
- **[[Thinkers/William James]]** — *The Will to Believe* licenses faith where evidence cannot decide. Nietzsche §347 measures the *need* to believe as weakness. Kinship of temperament, opposite verdict on the right to ungrounded conviction.
- **[[Thinkers/René Descartes]]** — §276 plays "Sum, ergo cogito: cogito, ergo sum" as New Year's weather, not as method. Intellectual conscience is not hyperbolic doubt; it is shame at slack faith.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] §2's "desire for certainty" and §344's "will to truth" as possible will to death are the same honesty at two dates (1882 / 1887). Do not collapse them, and do not treat the later suspicion as a recantation of §2.
