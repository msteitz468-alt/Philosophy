# Cultural Lexicon - Words vs Concepts (Skinner)

> Chapter 9 of [[Sources/Visions of Politics Volume 1 - Regarding Method - Quentin Skinner (2002)|*Regarding Method*]]: "**what we need to know is not what words they use but rather what concepts they possess**." Raymond Williams's *Keywords* is the stalking-horse; the target is the whole practice of writing a history of a concept by tracking a word.

## Words Are Not Concepts

Grasping a word's meaning is neither necessary nor sufficient for possessing the corresponding concept:

- **Not necessary** — Milton had the concept of originality a century before the word was available.
- **Not sufficient** — following Kant and Wittgenstein, one may falsely believe one possesses a concept (*being*, *infinity*).

A systematic relationship nonetheless holds, and it is what makes lexical history worth doing at all: "The surest sign that a group or society has entered into the self-conscious possession of a new concept is that a corresponding vocabulary will be developed." But the inference runs one way only, and Skinner's warning is blunt: a survey of a word's history "would by no means be the same as a history of the concept."

## What Williams Missed

Williams claimed that studying "variations and confusions of meaning" gives an "extra edge of consciousness" about social debate. Skinner's objection is that Williams never isolates the class of terms that matter — the **appraisive** or "strong," value-involving terms — and so can speak only vaguely of "changes of meaning."

Mastering an appraisive term requires three distinct things:

1. its **criteria of application** (its sense);
2. its **range of reference**;
3. the **range of speech acts** it standardly performs — the attitudes it expresses and solicits.

> To call an action courageous is not merely to describe it but to place it in a specific moral light.

Because Williams collapses (2) and (3) into (1), he reads every dispute as a dispute about meaning. In fact there are three different kinds:

- **Over criteria** — Duchamp's readymades are not a quarrel about the word *art* but the advancing of a rival social theory.
- **Over whether the criteria obtain** — are housewives "exploited"?
- **Over evaluative force** — *myth*, *elite*, *liberal*, *black*, *queer*.

Skinner notes that Hampshire commits the same conflation in his debate about the term "political."

## Polysemy as the Trace of Failure

The striking result: arguments of the second kind, when they *succeed*, yield new **social perceptions**, not new meanings. New meanings arise when they fail.

> It is only when such arguments fail that new meanings tend to arise.

Hence polysemy — a word acquiring divergent senses — is the archaeological trace of an ideological defeat. His examples: *religious*; corporate uses of "philosophy" and "literature"; *patriot* (Johnson's dictionary: "a factious disturber of the government"); *naughty*; *commodity*.

## Holism

Because "a term such as art gains its meaning from the place it occupies within an entire conceptual scheme," changing its criteria changes "a great deal else besides." Skinner sides with Putnam against Feyerabend's excesses here, while judging Putnam overstated on *gold*.

## The Causal Payoff

The chapter ends by turning Williams's own metaphor around. Williams treats language as a mirror of social reality; Skinner argues the causal arrows can point the other way. The merchant must tailor his projects to the moral language available to describe them, so normative vocabulary is "not an epiphenomenon of their projects, but one of the determinants of their behaviour," and "our social vocabulary helps to constitute the character of those practices." He endorses Charles Taylor on "the artificiality of the distinction between social reality and the language of description of that social reality."

This is the same conclusion reached in [[Concepts/Innovating Ideologist and Legitimation (Skinner)]], arrived at from the lexical rather than the ideological side.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] A working constraint on this wiki itself. Many vault concept pages are named for a *term* and track it across thinkers. Skinner's argument is that this is legitimate only if the page tracks *uses in argument* rather than presupposing a stable concept underneath — which is roughly what the vault's "How Different Thinkers Use This" convention already does, but the pressure is real for pages that narrate a term's development as a single story.
- > [!warning] Against [[Thinkers/Arthur O. Lovejoy]]: this is the lexical form of the anti-unit-idea argument. See [[Concepts/Mythologies in the History of Ideas (Skinner)]] and [[Concepts/Rhetorical Redescription and Paradiastole (Skinner)]].
- Note: Skinner records that Williams withdrew or modified many of the 1976 *Keywords* claims in the 1983 edition. His critique is aimed at the position, and he says so.
