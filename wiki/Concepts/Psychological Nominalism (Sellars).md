# Psychological Nominalism (Sellars)

> "All awareness of *sorts, resemblances, facts*, etc., in short, all awareness of abstract entities — indeed, all awareness even of particulars — is a **linguistic affair**" (§29). The doctrine that closes off every remaining exit from the [[Concepts/Myth of the Given (Sellars)|Myth of the Given]].

## The Statement

Sellars reaches the doctrine by asking what an association between a word and the world would have to be. If the association is mediated by an awareness of facts of the form *x resembles y*, "we are back with an unacquired ability to be aware of repeatables, in this case the repeatable *resemblance*." If it is mediated by the awareness that particulars *are red*, "the conceptualistic form of the myth has merely been replaced by a realistic version, as in the classical sense-datum theory." The remaining option:

> If, however, the association is not mediated by the awareness of facts either of the form *x resembles y* or of the form *x is φ*, then we have a view of the general type which I will call **psychological nominalism**, according to which all awareness of sorts, resemblances, facts, etc., in short, all awareness of abstract entities — indeed, all awareness even of particulars — is a linguistic affair. According to it, not even the awareness of such sorts, resemblances, and facts as pertain to so-called immediate experience is presupposed by the process of acquiring the use of a language.

Rorty draws out the consequence: since Sellars, "like the later Wittgenstein but unlike Kant, identified the possession of a concept with the mastery of the use of a word… **mastery of a language is prerequisite of conscious experience**." The doctrine "entails that Locke, Berkeley, and Hume were wrong in thinking that we are 'aware of certain determinate sorts … simply by virtue of having sensations and images'" (§28).

## The Historical Argument (§§26–29)

Sellars's route to the doctrine runs through the British empiricists' treatment of abstract ideas, and his key historical observation is that they *shared a presupposition they never noticed*:

- **[[Thinkers/Locke]]** — for Locke, "the problem of how we come to be aware of determinate sense repeatables is *no problem at all*. Merely by virtue of having sensations and images we have this awareness." The problem of abstract ideas is only the problem of *determinables* (how we think of colour as against crimson). Locke's "additive theory of specification" — the idea of a determinate *A* as the idea of being *A* plus something — "won't account for the relation of the idea of being *red* to the idea of being *crimson*," because he treated conjunction as the fundamental relation for building complex ideas and so "precluded himself from giving even a plausible account" of the determinable/determinate relation. Sellars speculates on how differently things might have gone had Locke admitted *disjunctive* complex ideas.
- **[[Thinkers/George Berkeley]]** — his question is not "how do we go from ideas of particulars to ideas of repeatables?" but "granted that in immediate experience we are aware of absolutely specific sense qualities, how do we come to be conscious of *generic* properties?" Berkeley insists "we can have an idea of a genus only by having an idea of the genus as… '*contracted*' into one of its species."
- **[[Thinkers/Hume]]** — likewise "takes for granted the ability to be aware of determinate repeatables." Hume differs from the other two only in *denying* that there are occurrent thoughts of determinables while granting occurrent thoughts of determinate repeatables. "However much Locke, Berkeley, and Hume differ on the problem of abstract ideas, they take for granted that the human mind has an innate ability to be aware of certain determinate sorts… simply by virtue of having sensations and images."

"Now, it takes but a small twist of Hume's position to get a radically different view" — the twist being to deny that innate ability outright.

Sellars adds two immediate qualifications (§29):
1. The Ryle-flavoured version he sketches — treating a verbal episode as the manifestation of associations of word-object and word-word types, "a mongrel categorical-hypothetical" — "is impossibly crude and inadequate as an account of even the simplest concept." Psychological nominalism is not behaviorism about meaning.
2. Once sensations are "purged of epistemic aboutness," the reason for thinking the fundamental word-world tie must run between words and *immediate experiences* disappears; "the way is clear to recognizing that basic word-world associations hold, for example, between 'red' and red *physical objects*, rather than between 'red' and a supposed class of private red particulars." This does not deny that private sensations may be *causally* essential to forming those connections.

## The Language-Learner Fallacy (§30)

A separate source of the Myth, to which even philosophers "suspicious of the idea of inner episodes can fall prey": when we picture someone learning a language, "it is we who are familiar with his logical space" — and so "we run the danger, if we are not careful, of picturing the language learner as having an almost *ex nihilo* degree of awareness… of this same logical space. We picture his state as though it were rather like our own when placed in a strange forest on a dark night."

The mistake is "to take for granted that the process of teaching a child to use a language is that of teaching it to *discriminate* elements within a logical space of particulars, universals, facts, etc., of which it is already undiscriminatingly aware, and to associate these discriminated elements with verbal symbols." Crucially: "this mistake is in principle the same whether the logical space… is conceived to be that of physical objects or of private sense contents." Escaping sense-data does not escape the myth.

Sellars adds a methodological test: "The real test of a theory of language lies not in its account of what has been called (by H. H. Price) 'thinking in absence,' but in its account of 'thinking in presence'" — the perceptual occasions where the temptation to posit a given is greatest.

## The Logic of 'Means' (§31, §VII)

A supporting argument clears away a semantic obstacle. It is tempting to think that "red" means the quality red by virtue of two facts: it has the syntax of a predicate, and we tend to respond to red objects with "This is red." But that account — which H. H. Price "correctly stigmatized as the 'thermometer view'" — draws its plausibility from assimilating

> "*Rot*" means red

to relational statements like "Cowley adjoins Oxford," i.e. to the form *xRy*. "Once one assimilates the form… and then takes it for granted that *meaning* is a relation between a word and a nonverbal entity, it is tempting to suppose that the relation in question is that of *association*."

Sellars's correction: "statements of the form '… means ---' are **not relational statements**." The rubric "is a linguistic device for conveying the information that a mentioned word, in this case a German word, plays the same role in a certain linguistic economy… as does the word 'red,' which is not mentioned but *used*." This is why "'*Und*' means *and*" and "'*Rot*' means *red*" can convey quite different kinds of information without either committing us to a "meaning relation" holding to Conjunction or to Redness.

The payoff:

> Nothing whatever can be inferred about the complexity of the role played by the word "red"… from the truth of the semantical statement "'red' means the quality red." And no consideration arising from the '*Fido*'-Fido aspect of the grammar of "means" precludes one from claiming that the role of the word "red"… is a complicated one indeed, and that one cannot understand the meaning of the word "red" — "know what redness is" — unless one has a great deal of knowledge which classical empiricism would have held to have a purely contingent relationship with the possession of fundamental empirical concepts.

This is the semantic counterpart of the holism argued in [[Concepts/The Logic of Looks (Sellars)]]: one has the concept *green* "only by having a whole battery of concepts of which it is one element."

## How This Bears on Other Vault Pages

- **[[Concepts/Impressions and Ideas - Copy Principle (Hume)]]** — Hume's meaning-test ("from what impression is that idea derived?") presupposes exactly the innate awareness of determinate repeatables that psychological nominalism denies. If Sellars is right, the Copy Principle cannot do the work Hume asks of it, because *recognizing* an impression as of a given sort is already a learned, linguistic achievement.
- **[[Concepts/Knowledge as Perception of Agreement or Disagreement of Ideas (Locke)]]** and **[[Concepts/Primary and Secondary Qualities (Locke)]]** — both presuppose a pre-linguistic awareness of ideas and their sorts.
- **[[Concepts/Concept Possession as Ability (Wittgenstein)]]** and **[[Concepts/Rule-Following (Wittgenstein)]]** — the closest ally. Sellars identifies concept possession with mastery of a word's use, and Rorty reads his argument as spelling out "the moral of many of the aphorisms of *Philosophical Investigations*."
- **[[Concepts/Analytic-Synthetic Distinction (Quine)]]** — a parallel attack on the idea that meaning can be settled independently of a whole web.
- **[[Concepts/Archaeology of Knowledge (Foucault)]]** — a distant cousin: awareness structured by a prior discursive framework rather than by unmediated contact with objects, though Foucault's framework is historical-institutional rather than semantic.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Head-on collision with classical empiricism as the vault holds it — [[Thinkers/Locke]], [[Thinkers/George Berkeley]], [[Thinkers/Hume]]. All three take awareness of determinate sensory sorts to be innate and pre-linguistic; Sellars denies it and shows the assumption is *shared and unargued* across their disagreements about abstract ideas. This is the vault's sharpest single challenge to the empiricist starting point.
- > [!warning] The doctrine is strikingly strong: it appears to entail that pre-linguistic infants and non-linguistic animals have no *awareness* in the relevant sense — a consequence critics have pressed hard. Sellars's own guard is that sensations remain real and causally efficacious; what they lack is epistemic *aboutness*. Whether that guard is enough is a live question, and one [[Thinkers/John McDowell]] takes up directly.
- Note the label: "nominalism" here is *psychological*, about awareness, not the medieval metaphysical thesis about universals. Sellars is explicit that the classical problem of universals and the empiricist problem of abstract ideas are not the same problem.
