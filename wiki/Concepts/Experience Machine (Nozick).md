# Experience Machine (Nozick)
> What matters is not only how life feels from the inside. We want to *do* things, to *be* a certain sort of person, and to be in actual contact with a deeper-than-man-made reality.

## Summary

The experience machine is a short section of *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* Ch. 3, written to block a hedonist reading of “what matters for people” — and, locally, to block felt experience as the sole limit on treating animals. Reception has treated it as a free-standing argument against hedonism and as a test for virtual reality. This page is the **primary**.

Suppose a machine could give you any experience you desired, indistinguishable from the inside. Neuroscientists stimulate your brain; you would think you were writing a great novel, making a friend, reading an interesting book. Should you plug in for life? Nozick thinks not, for three reasons:

1. **Do.** “We want to *do* certain things, and not just have the experience of doing them.”
2. **Be.** “We want to *be* a certain way, to be a certain sort of person.” Someone floating in a tank is an “indeterminate blob.” “Plugging into the machine is a kind of suicide.”
3. **Contact.** “Plugging into an experience machine limits us to a man-made reality… There is no *actual* contact with any deeper reality, though the experience of it can be simulated.”

Two successor machines do not repair this. A **transformation machine** transforms you into whatever sort of person you want to be. A **result machine** produces the results in the world that your activity would have produced. Still “we want to live (an active verb) ourselves, in contact with reality. (And this, machines cannot do *for* us.)”

In the book the example is not about VR. It is a probe: until “what matters for people” is settled and shown **not** to apply to animals, felt experience cannot be the sole constraint on how animals may be treated. Drugs are mentioned as a local, limited experience machine.

## How This Relates to Prior Thinkers

- **[[Thinkers/John Stuart Mill]]** / Bentham: the machine is a thought-experiment against experientialism, including qualitative hedonism. Mill’s higher pleasures still live *inside* experience; Nozick’s three reasons point outside it.
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]** / [[Concepts/Value in Virtual Worlds (Chalmers)]]: *Reality+* ch. 17 answers the three reasons by calling VR a **reality machine**, not an experience machine — known, interactive, shared. That is a reply, not a restatement. Reception flattened: the local job (animals / “felt from the inside”); the “suicide”/blob line; the transformation and result machines; “live ourselves.”
- **[[Thinkers/Andy Clark]]** / [[Sources/The Experience Machine - Andy Clark (2023)]]: title reused for **predictive processing** — ordinary perception is already a constructed “controlled hallucination.” That is a *descriptive* claim that experience is machine-like. Nozick’s is a *normative* refusal of hedonism. The shared title has taught later agents the wrong thesis.
- **[[Thinkers/Plato]]**: the Cave’s verdict that the outside is more valuable is the contact reason in another key. Chalmers denies that verdict for virtual worlds.
- **[[Concepts/Side Constraints (Nozick)]]**: the machine sits in the chapter that argues we are not goal-maximizers about what matters; persons have lives to *lead*.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Chalmers’s parity thesis (life in rich VR can have the same value as nonvirtual life) is a reply to this page, not a reading of it. See [[Concepts/Value in Virtual Worlds (Chalmers)]].
- Survey result Chalmers cites — 13% of philosophers would enter, 77% would not — is reception, not in Nozick.
- The book does not decide whether a *known, interactive, shared* simulation would still fail the contact reason. That is Chalmers’s question.
