# Existence Precedes Essence (Sartre)
> The first principle of existentialism, stated for the public in 1945/46: man first exists, surges up in the world, and defines himself afterwards — there is no human nature, because there is no God to have a conception of it.

## Summary

The lecture is the canonical statement. Welten's 2020 biography already had the slogan; this page is now keyed to Sartre's own argument.

**The paper-knife.** An article of manufacture (a book, a paper-knife) is made by an artisan who has a conception of it and a pre-existent technique. Its essence — "the sum of the formulae and the qualities which made its production and its definition possible" — precedes its existence. Production precedes existence.

**God as supernal artisan.** "When we think of God as the creator, we are thinking of him, most of the time, as a supernal artisan." In Descartes and Leibniz the will follows, or at least accompanies, the understanding: God knows precisely what he is creating. The conception of man in the divine understanding is to man as the artisan's formula is to the paper-knife. Each individual is the realisation of a certain conception.

**Eighteenth-century atheism keeps the essence.** Diderot, Voltaire, and Kant suppress God but not the priority of essence. "Man possesses a human nature; that 'human nature,' which is the conception of human being, is found in every man; which means that each man is a particular example of a universal conception, the conception of Man." In Kant the universality is so complete that "the wild man of the woods, man in the state of nature and the bourgeois are all contained in the same definition and have the same fundamental qualities." Essence still precedes historic existence.

**Atheistic existentialism is more consistent.** "If God does not exist there is at least one being whose existence comes before its essence, a being which exists before it can be defined by any conception of it. That being is man or, as Heidegger has it, the human reality." "Man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world — and defines himself afterwards." He is not definable to begin with "because to begin with he is nothing. He will not be anything until later, and then he will be what he makes of himself. Thus, there is no human nature, because there is no God to have a conception of it." "Man is nothing else but that which he makes of himself. That is the first principle of existentialism."

Not what he *wishes* to be (a later, conscious decision — to join a party, write a book, marry — is usually the manifestation of a prior spontaneous decision) but what he *wills* after the leap toward existence. "If, however, it is true that existence is prior to essence, man is responsible for what he is." The first effect is to put every man in possession of himself and to place "the entire responsibility for his existence squarely upon his own shoulders."

**Choosing for all.** Subjectivism means both the freedom of the individual and — "the deeper meaning of existentialism" — that man cannot pass beyond human subjectivity. "In choosing for himself he chooses for all men." Every action creates "an image of man such as he believes he ought to be." "What we choose is always the better; and nothing can be better for us unless it is better for all." The worker who joins a Christian rather than a Communist union signifies resignation as the attitude that best becomes a man, and "resignation is my will for everyone." The decision to marry and have children "commits not only myself, but humanity as a whole, to the practice of monogamy." "In fashioning myself I fashion man."

**Human condition, not human nature.** Later in the lecture Sartre grants a universality that is not an essence: the **condition** of being in the world, having to labour and to die. Historical situations vary (slave, feudal baron, proletarian); those limitations do not. They are objective (met everywhere) and subjective (nothing if not lived). Every purpose is an attempt to surpass, widen, deny, or accommodate them, and is therefore comprehensible across cultures. "There is a human universality, but it is not something given; it is being perpetually made."

### From *Being and Nothingness*, Introduction (2026-08-12)

BN does not use the slogan "existence precedes essence." It states a tighter, regional claim about *consciousness*. After locating the pre-reflective cogito, Sartre writes that the type of being of consciousness is the opposite of the ontological proof of God: "Since consciousness is not possible before being, but since its being is the source and condition of all possibility, its existence implies its essence" (BN lv). Husserl's "necessity of fact": for there to be an essence of pleasure, there must first be the fact of a consciousness (of) this pleasure. There is no law *of* consciousness — a law is a transcendent object; there can be consciousness of a law. "The ontological error of Cartesian rationalism is not to have seen that if the absolute is defined by the primacy of existence over essence, it can not be conceived as a substance" (BN lvi).

So the lecture's public slogan (no God → no human nature → man is what he makes of himself) and BN's technical claim (consciousness's existence implies its essence; therefore not a substance) are not the same sentence. The lecture *popularises* a consequence; the Introduction *argues* a regional ontology of the for-itself. Welten's gloss below reads the slogan off pour-soi / en-soi rather than off either sentence.

### Welten's ontological gloss (from the biography)

Welten reads the slogan off the pour-soi / en-soi distinction rather than off the artisan analogy. Being-in-itself simply *is* what it is and has an essence; the human being is consciousness, which *is not*, and so "exists" in the literal sense of *ex-sistere* — standing out, perpetually stepping outside itself. A human *with* an essence is a corpse; "Napoleon, for us today, is nothing but an essence: he is dead." Essence is not abolished: without existence there would be no perspective from which being could appear. See [[Concepts/Being-for-itself and Being-in-itself (Sartre)]].

## Key Claims / Positions

- Artifacts and creatures of a divine artisan: essence precedes existence.
- 18th-c. atheism (Kant included) keeps a universal human nature after dropping God.
- Atheistic existentialism: no God → no human nature → man is what he makes of himself.
- Responsibility is for all men: every choice legislates an image of humanity.
- Universality of *condition* (world, labour, death), not of *nature*; universality is made.

### How Sartre Differs From Prior Thinkers in the Wiki

- **vs [[Thinkers/Aristotle]]** and the classical/medieval tradition: reverses the priority of essence (*to ti ên einai*, formal/final cause) over existence; there is no human *telos* or fixed form.
- **vs the [[Concepts/Great Chain of Being (Lovejoy)|Great Chain of Being]]**: no fixed rung assigns the human its place.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Descartes]] and Leibniz**: God is not a supernal artisan whose concept of man precedes men.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Kant]]**: Kant is named as the limit case of essence-first atheism (one definition covering wild man, natural man, and bourgeois). The lecture later agrees that freedom wills itself and others' freedom, and denies that the formal/universal decides the concrete case.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] If there is no human essence and values are ungrounded, on what basis can Sartre condemn bad faith, cowards, scum, or Fascism? The lecture's answer is the will to freedom as the only end consistent with freedom itself — see [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)]] and [[Concepts/Existential Humanism (Sartre)]]. Whether that answer smuggles an essence back in is the standing problem.
- "Fascism will then be the truth of man" if men so decide: the same thesis that frees also withholds a nature that could convict a Fascist future of *being false to man*.
