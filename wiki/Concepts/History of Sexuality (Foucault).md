# History of Sexuality (Foucault)
> Not a history of Victorian silence or of sexual liberation: a genealogy of how the West was made to speak sex as truth, and of the *dispositif* that produced "sex" as the secret of who we are.

## Summary

*La Volonté de savoir* (Gallimard, 1976; Robert Hurley trans., *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1: An Introduction*, Pantheon 1978) is the vault's primary text for this page. The Macey biography already had the slogans; the book supplies the argument.

The received story — seventeenth-century frankness, then the imperial prude, then Freud as cautious first light — is restated in order to be displaced. Foucault's question is not "Why are we repressed?" but "Why do we say… that we are repressed?" (pp. 8–9). Three doubts (historical, historico-theoretical, historico-political) put the **[[Concepts/Repressive Hypothesis (Foucault)|repressive hypothesis]]** back inside a general economy of discourses on sex since the seventeenth century. The object is "the regime of power-knowledge-pleasure that sustains the discourse on human sexuality in our part of the world" (p. 11) — "the over-all 'discursive fact,' the way in which sex is 'put into discourse.'" Prohibition is not a ruse; making prohibition the constitutive principle of the history *is* a ruse (p. 12).

The counter-history: a "discursive explosion" (p. 17) via the confession of the flesh, the eighteenth-century "police" of sex, the emergence of **population** with sex at its heart, a pedagogy of children's sex, and a nineteenth-century specification of individuals. "As defined by the ancient civil or canonical codes, sodomy was a category of forbidden acts; their perpetrator was nothing more than the juridical subject of them. The nineteenth-century homosexual became a personage, a past, a case history, and a childhood… The sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species" (p. 43). "We must therefore abandon the hypothesis that modern industrial societies ushered in an age of increased sexual repression" (p. 49).

Two procedures for producing the truth of sex: **[[Concepts/Scientia Sexualis and Ars Erotica (Foucault)|ars erotica vs scientia sexualis]]**. The West, "on the face of it," has no erotic art and instead practices a science of sex nucleated in confession. "Sexuality" is "the correlative of that slowly developed discursive practice" (p. 68), not a pre-given object ideology distorts.

The **[[Concepts/Deployment of Sexuality (Foucault)|deployment of sexuality]]** is then analyzed as a historical construct superimposed on the older deployment of alliance. Four strategic unities — hysterization of women's bodies, pedagogization of children's sex, socialization of procreative behavior, psychiatrization of perverse pleasure — go by way of a family that is a *sexualizer*, not a prohibitor. Sexuality is "originally, historically bourgeois." Reich's "sexual revolution" is a tactical shift inside the deployment (p. 131).

Part Five joins this apparatus to a transformation in the right of death: sovereign **take life or let live** is replaced by a power to **foster life or disallow it to the point of death**. Anatomo-politics of the body plus bio-politics of the population = **[[Concepts/Biopolitics and Governmentality (Foucault)|bio-power]]**; the deployment of sexuality is one of the most important nineteenth-century arrangements joining the two poles. "Sex" is not the real anchorage of sexuality but a fictitious, imaginary point the deployment requires. "We must not think that by saying yes to sex, one says no to power" (p. 157). Rallying point: **bodies and pleasures**, not sex-desire. Last sentence: "The irony of this deployment is in having us believe that our 'liberation' is in the balance" (p. 159).

### Later volumes (Macey; not yet primary)

The planned *History of Sexuality* was originally six volumes. Foucault rewrote the project before his death. *L'Usage des plaisirs* and *Le Souci de soi* (both 1984) shift from modern sexuality to Greco-Roman practices of self-governance. Volume 1's "bodies and pleasures" and its qualified *ars erotica* are 1976 gestures, not yet the 1984 *souci de soi*. See [[Concepts/Ethics of the Self and Care of the Self (Foucault)]].

## Key Claims

- Liberation discourse — speaking about sex, revealing the truth of one's sexuality — is not an exit from power; it is the form in which the deployment attaches us to the obligation to know sex.
- Sexual identity is a historical product: "the homosexual" as a person-type was produced by nineteenth-century medicine (Westphal 1870), not discovered.
- "Sex" is subordinate to sexuality: sexuality is a real historical formation that gave rise to the speculative notion of sex.
- Bio-power invests sexuality because sex is access both to the disciplined body and to the regulated population.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: Part One reports the work-imperative version of the repressive hypothesis as what makes the story easy to uphold. IV.4 abandons labor-capacity energetics. Part Five re-joins capitalism: bio-power is indispensable to inserting bodies into production and adjusting population to economic processes.
- **[[Thinkers/Ian Hacking]]**: "the homosexual was now a species" is the vault's canonical making-up-people sentence.
- **[[Thinkers/Jean-Paul Sartre]] / Freud**: Sartre vs Freud fights over lucidity vs the unconscious. Foucault's third pole is political: Freud is a relay of the deployment (p. 159), not the breaker of a millennial silence. See [[Contradictions/Sartre vs Freud - Lucidity vs the Unconscious]].
- **[[Thinkers/Pierre Hadot]]**: the late-ethics clash is about Volumes 2–3, not Volume 1. See [[Contradictions/Hadot vs Foucault - Care of the Self vs Cosmic Universalization]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Volume 1 is almost silent on feminism and on the specific technologies of power applied to women's bodies beyond "hysterization." Macey already flagged the indifference; the primary text does not repair it.
- "Bodies and pleasures" is a rallying point without a method. Whether a counterattack is possible *inside* a power that "comes from everywhere" (p. 93) is unresolved here.

> [!warning] The repressive-hypothesis demolition sits in tension with [[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]'s understanding of ideology as suppression of truth by ruling-class interests. For Foucault, power does not *hide* the truth of sexuality — it *produces* it. See [[Concepts/Repressive Hypothesis (Foucault)]].
