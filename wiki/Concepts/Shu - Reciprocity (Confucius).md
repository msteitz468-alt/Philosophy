# Shu — Reciprocity (Confucius)

> In Annping Chin's *Analects*, *shu* is **putting oneself in the other person's place** — "treating others with an awareness that they, too, are alive with humanity" (appendix; 4.15). Asked for a single word to guide a life, Confucius says "perhaps the word *shu*. Do not impose on others what you yourself do not want" (15.24). Chin links 15.24 to 4.15 and 6.30. She does **not** call it the Golden Rule.

## Load-bearing claims in the text

- **The one thread (4.15).** "The Master's way has one thread running through it." After he leaves, Zengzi says: "The Master's way consists of doing one's best to fulfill one's humanity [*zhong*] and treating others with an awareness that they, too, are alive with humanity [*shu*]." Chin: Zengzi's stress is self-cultivation; Confucius's is also government. Jiao Xun resists reading *shu* as measuring others by oneself; Shun realizes others' *unequal* natures (he points to 15.3).
- **Analogical method (6.30).** The word *shu* is not in the saying. Humaneness: "wishes to steady himself, and so he helps others to steady themselves. … The ability to make an analogy from what is close at hand is the method and the way of realizing humaneness." Zigong's wish to give all people relief is already sage-work, beyond *ren*.
- **In office (12.2).** After guest/sacrifice demeanor: "Do not impose on others what you do not desire for yourself."
- **Lifelong word (15.24).** Zigong asks if there is a single word that can serve as the guide to conduct throughout one's life. "It is perhaps the word *shu*. Do not impose on others what you yourself do not want [others to impose on you]."
- **Zhong as a taught item (7.25).** Four teachings: culture, conduct, doing one's best [*zhong*], and trustworthiness. The 4.15 pair is not restated as a pair in Books 6–10.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Vs. [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)]]**: both forbid using the other as a mere convenience. Kant grounds the prohibition in rational nature as end in itself, valid for every rational being. *Shu* is analogical — from what is close at hand (6.30) — and is practiced inside roles already named by *li* (guest, sacrifice, father, ruler). It is not derived from autonomy.
- **Vs. [[Concepts/Categorical Imperative - Universal Law Formulation (Kant)]]**: "do not impose what you do not want" is a negative reciprocity formula, not a test of whether a maxim can be willed as universal law. A person could consistently not want X and still fail Kant's test, or pass Kant's test while failing *shu*'s concrete analogy.
- **Vs. [[Concepts/Duty (Kant)]]**: *shu* does not require the motive of respect for law. 4.2's humane person "feels at home" in humaneness; the wise person may practice it because he sees benefits. *Shu* is the method of the first.
- **Vs. Christian "do unto others"**: Chin does not make the equation. 15.24 is negative ("do not impose"), 6.30 is positive (help others steady themselves as you steady yourself). Do not collapse them into one imported slogan.
- **Vs. Laozi ch. 49**: the sage "regard[s] the not-good as good" and has "no intentions of his own." *Shu* still distinguishes: do not impose what *you* do not want — a self remains the analogical base.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Jiao Xun vs the *zhong*/*shu* consensus: if *shu* is not measuring others by oneself, 15.24's wording still looks like that measure. Chin records both.
- Liu/Qian deny that 15.3 is the same "single thread" as 4.15. Do not fuse every "one" saying.
- Do not title this page "Golden Rule." That name is reception, not Chin.
