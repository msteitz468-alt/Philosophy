# Myth of Jones (Sellars)

> §§45–63 of [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|EPM]]: a piece of "anthropological science fiction" in which thoughts and sense impressions are introduced as **theoretical postulates** of a public explanatory framework, and only later acquire a reporting use. "I have used a myth to kill a myth — the Myth of the Given."

## The Problem It Solves

Having destroyed the [[Concepts/Myth of the Given (Sellars)|Given]], Sellars owes an account of what inner episodes *are*. The pressure is real: at the time he wrote, Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949) and Wittgenstein's *Investigations* (1953) had made opposition to "a private language" and to "entities capable of being known by only one person" seem inseparable from Ryle's polemic against "the ghost in the machine." Sellars wants to show, as Rorty puts it, "why we can be naturalists without being behaviorists, why we can accept Wittgenstein's doubts about … 'self-authenticating non-verbal episodes' without sharing Ryle's doubts about the existence of such mental entities as thoughts and sense-impressions."

Sellars is explicit that he is defending a *revised* classical view (§47): "each of us belongs a stream of episodes, not themselves immediate experiences, to which we have privileged, but by no means either invariable or infallible, access." These episodes "can occur without being 'expressed' by overt verbal behavior, though verbal behavior is — in an important sense — their natural fruition." He denies that "privileged access" must be modelled perceptually: it is "a mistake to suppose that we must be having verbal imagery — indeed, any imagery — when we 'know what we are thinking.'"

## Step 1: Our Rylean Ancestors (§§48–49)

Imagine "a stage in pre-history in which humans are limited to what I shall call a **Rylean language**, a language of which the fundamental descriptive vocabulary speaks of public properties of public objects located in Space and enduring through Time." It is not impoverished: it has conjunction, disjunction, negation, quantification, "especially the subjunctive conditional," plus the vagueness and open texture of ordinary discourse.

The question: "What resources would have to be added to the Rylean language of these talking animals in order that they might come to recognize each other and themselves as animals that *think, observe*, and have *feelings* and *sensations*, as we use these terms? And how could the addition of these resources be construed as *reasonable*?"

**First addition: semantical discourse** — the resources for saying "'*Rot*' means red." Given [[Concepts/Psychological Nominalism (Sellars)|the logic of 'means']], semantical statements are not relational and not shorthand for statements about causes and effects of verbal performances. With them, the ancestors can already say a great deal, because "characteristic of thoughts is their *intentionality*, reference, or aboutness, and it is clear that semantical talk about the meaning or reference of verbal expressions has the same structure as mentalistic discourse concerning what thoughts are about."

## Step 2: Theories, Models, and Commentaries (§§51–55)

Sellars pauses to get the philosophy of science right, because everything turns on it.

- A theory is not built by "constructing uninterpreted calculi" and correlating them with observation. It is built by means of a **model** — "a domain of familiar objects behaving in familiar ways" — and, crucially, **a commentary on the model** "which places more or less sharply drawn restrictions on the analogy between the model and the theoretical entities." The commentary is what keeps the model from being taken literally.
- "Science is continuous with common sense, and the ways in which the scientist seeks to explain empirical phenomena are refinements of the ways in which plain men, however crudely and schematically, have attempted to understand their environment and their fellow men since the dawn of intelligence."
- Methodological behaviorism is a thesis about *concept formation*, not about the analysis of common-sense mentalistic concepts. It "does not deny that there is such a thing as introspection" nor that it is often reliable; the essential point is "that we introspect in terms of common-sense mentalistic concepts," and the behaviorist proposes to construct his own concepts from scratch. Sellars judges the austere version "unduly restrictive" — physics is not built from concepts explicitly definable in observation terms, and neither is chemistry.

He also warns against the reading he does *not* want: the theoretical episodes are "in" language-using animals "as molecular impacts are 'in' gases, not as 'ghosts' are in 'machines.'" They are "non-empirical" only "in the simple sense that they are theoretical — not definable in observational terms."

## Step 3: Jones's Theory of Thoughts (§§56–59)

Enter **Jones**, "a genius," who develops a theory according to which overt utterances are the culmination of a process that begins with inner episodes — modelled on *speech itself*. "Thus, even when a hungry person proceeds to eat, the true cause of the behavior is not simply the hunger."

Two features:
1. **The model is overt speech**, so the theory carries over to inner episodes the applicability of *semantical categories*: as one says of an overt utterance that it means this or that, so one now says of a thought that it is *of* this or that.
2. **A commentary** restricts the analogy — in particular, thoughts are not to be confused with **verbal imagery**. (Jones does not yet even have the concept of an image.) The theory is perfectly compatible with the idea "that the ability to have thoughts is acquired in the process of acquiring overt speech and that only after overt speech is well established can 'inner speech' occur without its overt culmination."

**The dénouement (§59).** Jones teaches his compatriots to use the theory to interpret one another's behavior. Then:

> It is but a short step to the use of this language in *self-description*. Thus, when Tom, watching Dick, has behavioral evidence which warrants the use of the sentence (in the language of the theory) "Dick is thinking '*p*'" … Dick, using the same behavioral evidence, can say, in the language of the theory, "I am thinking '*p*'." And it now turns out — need it have? — that Dick can be trained to give reasonably reliable self-descriptions, using the language of the theory, without having to observe his overt behavior. Jones brings this about, roughly, by applauding utterances of "I am thinking that *p*" by Dick when the behavioral evidence strongly supports the theoretical statement "Dick is thinking that *p*"; and by frowning on utterances of "I am thinking that *p*" when the evidence does not support this theoretical statement. Our ancestors begin to speak of the privileged access each of us has to his own thoughts. **What began as a language with a purely theoretical use has gained a reporting role.**

That last sentence is the whole trick. A reporting, non-inferential use is *earned within* a public framework rather than presupposed as its foundation.

The morals Sellars draws:
- Concepts of thoughts are "primarily and essentially **intersubjective**, as intersubjective as the concept of a positron," and the reporting role — privileged access — "constitutes a dimension of the use of these concepts which is **built on and presupposes** this intersubjective status."
- Privacy is not *absolute* privacy: the concepts have a reporting use in which one draws no inferences from behavioral evidence, yet "the fact that overt behavior *is* evidence for these episodes *is built into the very logic of these concepts*, just as the fact that the observable behavior of gases is evidence for molecular episodes is built into the very logic of molecule talk."

## Step 4: Jones's Theory of Impressions (§§60–62)

The same construction is run again for **sense impressions**, the entities the [[Concepts/The Logic of Looks (Sellars)|logic of looks]] left owing an account. Jones postulates "a class of inner — theoretical — episodes … which are the end results of the impingement of physical objects and processes on various parts of the body."

The commentary does heavy work here:
- **Impressions are states of the perceiver, not particulars.** The model is the occurrence "in" perceivers of *replicas* — "inner replicas which, when brought about in standard conditions, share the perceptible characteristics of their physical source." But "by misunderstanding the role of models … it mistakenly assumes that if the entities of the model are particulars, the theoretical entities which are introduced by means of the model must themselves be particulars." They are not. This is exactly where the sense-datum theorist went wrong.
- **Impressions are not red.** "It would be a mistake to suppose that since the model for the impression of a red triangle is a red and triangular wafer, the impression itself is a red and triangular wafer." What can be said is that it is *analogous* to one. "**The essential feature of the analogy is that visual impressions stand to one another in a system of ways of resembling and differing which is structurally similar to the ways in which the colors and shapes of visible objects resemble and differ.**"
- **But not "purely formal" either.** One might conclude that the concept is a bare logical form needing "content" from ostensive definition — that immediate experience is intersubjectively "purely structural" and its content incommunicable. "Yet this line of thought is but another expression of the Myth of the Given." Theoretical concepts are meaningful in the ordinary way; "the contrast between a theoretical language and the language of observable fact has nothing to do with the epistemological contrast between 'ostensive definition' and…"
- Nor are impressions *thoughts*: it would be an error to give "the language of impressions the logic of the language of thoughts," since seeing is a cognitive episode.

Finally the same reporting transition occurs: Jones "succeeds in training them to make a reporting use of this language" — to say "I have the impression of a red triangle" when and only when they indeed have one. And again privacy is not absolute.

## The Diagnosis of the Sense-Datum Theorist (§62)

Sellars can now say exactly where his opponent went wrong. "The spirit of Jones is not yet dead": the particulars of the micro-theory "are the solid core of the sense contents and sense fields of the sense-datum theorist." The sense-datum philosopher "has taught himself to notice impressions" and then mistakes this for "a 'discovery' that there are such things" — dislocating the truth of these conceptions and treating the impressions as antecedent objects of knowledge that "have somehow been in the framework from the beginning." He speaks of "the given" *in the very act of taking*.

The language of impressions, Sellars insists, "was no more tailored to fit antecedent noticings of these entities than the language of molecules was tailored to fit antecedent noticings of molecules."

## The Closing Passage (§63)

> I have used a myth to kill a myth — the Myth of the Given. But is my myth really a myth? Or does the reader not recognize Jones as Man himself in the middle of his journey from the grunts and groans of the cave to the subtle and polydimensional discourse of the drawing room, the laboratory, and the study, the language of Henry and William James, of Einstein and of the philosophers who, in their efforts to break out of discourse to an *arché* beyond discourse, have provided the most curious dimension of all?

Rorty links this to Hegel's *Phenomenology* — "the transition from sense-perception to consciousness to self-consciousness — and, more generally, from Nature to Spirit" — and reads the closing clause as "a rebuke to all those philosophers, from Plato to Ayer, who hoped to 'break out of discourse'… though there is no such *arché*, we are none the worse for that."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Against [[Thinkers/Gilbert Ryle|Ryle]] and philosophical behaviorism: Sellars keeps genuine inner episodes with genuine privileged access, so the ghost is not exorcised, only re-founded. Against [[Thinkers/Descartes]] and the classical view: those episodes are *not* self-intimating, not incorrigible, and their concepts are logically parasitic on public discourse — so the Cartesian starting point is inverted. Sellars occupies a position both sides deny is available.
- > [!warning] Bears directly on [[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]] and the vault's qualia cluster. Sellars is the ancestor of the attack on incorrigible immediate experience, but unlike Dennett he *retains* impressions as real theoretical posits with a genuine reporting use. Where Dennett dissolves the explanandum, Sellars relocates it. Compare also [[Concepts/Epiphenomenal Qualia (Jackson)]]: Jackson's Mary learns something on release; on Sellars's account, what she acquires is a place in a framework, not acquaintance with a Given.
- > [!warning] Live tension with [[Concepts/Sense-Data vs. Physical Objects (Russell)]]: Sellars ends by *conceding* that there may be something right in the sense-datum theorist's particulars — but relocated into the micro-theory of the [[Concepts/Scientia Mensura (Sellars)|scientific image]], not found by analysis of ordinary discourse.
- Open: whether the "reporting role gained by a theoretical language" really escapes the Given, or merely defers the question of what makes a non-inferential report *authoritative*. This is the question [[Thinkers/John McDowell]] and [[Thinkers/Robert Brandom]] take up in opposite directions.
