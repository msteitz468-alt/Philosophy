# Existential Humanism (Sartre)
> Not the cult of Man as a finished supreme value, but the claim that man, abandoned, is his own legislator and exists only by surpassing himself toward an aim.

## Summary

The title of the 1945/46 lecture is itself the thesis under defence. Objectors had quoted *La Nausée* against him: he had ridiculed the humanists, so how can existentialism be a humanism? Sartre splits the word.

**Humanism as cult of Man.** "A theory which upholds man as the end-in-itself and as the supreme value." Cocteau's character, flying over mountains, declares "Man is magnificent!" — ascribing to himself, as a man, the inventions of some men. "Only the dog or the horse would be in a position to pronounce a general judgment upon man and declare that he is magnificent, which they have never been such fools as to do." A man has no right to pronounce judgment upon Man. "An existentialist will never take man as the end, since man is still to be determined." The cult of humanity "after the manner of Auguste Comte" is "shut-in upon itself, and — this must be said — in Fascism. We do not want a humanism like that."

**Humanism as self-surpassing.** "Man is all the time outside of himself: it is in projecting and losing himself beyond himself that he makes man to exist; and, on the other hand, it is by pursuing transcendent aims that he himself is able to exist." Transcendence here is not God but self-surpassing. There is "no other universe except the human universe, the universe of human subjectivity." The relation of that transcendence to subjectivity — man not shut up in himself but forever present in a human universe — "is this that we call existential humanism." It is a humanism "because we remind man that there is no legislator but himself; that he himself, thus abandoned, must decide for himself; also because we show that it is not by turning back upon himself, but always by seeking, beyond himself, an aim which is one of liberation or of some particular realisation, that man can realize himself as truly human."

The closing sentences of the lecture fix the sense: existentialism "is nothing else but an attempt to draw the full conclusions from a consistently atheistic position." It is not a demonstration that God does not exist. "Even if God existed that would make no difference." "What man needs is to find himself again and to understand that nothing can save him from himself, not even a valid proof of the existence of God." In that sense it is optimistic: a doctrine of action.

This is also how the lecture answers the charge that invented values cannot be serious. "If I have excluded God the Father, there must be somebody to invent values." "There is no sense in life *a priori*. Life is nothing until it is lived; but it is yours to make sense of, and the value of it is nothing else but the sense that you choose." From that invention "there is a possibility of creating a human community."

## Key Claims / Positions

- Two humanisms: cult of Man (Comte, Cocteau → Fascism) vs self-surpassing (existential).
- Man is not an end, because man is still to be determined.
- Transcendence = self-surpassing, not a God above.
- No legislator but man; values invented; no *a priori* sense of life.
- Even a proof of God would not save man from himself.

### How This Compares to Other Thinkers in the Wiki

- **vs Welten's Sartre**: [[Sources/Sartre - Ruud Welten (2020)]] calls Sartre an *anti-humanist* who refuses any base set of human values ("the human is the future of the human"). The lecture keeps the refusal of a *given* humanity and *claims* the name humanism for that refusal. Welten reported the paradox; the lecture is the distinction that makes it coherent.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Kant]]**: Kant's Formula of Humanity treats rational nature as an end in itself with absolute worth. Sartre agrees that freedom wills itself and others' freedom, and refuses to take *finished* Man as an end. He denies that the formal/universal suffices to decide action. See [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)]].
- **vs [[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: the community to be created is not guaranteed by a human essence or by the future of the proletariat; it is invented in commitments (and can fail — Fascism as "the truth of man").
- **vs [[Thinkers/Michel Foucault]]**: the later "specific intellectual" is defined against Sartre as *universal* intellectual speaking for humanity. The lecture's humanism of a still-undetermined man is exactly the universalist stance Foucault refuses. See [[Concepts/Specific Intellectual (Foucault)]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Whether "willing freedom for others" plus "no *a priori* values" can ground condemnation of cowards, scum, and Fascism without smuggling back a human essence is the standing ethical problem of the lecture — the same problem Welten posed from BN.
- Heidegger's *Letter on Humanism* (1947) is the nearby refusal of this entire construction; not yet a vault source.
