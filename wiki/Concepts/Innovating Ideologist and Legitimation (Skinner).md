# Innovating Ideologist and Legitimation (Skinner)

> Chapters 7–8 of [[Sources/Visions of Politics Volume 1 - Regarding Method - Quentin Skinner (2002)|*Regarding Method*]] — where the method stops being merely interpretive and becomes **causal**. Even an agent who never believes his professed principles is constrained by them, because "any course of action will be inhibited to the degree that it cannot be legitimised."

## The Problem: Are Professed Principles Idle?

Namier — and, as Skinner notes with relish, the Marxist historians Namier despised — held that professed ideals are *ex post facto* rationalisations giving conduct "a spurious air of morality and rationality." On this view "party names and cant" are epiphenomena, causally idle.

Butterfield-style critics replied that the agents were "sincerely attached to the ideals." Skinner's move is to refuse the shared premise both sides accept: **that principles matter only if they are motives**. He therefore takes the hardest possible case — an agent who never believes a word of his own professed principles — and argues that the principles still constrain what he can do.

## The Mechanism

The key situation is an agent doing something "in some way questionable, and at the same time possess[ing] a strong motive for attempting (in Weberian phrase) to legitimise it." Skinner's model case is Weber's early-modern entrepreneurs, condemned as usurious "city cormorants," who needed their conduct described in favourable terms.

The **innovating ideologist** faces a hard constraint: he cannot simply invent a favourable vocabulary, because it would persuade no one. He must show that *existing* commendatory terms somehow apply to what he is doing.

> All revolutionaries are to this extent obliged to march backwards into battle.

His instruments are **evaluative-descriptive terms** — words that simultaneously describe and appraise (the emotivists' insight, corrected by Urmson via Austin's illocution/perlocution distinction). Two strategies:

1. **Manipulate the speech-act potential of terms** — coin new favourable terms (*frugality*); metaphorically extend neutral ones (*discerning*, *penetrating*); neutralise unfavourable ones (*ambition*); reverse them (*shrewd*); and the mirror-image tactics for denigration (*spendthrift*, *exorbitant*, *obsequious*, *condescending*).
2. **Manipulate the criteria for applying existing commendatory terms** — simpler and, Skinner says, far more important. His evidence is the early-modern reworking of *providence* / *provident* and *religious*, traced in Wheeler, Mun, Roberts and Alexander.

The sign that ideological work has been done is that meanings become "stretched and confused" — and, in the emblematic case, *laborare et orare* ("work and pray") gives way to *laborare est orare* ("to work is to pray").

## The Conclusion

Even where principles are not motives:

> they will find themselves committed to behaving in such a way that their actions remain compatible with the claim that their professed principles genuinely motivated them.

Hence principles are **enabling conditions** and **constraints** rather than causes in the motive sense:

> Any principle that helps to legitimise a course of action will therefore be among the enabling conditions of its occurrence… they can only hope to legitimise, and hence to perform, a correspondingly restricted range of actions. To study the principles they invoke will thus be to study one of the key determinants of their behaviour.

This is the answer to the charge that intellectual history is ornamental. Political vocabulary is not a reflection of practice but, in the phrase from chapter 9, "one of the engines of social change."

Skinner defends Weber against Trevor-Roper on this basis: the claim is that Protestantism **legitimised** rather than caused capitalism, and Skinner's own case *assumes* — rather than denies — that capitalism predated the Reformation. He concedes Tawney is more vulnerable. His closing note on the ideologists' success is characteristically dry: the outcome came "greatly to the horror of the religious, who saw themselves as the victims of a trick… If it was a trick, however, it certainly worked."

## The Groundwork: 'Social Meaning' (ch. 7)

Chapter 7 clears the way by attacking four positions at once — that decoding meaning just *is* motive-explanation; that meaning-explanation is incompatible with causal explanation; that redescription cannot explain anything; and that meaning is no bar to full causal explanation. Skinner argues all four are mistaken.

His positive account: decoding an action's meaning is identifying the **illocutionary act** performed (see [[Concepts/Illocutionary Force and Historical Meaning (Skinner)]]), tied via Grice's non-natural meaning to the agent's *primary* intention. This extends beyond speech to action itself — Yoruba cowrie boxes as protecting souls; Machiavelli's *Il Principe* ch. 15 as repudiating a moral commonplace.

Against the claim that redescription explains nothing: "what an illocutionary redescription will characteristically explain about a social action will be its **point**," and removing puzzlement just is explaining. Against the claim that this is covert causal explanation: it "is to focus on a feature of the policeman's action, not on an independently specifiable condition" — so it is non-causal, and therefore one *stage* of an account that can have causal stages later (motives; the grounds of motives). He locates himself in a "middle ground," close to Weber.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Direct bearing on [[Thinkers/Karl Marx]] and the vault's ideology material. Skinner grants the Marxist point that professed principles often are not motives, then denies the inference that they are therefore causally idle. Ideology constrains from the outside even when insincere — a conclusion available to neither the Namierite nor the vulgar-Marxist.
- > [!warning] Convergence with [[Concepts/Power-Knowledge (Foucault)]]: both make vocabulary constitutive of the field of possible action rather than a description of it. Skinner quotes Foucault approvingly that "the history which bears and determines us has the form of a war." The difference remains the agent: Skinner's ideologist *chooses* his redescriptions.
- > [!warning] Bears on [[Concepts/Negative and Positive Liberty (Berlin)]]. Skinner's whole apparatus implies that "liberty" has no stable meaning to be partitioned into two concepts — only a history of contested uses. The vault holds Berlin's distinction from [[Sources/Isaiah Berlin and the Politics of Freedom - Baum and Nichols (2013)]]; Skinner's later *Liberty before Liberalism* is the direct engagement and is **not** in this volume, so no vault page should attribute a developed critique of Berlin to this source.
