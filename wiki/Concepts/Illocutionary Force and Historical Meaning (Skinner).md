# Illocutionary Force and Historical Meaning (Skinner)

> Chapters 5–6 of [[Sources/Visions of Politics Volume 1 - Regarding Method - Quentin Skinner (2002)|*Regarding Method*]]: the machinery behind "seeing things their way." "Anyone issuing a serious utterance will always be doing something as well as saying something, **and doing it in virtue of saying what is said**."

## Three Senses of "Meaning"

Skinner's first move is to prise apart three questions that anti-intentionalist debate had run together:

- **meaning₁** — what the words and sentences of a text mean;
- **meaning₂** — what the text means *to me*, the reader;
- **meaning₃** — what a writer meant by what he or she said.

Sorting the literature by this grid dissolves much of the quarrel: Wimsatt and Beardsley's intentional fallacy targets mainly meaning₁; Iser, Fish and Ricoeur are about meaning₂ ("the reader's response is not *to* the meaning; it *is* the meaning" — Fish); Derrida's critique of logocentrism targets meaning₁, though his umbrella fragment targets meaning₃.

## Motives vs. Intentions

The distinction that does the historical work:

> **Motives** are antecedent to and contingently connected with a work — genuinely "outside" it, and hence irrelevant to meaning in all three senses. **Intentions *in* writing** are a feature of the work itself.

And within intentions, a further cut borrowed from Anscombe and Kenny: an intention **to do** *x* (a plan or design, a contingent antecedent, like a motive) versus an intention **in** *x*-ing (which characterises the work as embodying a particular aim or point).

Against the claim that intentions are unrecoverable private states, Skinner's reply is Wittgensteinian: in any *successful* act of communication the intentions are *ex hypothesi* publicly legible. Recovering them is grasping a convention, not peering inside a head. He quotes Geertz: ideas are "envehicled meanings"; they "are not, and have not been for some time, unobservable mental stuff."

## Austin's Apparatus

From *How to Do Things with Words*: every serious utterance has both a dimension of **meaning** (sense and reference) and a dimension of **force**.

- **Illocutionary** — what we do *in* saying something (warning, promising, ridiculing, repudiating).
- **Perlocutionary** — what we bring about *by* saying it (inducing sadness, causing alarm).
- **Uptake** — grasping the illocutionary force "will be equivalent to understanding what the speaker was *doing* in issuing it."

Skinner's worked example, adapted from Strawson, is a policeman saying "The ice over there is very thin": the meaning is transparent; the act is a warning.

The historical payoff is that **perlocutionary** intentions need no separate study, while **illocutionary** ones do. And the central thesis: illocutionary intentions are irrelevant to meaning₁ and meaning₂, but **equivalent to meaning₃**.

He corrects Austin on two points. Austin wavered toward individuating illocutionary acts by *conventions*; Skinner individuates them by the agent's **intentions**, expanding Austin via Strawson, Searle, Schiffer and Holdcroft. And he denies the existence of "unintentional illocutionary acts": one may warn someone unintentionally, but then no illocutionary act of warning has been performed. Force is "a resource of language"; acts are identified by intentions — so utterances routinely carry force their authors did not intend.

## What Skinner Is *Not* Claiming

An important guard, frequently missed by his critics:

> Skinner is **not** an intentionalist about textual meaning.

He rejects Hirsch and Juhl explicitly, largely endorses the anti-intentionalist case about meaning₁, and accepts Ricoeur's **surplus meaning**: "Paul Ricoeur has spoken in this connection of surplus meaning, and with this formulation I am in complete agreement." A work may carry meanings its author could not have intended. Authors' own statements of intention are privileged but not final — self-deception and incompetence are "failures to which all flesh is perpetually heir."

The two questions — what the text means, and what its author meant — are "separable but not separate." Both must be answered.

**Irony** is the clean test case. In Defoe's *The Shortest-Way with the Dissenters* the meaning is perfectly clear; what is hard is the *act* — ridiculing intolerance. No amount of reading the text "over and over" recovers it.

## The Method

Elucidate meaning, then reconstruct the **argumentative context**, in order to read off the nature of the **intervention**. Texts are to be seen as arguments and moves, not strings of propositions: "to argue is always to argue for or against a certain assumption or point of view." This generalises Collingwood's logic of question and answer — though Skinner rejects Collingwood's talk of "thinking their thoughts after them."

Context need not be immediate: with Pocock, attention shifts to the "languages" of debate. But the author cannot be dispensed with, since language is a resource as well as a constraint. Skinner calls "the death of the author" **exaggerated**.

## Two Objections Answered

- **Hollis and Tully: "the cap fits, but was the author wearing it?"** Skinner's reply is that the gap is closed the way any historical gap is — by hypothesis-testing against the author's other beliefs, against motives (which *corroborate* intention-ascriptions even though they do not constitute meaning), and against the coherence of the whole network of attitudes.
- **Derrida: there is no infallible route to intention.** Conceded — and conceded too that the umbrella fragment lacks context. But: "Haunted as Derrida appears to be by the ghost of Descartes, he has concentrated on attacking a position that no theorist of intentionality need defend." All ascriptions of intention are defeasible inferences from the best available evidence, and were never advertised as anything else.

## Why It Matters

Recovering the historical identity of texts denaturalises our own beliefs. Skinner insists this is not relativism but a means of testing and strengthening them — closing chapter 6 with the line that "to denounce such studies is not a defence of reason but an assault on the open society itself."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Sharpens the vault's treatment of [[Concepts/Language-Games (Wittgenstein)]] and [[Concepts/Intentionality (Searle)]] by putting speech-act theory to *historical* work. Note that Skinner's use diverges from [[Thinkers/John Searle]]'s: Searle systematises illocutionary types; Skinner wants only the identification of what a particular author was doing on a particular occasion.
- > [!warning] Tension with [[Thinkers/Michel Foucault]] and [[Concepts/Archaeology of Knowledge (Foucault)]]. Both historicise discourse and refuse timeless meanings, and Skinner quotes Foucault approvingly on classification and on history as war. But archaeology brackets the author in favour of anonymous discursive rules, where Skinner's entire method turns on recovering an agent's intention. This is the live methodological fork in the vault's history-of-ideas material.
- The unresolved question critics keep pressing: if illocutionary force must be inferred from conventions, and conventions are reconstructed from surviving texts, how much circularity is tolerable? Skinner's answer — defeasible inference, no Cartesian guarantee — is a stance, not a proof.
