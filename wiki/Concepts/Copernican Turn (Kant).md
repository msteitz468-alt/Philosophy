# Copernican Turn (Kant)

> Assume not that cognition must conform to objects, but that objects must conform to our cognition — so that we can cognize of things a priori only what we ourselves have put into them.

## Summary

In the B Preface Kant names the failed assumption of all previous metaphysics: "Up to now it has been assumed that all our cognition must conform to the objects" (B xvi). On that assumption, a priori determination of objects is hopeless. The experiment is to reverse the relation, "just like the first thoughts of Copernicus, who… tried to see if he might not have greater success if he made the observer revolve and left the stars at rest" (B xvi). Objects of experience, and the experience in which alone they are given, must conform to our intuition and concepts. Then we can know a priori the forms *we* contribute: space and time as forms of sensibility, the categories as concepts of an object in general.

The same turn is a limit. The experiment succeeds for metaphysics' first part — a priori concepts to which objects can be given in experience — but shows we never get beyond possible experience. Such cognition reaches [[Concepts/Phenomena and Noumena (Kant)|appearances]] only; the thing in itself remains "actual for itself but uncognized by us" (B xx). The unconditioned that reason demands is not in things as given to us. Speculative reason therefore leaves the supersensible empty for practical data to fill: "I had to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith" (B xxx).

This is a revolution in the *way of thinking*, modeled on how mathematics and experimental physics found the "secure course of a science." Reason "has insight only into what it itself produces according to its own design" and must approach nature "like an appointed judge who compels witnesses to answer the questions he puts to them" (B xiii). The critique is "a treatise on the method, not a system of the science itself" (B xxii).

Korsgaard later applies the same turn to morals in the *Groundwork*: we do not *find* the rational order, we *impose* it through action. See [[Sources/Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals - Immanuel Kant (Gregor, Cambridge 1998)]].

## How Kant Uses This

- **Vs. Locke / Hume.** Not a "physiology of the human understanding" (A ix) that traces ideas to impressions. The forms are contributed by the mind, but they are conditions of objects of experience, not psychological habits. Hume came closest (B 19) but stopped at causality and inferred metaphysics is a delusion of habit.
- **Vs. Descartes / Leibniz.** Not innate *contents* implanted by God. Kant agrees with Locke that we have no innate knowledge of particular propositions; what is a priori is the *form* of receptivity and of thought.
- **Vs. Hegel.** The turn *establishes* the limit Hegel will refuse: appearances vs things in themselves. See [[Contradictions/Hegelian Monism vs. Kantian Dualism]].
- **Vs. the Groundwork's two standpoints.** The B Preface already states the same will can be naturally necessitated as appearance and free as thing in itself (B xxvii–xxviii). The moral philosophy presupposes this theoretical result.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] This is the historical synthesis of [[Contradictions/Rationalism vs Empiricism on Innate Ideas]] — forms from the mind, content from the senses. Whether it *succeeds* is the rest of the *Critique*.
- > [!warning] Hegel reads the residual thing-in-itself as a self-contradiction: to mark a limit is already to be beyond it. See [[Contradictions/Hegelian Monism vs. Kantian Dualism]].
