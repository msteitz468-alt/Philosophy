# Perfect and Imperfect Duties (Kant)

> "Some actions are so constituted that their maxim cannot even be *thought* without contradiction as a universal law of nature… In the case of others that inner impossibility is indeed not to be found, but it is still impossible to *will* that their maxim be raised to the universality of a law of nature because such a will would contradict itself." (4:424)

## Summary

In §II of the [[Sources/Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals - Immanuel Kant (Gregor, Cambridge 1998)|*Groundwork*]] Kant illustrates the [[Concepts/Categorical Imperative - Universal Law Formulation (Kant)|categorical imperative]] with four examples, provisionally arranged as duties **to oneself / to others** × **perfect / imperfect**. He warns that the official division is reserved for the later *Metaphysics of Morals* (4:421n). Perfect duty "admits no exception in favor of inclination" (4:421n); imperfect duty is "wide (meritorious)" (4:424).

The test is not "what if everyone did this?" as a prediction of bad consequences. It is whether one can *will* the maxim as a universal law of nature.

| | To oneself | To others |
|---|---|---|
| **Perfect** (contradiction in *conception*; strict / unremitting) | Suicide from self-love | False promise to borrow |
| **Imperfect** (contradiction in the *will*; wide / meritorious) | Neglect of talents | Non-beneficence |

1. **Suicide** (4:422). A maxim of self-love that destroys the life it is "destined to further" cannot be thought as a law of nature — the nature would "contradict itself."
2. **False promise / borrowing** (4:422). If the maxim of promising with no intention to keep were a law of nature, "the promise and the end one might have in it itself" would become impossible. Same structure as §I's lying-promise (4:403).
3. **Neglected talents** (4:423). A nature of rusting capacities "could indeed always subsist," but a rational being "cannot possibly *will*" that his own powers, given for possible purposes, go unused.
4. **Non-beneficence** (4:423). A race of mutually indifferent helpers "could admittedly very well subsist," but the agent "would rob himself of all hope of the assistance he wishes for himself."

The first pair cannot even be *conceived* as universal laws; the second can be conceived but cannot be *willed*. Transgression of either is typically not willing the maxim *as law* but making an *exception* for one's own inclination — degrading *universalitas* to *generalitas* (4:424).

The same four are recast under the [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)|Formula of Humanity]] (4:429–430): suicide uses one's person "merely as a means to maintain a tolerable condition"; a lying promise treats the other as a means who "cannot possibly agree"; talents and beneficence are not satisfied by mere non-conflict with humanity — one must *harmonize* with it and make others' ends one's own as far as possible.

Korsgaard: the four examples are *illustrations* of two failure-modes, not a complete casuistry. She notes a later tension with the 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*, where suicide is treated as a perfect duty that is nonetheless "wide" in application.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]**: the 2×2 is a ladder, not the system. The *Groundwork* only needs enough cases to show that FUL/FLN and FH generate both strict prohibitions and positive required ends.
- **[[Thinkers/John Stuart Mill]]**: no act-type is unconditionally out. A lie, a broken promise, even a sacrifice of one for many, can be required if utility says so. Kant's perfect duties are the refusal of that flexibility. See [[Contradictions/Utilitarian Consequentialism vs. Kantian Deontology]].
- **[[Thinkers/Aristotle]]**: particular virtues are means relative to us, found by [[Concepts/Practical Wisdom - Phronesis (Aristotle)|*phronēsis*]] in circumstances. Kant's perfect duties are not means and do not admit exception "in favor of inclination."
- **[[Thinkers/Hume]]**: promise-keeping is an artificial virtue, the child of convention and interest. For Kant the lying-promise maxim *destroys itself* as a law; the institution is not the ground of the duty.
- **Marcia Baron** (Wood/Yale essays): "from duty" is compatible with inclination; the suicide and grief examples isolate the moral motive, they do not require gritted teeth. Same point Korsgaard makes against the reluctant-duty myth.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The *Groundwork* treats lying as a perfect duty with no exception for inclination. Kant's 1797 essay "On a Supposed Right to Lie" pushes this to the notorious rigorist extreme. That essay is not in the vault; the contradiction page currently states the rigorist reading as Kant's position.
- Contradiction-in-conception vs contradiction-in-the-will is itself disputed (logical / teleological / practical readings — Korsgaard lists all three). The "practical contradiction" reading is her preferred one.
- Official duty-tables belong to the 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*, still not ingested.
