# Conscious Access (Dehaene)

> The tractable core of consciousness: some attended information enters awareness and becomes reportable. Vigilance, attention, self, and "phenomenal awareness" are neighboring phenomena, not the thing to start with.

## Summary

*Consciousness and the Brain* opens by carving the folk word. Everyday *consciousness* "is loaded with fuzzy meanings." Dehaene distinguishes at least three:

1. **Vigilance** (intransitive consciousness): the sleep–wake / anesthesia dimension. Necessary but not sufficient — vegetative patients still cycle through sleep and wake.
2. **Selective attention**: the unconscious sieve that isolates one object by salience or goals. Often a *gateway* to access, but dissociable — attention can be deployed to invisible cues (spatial cueing, N2pc to masked targets).
3. **Conscious access**: "the simple fact that usually, whenever we are awake, whatever we decide to focus on may become conscious" — reportable to others, available to memory, intention, and planning.

What counts as genuine consciousness, he argues, is access. William James's "taking possession by the mind" is almost right, but "conflates two different notions with distinct brain mechanisms: *selection* and *access.*" Damasio's "self in the act of knowing" is rejected as a *starting* definition: self-consciousness is just access to representations of "me," not a prerequisite for seeing a color. Gallup's mirror test is not a litmus test.

Access is **vast in potential, one-chunk in actuality**. Items can sit **preconscious** — "accessible but not accessed" — until they win the competition. The same stimulus, at threshold, is seen on half the trials; the brain, not the world, does the switching. Crossing the threshold is late (~⅓–½ second), all-or-none, and indexed by the [[Concepts/Signatures of Consciousness and Ignition (Dehaene)|signatures]].

The introduction already refuses a leftover **phenomenal consciousness** distinct from access (Block, later named in Ch. 7): the notion "is highly misleading and leads down a slippery slope to dualism. We should start simple and first study conscious access. Once we clarify how any piece of sensory information can gain access to our mind and become reportable, then the insurmountable problem of our ineffable experiences will disappear." That is the book's wager against [[Concepts/Hard Problem of Consciousness (Chalmers)]].

## Key Claims / Positions

- Access, not vigilance or attention, is the experimental variable.
- Preconscious ≠ subliminal: the first is strong but unattended; the second is too weak to ignite (taxonomy completed in Ch. 5).
- Attention often gates access but can operate unconsciously.
- Self and metacognition are higher-order forms of the same access operation.
- Report is the *definiens*, not a theory of mechanism. "Subjective reports are the key phenomena that a cognitive neuroscience of consciousness purports to study" (with Naccache, 2001).

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Bernard Baars]]** (not yet a vault thinker page): psychological global workspace already treated access as broadcasting; Dehaene supplies the neuronal implementation.
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: access is the *psychological* concept of mind — awareness, the easy problem. Phenomenal consciousness is what remains. Dehaene denies the remainder. See [[Concepts/Phenomenal and Psychological Concepts of Mind (Chalmers)]] and [[Contradictions/Dehaene vs Chalmers - Access Consciousness vs the Hard Problem]].
- **[[Thinkers/Ned Block]]**: coined access vs phenomenal. Dehaene's Ch. 7: Block "concedes that the workspace machinery may explain conscious access" and still wants leftover qualia.
- **[[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]]**: "fame in the brain" is Dehaene's gloss on access; heterophenomenology is the methodological cousin (reports as data).
- **[[Thinkers/Jakob Hohwy]]**: conscious perception = winning first-order inference; ignition reinterpreted as the switch to active inference. Access is accepted, then given a PEM job.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] If access is *defined* as reportability, the method cannot detect a phenomenal overflow that is unreportable by construction — Block's and Chalmers's objection. Dehaene treats that leftover as a dualist temptation, not a datum.
