# Sovereign Power and the Spectacle of the Scaffold (Foucault)
> Public torture-execution is not lawless rage: it is a measured technique that reconstitutes injured sovereignty by dissymmetry and terror. The people must watch — and that opening is why the scaffold became politically dangerous.

## Summary

Part One of *Discipline and Punish* reconstructs the penal style that the timetable will replace. *Supplice* (Sheridan's "Torture," but the French covers public torture-execution as spectacle) is "a technique; it is not an extreme expression of lawless rage." It has three marks: a quantified, hierarchized production of pain; a legal code correlating that pain with the crime and the rank of the condemned; and a liturgy that marks the body and *must be seen*. The 1670 ordinance's arsenal (hanging, breaking, burning, drawing) was not everyday practice — capital sentences at the Châtelet were under 10 percent — but every serious penalty had to include an element of *supplice*.

Classical procedure (except England) is secret: truth is the sovereign's exclusive privilege. It tends toward **confession**, both one proof among others and the accused's ritual signature. **Judicial torture** (*la question*) is a regulated ordeal mixing investigation and punishment: each degree of proof already implies a degree of guilt. After sentence the displayed body continues the truth-ritual — *amende honorable*, placards, public confession, symbolic pinning of torture to the crime (Vico: "an entire poetics"), death-agony as last proof between human and divine judgement.

The public execution is a **juridico-political** ceremony. Crime attacks the prince personally; to punish is an aspect of *merum imperium*, war on enemies. Every offence contains a *crimen majestatis*; "in the least criminal a potential regicide." "The public execution did not re-establish justice; it reactivated power." **Atrocity** joins crime and punishment: it makes the crime visible in the penalty *and* exceeds it, mastering the challenge. This is a power without continual supervision, "recharged in the ritual display of its reality as 'super-power'."

The people are required as witnesses and limited co-avengers ("scaffold service"). That opening yields carnival inversion, last-word saturnalia, "disturbances around the scaffold," and solidarity with petty illegalities. Broadsheets are a battleground of two investments. Reformers first demand abolition of the *spectacle* because it is politically dangerous, not because they have discovered the criminal's soul.

Part Two then shows why the reformers' own alternative — a "punitive city" of public obstacle-signs, a semio-technique aimed at minds — did not win. They diagnosed the scaffold as the visible knot of unlimited sovereignty *and* of popular illegality. Their cry is first a legal limit (`Noli me tangere`: Enlightenment "man" is what power must leave intact), not yet a science of the criminal. They invented a new economy of power — "not to punish less, but to punish better" — whose "respectable name" is humanity. Within twenty years detention colonized the field they had reserved for analogical, public, lesson-penalties. Three technologies stood face to face at the end of the eighteenth century: sovereign mark/ceremony; reformer sign/representation; prison trace/exercise. Why the third won is the problem Parts Three and Four answer.

## Key Claims

- The condemned man is "the symmetrical, inverted figure of the king" (homage to Kantorowitz): surplus royal power vs. coded lack of power.
- Punishment-as-spectacle works by *certainty of being seen*, not by the later certainty of being caught. Its exemplary mechanics is terror, not inevitability.
- Ancien-Régime society ran on tolerated illegalities; the scaffold's popular festival was one of their political forms. Reform attacks both the sovereign's super-power and the infra-power of those illegalities.
- Foucault does not treat the reformers as hypocrites. Humanity is the name of a calculation (effects on spectators and judges), not a lie they tell.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Hobbes]]**: not cited. Adjacent material: punish as *merum imperium*/war on enemies; crime as personal affront to the prince; pardon as retained royal mastery. Do not equate Foucault's prince with the Leviathan without flagging the gap — Hobbes's sovereign is instituted by covenant; Foucault's scaffold-sovereign is a ceremony of dissymmetry. The later book will call the contract a *fiction* whose technical counterpart is the carceral continuum.
- **[[Thinkers/Jean-Jacques Rousseau]]**: Part Two's long quotation on the contractual right to punish (criminal as internal enemy) is Rousseau, not Hobbes.
- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]** / Enlightenment: "The 'Enlightenment', which discovered the liberties, also invented the disciplines" (III.3) — the scaffold's abolition is not the arrival of those liberties unaccompanied.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Historians have questioned how typical Damiens is, and how suddenly the spectacle died. Foucault himself notes irregular chronology (England loath to abandon public execution; 1760–1840 advances and retreats) and residual torture inside imprisonment.

### From *The History of Sexuality* I (1976)

Part Five restates the same pole as a **right**, not a ceremony. Classical sovereignty is "the right to *take* life or *let* live" (p. 136), symbolized by the sword, exercised as *deduction* (*prélèvement*). Foucault now names [[Thinkers/Thomas Hobbes]]: is this the transferred natural right of self-defense, or a right proper to the sovereign as juridical being (Pufendorf)? Left open. The successor right — "to *foster* life or *disallow* it to the point of death" (p. 138) — is [[Concepts/Biopolitics and Governmentality (Foucault)|bio-power]]. Death's pageantry (the scaffold as political ceremony) is disqualified because death is now power's *limit*, not its display. The death penalty recedes because execution contradicts a power whose role is to multiply life; killing is then justified by the criminal as "biological danger." Wars and genocides expand as the *underside* of a life-administering power, not as a return of the ancient right to kill.

> [!warning] This is the contrast pole of [[Concepts/Disciplinary Society and the Panopticon (Foucault)]]: power *without* continual supervision, recharged by individual spectacle, versus power that functions automatically through the possibility of being seen. "Our society is one not of spectacle, but of surveillance." Volume 1 adds a second contrast: take-life/let-live versus foster-life/disallow-it.
