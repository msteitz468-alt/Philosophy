# Bland Indifference Principle (Bostrom)

> If a fraction *x* of observers with human-type experiences live in simulations, and you have no information marking your own case as special, your credence that *you* are simulated should be *x*. The anthropic step of the simulation argument — weaker than Doomsday, weaker than Laplace.

## Summary

§V of [[Sources/Are You Living in a Computer Simulation - Nick Bostrom (2003)]] takes the further step from "almost all such observers are simulated" to "I should be almost certain that *I* am." Written as (#):

Cr(*SIM* | *f_sim* = *x*) = *x*

provided one has "no information that indicate that our own particular experiences are any more or less likely than other human-type experiences to have been implemented *in vivo* rather than *in machina*."

Bostrom distinguishes two cases and claims both:

1. **Qualitative identity.** Every relevant mind has exactly your information and experiences. Then the step is nearly trivial.
2. **Mere typicality.** The minds are "like" yours only as typical human-type minds, each with a distinct experiential history. The principle still applies if you have no information that bears on *which* of those minds are simulated.

The junk-DNA analogy is the intuition pump. If *x*% of the population has sequence *S* in junk DNA, *S* has no manifestations short of a gene assay, and there are no known correlations with observable traits, then — unless you have been sequenced — credence *x* that you have *S* is rational, "quite irrespective of the fact that the people who have *S* have qualitatively different minds and experiences." Being simulated is to be treated like having *S*.

A betting argument: if almost everyone is simulated, and everyone bets by (#), almost everyone wins; if they bet they are not simulated, almost everyone loses. A continuity argument: as the simulated fraction goes 98% → 99% → 99.9% → … toward the limit in which *everybody* is simulated (from which one can *deduce* that one is), credence should approach certainty in step.

## What the Principle Is Not

- **Not Laplacean indifference.** It "prescribes indifference only between hypotheses about which observer you are, when you have no information about which of these observers you are." It does not prescribe indifference among hypotheses whenever specific information is lacking. Hence it is "immune to Bertrand's paradox and similar predicaments."
- **Not the Doomsday argument.** Readers "may worry that the bland principle … is the same assumption that is responsible for getting the Doomsday argument off the ground." Bostrom denies this. Doomsday needs a *much* stronger premise: reason as if you were a random sample from all people who will ever have lived (past, present, *and future*) **even though we know that we are living in the early twenty-first century**. Bland indifference applies "only to cases where we have no information about which group of people we belong to." The full defense is deferred to "The Doomsday argument, Adam & Eve, UN++, and Quantum Joe" (*Synthese* 2001) and *Anthropic Bias* (2002) — neither yet in the vault.

This is the step Chalmers later calls an "indifference principle" when he rewrites the argument as: if there are no sim blockers, most humanlike beings are sims; if most are sims, we probably are.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Nick Bostrom]]**: the official engine of the move from (3) *f_sim* ≈ 1 to "we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation" ([[Arguments/Simulation Argument (Bostrom)]]).
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: accepts a version of the step inside his preferred two-premise form; he does not re-litigate Doomsday vs bland indifference in *Reality+*.
- **[[Thinkers/Rizwan Virk]]**: popular rendering ("this will be more than 1!") garbles the ratio *A*/(*A*+*B*) as something that can exceed unity. The principle assigns credence *x*, never greater than 1.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- The principle is only as good as the "no distinguishing information" clause. Apparent sim-signs or nonsim-signs (Chalmers: living early in the universe; biological-only consciousness) would license a departure from *x*.
- Self-sampling vs self-indication remains contested in anthropic reasoning generally; Bostrom's book is the place that debate lives, and it is not yet ingested.
- If simulated and biological streams are *systematically* different in ways we could in principle notice (glitches, conditional-rendering artifacts), the junk-DNA analogy fails.
