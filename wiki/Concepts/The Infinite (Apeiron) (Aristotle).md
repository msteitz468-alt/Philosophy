# The Infinite (Apeiron) (Aristotle)
> There is no **actual** infinite body. The infinite exists the way a day or the games exist: one finite thing after another. "It is not what has nothing outside it that is infinite, but what always has something outside it."

## Summary

*Physics* III.4–8. Motion belongs to the continuous; the infinite presents itself first in the continuous. Physics asks whether there is a **sensible** infinite magnitude.

Predecessors make the infinite a principle: Pythagoreans and Plato (Great and Small); the physicists; Anaxagoras; Democritus; Anaximander's "deathless and imperishable" that encompasses and steers. Aristotle: the infinite cannot be a separable substance, nor an actual body. Body is "bounded by a surface." An infinite element would annihilate its contraries. Natural places (up/down, centre/extremity) cannot belong to an infinite body. "There is no body which is actually infinite."

Denying the infinite altogether is also impossible: time, the divisibility of magnitudes, and number all require it. So it exists **in one sense, not another**.

Magnitude is not actually infinite; it is infinite **by division**. This "potential" is not like the statue that will later stand finished. The infinite *is* as day or the Olympic games *are* — successive being, always a different finite. Infinite **by addition** is the inverse of infinite by division inside a finite magnitude (proportional remainders). There is no potential infinite by addition that exceeds every assignable magnitude (that would require an actual infinite body). Number has a minimum (the one) and no maximum; magnitude has no actual maximum, and every assigned size can be surpassed toward smallness.

Definition: "A quantity is infinite if it is such that we can always take a part outside what has been already taken. On the other hand, what has nothing outside it is complete and whole." The infinite is matter and privation, contained not containing, unknowable *qua* infinite. Predecessors treated *apeiron* as what contains; Aristotle inverts: *qua* infinite it is contained.

Book VI applies this to Zeno: the runner contacts infinitely many halves **in respect of divisibility**, not a quantitative infinite of extremities in a finite stretch. Time is infinite in the same sense. There is no first part of a change. One process cannot occupy infinite time except rotatory locomotion.

## Key Claims / Positions

- No actual infinite body.
- Potential infinite = always another part, like a day.
- Infinite by division of a finite continuum; not an ever-larger cosmos.
- Whole/complete = nothing outside; infinite = always something outside.

### How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Concepts/Apeiron (Anaximander)]]**: named as deathless encompassing principle. Aristotle keeps the word and denies the cosmology — no extra body alongside the elements, no actual infinite.
- **[[Thinkers/Zeno of Elea]]**: the two infinites (divisibility vs extremities) are the diagnosis of the dichotomy and Achilles.
- **[[Thinkers/Plato]]**: Great and Small as material infinite; Aristotle treats this as a confusion of matter with a magnitude.
- **Modern mathematics**: completed infinities (Cantor) reject Aristotle's ban on the actual infinite. The potential infinite remains the working infinity of Greek geometry ("the mathematicians need only indefinite extendibility of the finite line").
- **[[Thinkers/Hegel]]**: bad vs true infinite is a later reworking of "always another" vs the whole.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] If time is eternal (Book VIII), is time an actual infinite? Aristotle's official line is successive being (the games), not a completed totality of nows.
- The ban on an actual infinite body is load-bearing for a finite cosmos with natural places. Once that cosmology falls, the argument against actual infinity has to be rebuilt.
