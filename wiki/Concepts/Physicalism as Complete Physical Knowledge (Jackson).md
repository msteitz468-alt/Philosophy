# Physicalism as Complete Physical Knowledge (Jackson)

> Physicalism is the claim that the world is *entirely* physical, not merely largely physical — so a physicalist is committed to holding that complete physical knowledge is complete knowledge *simpliciter*.

## Summary

This is the premise-fixing move that makes the [[Arguments/Knowledge Argument (Jackson)|knowledge argument]] a threat rather than a curiosity. Jackson opens "What Mary Didn't Know" by refusing the weak reading of physicalism: it is "not the noncontroversial thesis that the actual world is largely physical, but the challenging thesis that it is entirely physical."

The argument for the entailment is a world-comparison. Suppose complete physical knowledge were *not* complete knowledge. Then our world must differ from a world *W(P)* for which physical knowledge *is* complete. But our world and *W(P)* agree in all matters physical, so the difference must lie in nonphysical facts. Hence physicalism would be false at our world — "though contingently so, for it would be true at *W(P)*." The conclusion: physicalists cannot treat the completeness of physical knowledge as an optional extra; it falls out of the thesis itself.

A footnote blocks the obvious linguistic objection (raised by Terence Horgan): the claim is *not* that under physicalism only what is expressed in explicitly physical language counts as an item of knowledge. It is the conditional — if physicalism is true, then someone who knows everything expressed or expressible in explicitly physical language knows everything. The vocabulary is not privileged; the coverage is.

"Physical" is taken in a deliberately **wide** sense: everything in *completed* physics, chemistry, and neurophysiology, plus "all there is to know about the causal and relational facts consequent upon all this, **including of course functional roles**." Functionalism is thus explicitly inside the target, not an escape from it — a point Jackson reuses in his reply to Churchland's parity objection ("You do not need color television to learn physics or functionalist psychology").

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Frank Jackson]]** (1986): the definition is doing argumentative work. It converts "Mary learns something" from an observation about her psychology into a counterexample to a metaphysical thesis, and it forecloses the reply that physicalism was only ever a claim about what mostly exists.
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: the same commitment in modal dress. [[Concepts/Logical Supervenience and Reductive Explanation (Chalmers)]] defines materialism as the global logical supervenience of all positive facts on the physical, and explicitly matches it to Jackson's formulation that "every minimal physical duplicate of our world is a duplicate *simpliciter*." Where Jackson's *W(P)* comparison is a one-paragraph move in an epistemic argument, Chalmers builds it into two-dimensional semantics. The vault's Chalmers pages already cite Jackson's phrasing; this page supplies its primary-source origin.
- **[[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]]**: accepts the definition and turns it against the argument. Precisely because "all the physical facts" is exhaustive — including every reactive disposition — Dennett holds that a Mary who really had it would not be surprised by red at all ([[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]]). Same premise, opposite verdict on whether the thought experiment can be run as described.
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]]**: does not frame the issue as completeness of a knowledge set. His claim is that the *direction of travel* of physical objectivity abandons the point of view ([[Concepts/Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)]]), so physicalism is unintelligible rather than falsified by a missing fact. The contrast is instructive: Jackson needs physicalism to be a sharply defined thesis in order to refute it; Nagel argues we do not yet understand it well enough for refutation to be the relevant response.
- **[[Thinkers/John Searle]]**: agrees consciousness is not captured by formal or functional description, but ([[Concepts/Biological Naturalism (Searle)]]) counts consciousness itself as a biological and therefore physical feature — which is to reject Jackson's implicit assumption that "physical" and "phenomenal" cannot overlap in the completed science.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The definition is only as strong as the notion of "completed physics." Jackson's wide sense makes the premise generous to the physicalist, but it also makes it unverifiable: no one can display the completed science whose completeness is being tested. Compare the vault's [[Concepts/Anomaly and Crisis (Kuhn)|Kuhnian]] and [[Concepts/Basic Statements and the Empirical Basis (Popper)|Popperian]] doubts about any claim made on behalf of a finished physics.
- > [!warning] If Dennett is right that the stipulation is incoherent for phenomenal cases, the world-comparison argument survives untouched but the knowledge argument loses its instance. The dispute is over whether "all the physical facts" is a coherent epistemic state for a subject, not over what physicalism asserts.
