# Transcendental Idealism (Kant)

> Space, time, and the objects given in them are empirically real and transcendentally ideal: we cognize things only as they appear under the conditions of our sensibility, not as they are in themselves.

## Summary

Transcendental idealism is the official doctrine of the Transcendental Aesthetic, used for the rest of the *Critique* both positively (to explain synthetic a priori cognition) and negatively (to limit cognition to appearances).

**Empirical reality.** Space and time have objective validity for all objects of the senses. Bodies do not merely *seem* to exist outside me (B 69). This is not Berkeleyan subjective idealism.

**Transcendental ideality.** Space and time are "nothing as soon as we leave out the condition of the possibility of all experience, and take [them] as something that grounds the things in themselves" (A 28 / B 44). We speak of them only from the human standpoint (A 26–30 / B 42–45). Through sensibility "we do not cognize the constitution of things in themselves merely indistinctly, but rather not at all" (A 44 / B 62). The Leibniz–Wolff contrast of distinct vs confused representation is a *logical* distinction; Kant's sensibility/understanding contrast is *transcendental* — origin and content, not degree of clarity.

**Think vs cognize.** "We can cognize no object as a thing in itself, only as appearance; yet we must be able to *think* things in themselves, else there would be an appearance without anything that appears" (B xxvi–xxvii). One may think whatever does not contradict itself; to *cognize* requires proving possibility, by experience or a priori (B xxvi n.).

**What the doctrine does in the book.**
- *Positively:* it accounts for synthetic a priori cognition in mathematics (construction in pure intuition) and, later, for the principles of pure understanding.
- *Negatively:* it limits the categories to possible experience and diagnoses the Dialectic — reason treating appearances as if they were things in themselves generates the Paralogisms, Antinomies, and Ideal.
- *Practically:* the same will can be naturally necessitated as appearance and free as thing in itself (B xxvii–xxviii). This is the theoretical warrant for the *Groundwork*'s [[Concepts/Two Standpoints: Sensible World and Intelligible World (Kant)|two standpoints]].

The Antinomies will call transcendental idealism "the key" to the cosmological dialectic: the world-whole is not a given object. The B Refutation of Idealism will then argue that consciousness of my own existence in time proves something persistent *in space* — empirical realism inside transcendental idealism.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Berkeley.** Closest historical look-alike, and Kant's standing worry. Appearance ≠ illusion; empirical reality of bodies is asserted, not reduced to ideas in a mind. See [[Thinkers/George Berkeley]], [[Contradictions/Subjective Idealism vs. Physical Realism]].
- **Descartes.** The "scandal" that outer things must be assumed on faith (B xxxix n.) is the leftover of Cartesian doubt. The Refutation of Idealism is aimed here.
- **Hegel.** The thing-in-itself is the limit Hegel sublates: to draw the boundary is already to have thought beyond it. See [[Contradictions/Hegelian Monism vs. Kantian Dualism]].
- **Husserl.** Phenomenology continues the critique of reason as investigation of conditions of givenness, but "when a perceptual intention is intuitively fulfilled, it reaches the existing thing *itself*." The *Ding an sich* is rejected. See [[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]].
- **Heidegger.** Reads the A-edition imagination as the root Kant "shrank back" from in B. Transcendental idealism becomes a failed ontology of finite temporality. See [[Thinkers/Martin Heidegger]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The precise meaning of "transcendentally ideal" remains contested even among Kant scholars (Guyer/Wood flag this). The vault records Kant's official pairing — empirical reality + transcendental ideality — without choosing between "two-object" and "two-aspect" readings. The *Groundwork* (4:458) treats the intelligible world as a *standpoint*, which pulls toward two-aspect.
- > [!warning] If noumena are *nothing* for us, freedom, God, and immortality become empty; if they are a second knowable realm, the restriction of the *Critique* is abandoned. Kant's official line: thinkable, not knowable; fillable by practical reason.
