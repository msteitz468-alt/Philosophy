# Knight of Faith and Infinite Resignation (Kierkegaard)
> Two movements, not two temperaments: resignation gives up the finite by one's own strength; faith gets the finite back on the strength of the absurd.

## Summary

Johannes *de silentio*'s "Preamble from the Heart" draws the distinction on which the rest of *Fear and Trembling* depends. There is no faith without prior resignation; caricatures of faith (lukewarm "there's no need to worry," miserable "who knows, it's possible") have already been scorned by infinite resignation.

**Infinite resignation.** A young man sets his heart on a princess. Understanding concludes the relation cannot be translated into reality. He does not forget her and become someone else (only lower natures forget themselves). He concentrates the whole content of his life in one wish and the whole result of reflection in one act of consciousness, then performs the movement. The love takes on "the expression of eternal love," is transfigured into a love of the eternal being. He is enough unto himself; what the princess later does cannot disturb him. Peace and repose in the pain. Johannes can do this: it is "a purely philosophical movement," "my eternal consciousness is my love of God," and it is higher than anything. It takes a purely human courage to renounce the whole of temporality in order to win eternity.

**Faith.** The knight of faith does exactly the same — and then one more movement. "I nevertheless believe that I shall get her, namely on the strength of the absurd, on the strength of the fact that for God all things are possible." The absurd is not the improbable, the unexpected, or the unforeseen. It is what the understanding has, with passion, concluded to be humanly impossible. To suppose one has faith without recognizing that impossibility is to deceive oneself and not yet to have reached resignation. "Through faith I don't renounce anything, on the contrary in faith I receive everything." Abraham did not renounce his claim on Isaac; through faith he received Isaac.

**The shopman.** Knights of resignation are recognizable (gliding, bold gait; they waver an instant on landing). The knight of faith looks like a tax-gatherer. He belongs altogether to finitude — Sunday church, roast head of lamb, building-sites, his pipe — and yet "not the least thing does he do except on the strength of the absurd." He drains the deep sorrow of existence in infinite resignation and finitude tastes as good as to one who never knew anything higher. The dancer's hardest task is to leap straight into a definite position; "to express the sublime in the pedestrian absolutely — that is something only the knight of faith can do — and it is the one and only marvel."

**Abraham's two movements.** He believed God would not demand Isaac of him, while still willing to offer him if demanded. He believed on the strength of the absurd. Even the gleam of the knife did not stop the second movement. Had Isaac actually been sacrificed, faith was still that God could give a new Isaac, or bring him back, *in this life*. A faith only for a future life would have been easier — "not really faith but only its remotest possibility." Johannes would have made resignation and then been at a loss if Isaac were returned; "what Abraham found the easiest of all would for me be hard, to find joy again in Isaac."

Johannes can describe the movements (like swimming in a belt from the ceiling) and cannot perform the last. He is "wedded to the left hand"; faith "is humble enough to demand the right."

## Key Claims / Positions

- Resignation is the last stage before faith; without it there is no faith.
- Resignation is within human strength; faith is a marvel, a paradoxical and humble courage.
- Faith is not an aesthetic emotion and not the immediate inclination of the heart. Philosophy has no right to substitute the immediate for it.
- The movement of faith must be made continually. One does not lose finitude; one gains it all of a piece.
- Irony and humour belong in infinite resignation (the individual's incommensurability with reality). They are not faith.

### How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Johannes *de silentio*** (this source): the distinction is conceptual analysis of "hard to face" differences, not a psychology of types.
- **[[Thinkers/Socrates]]**: his ignorance is infinite resignation. He is not a knight of faith.
- **[[Thinkers/Jean-Paul Sartre]]**: the lecture's Abraham is a legislator without a sign. Johannes's Abraham is a double-mover who already has God. Sartre's anguish is closer to the tragic hero's security in the universal than to this second movement.
- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]] / Deleuze**: Kierkegaard's leap vs Nietzsche's unbelief. This page is the leap. Eternal return is not a second movement that gets the princess back; it is selective ontology of what can be willed again. See [[Concepts/Eternal Return as Selective Ontology (Deleuze-Nietzsche)]].
- **Hadot**: philosophy as a way of life already listed Kierkegaard among modern recoveries. Resignation as a spiritual exercise one can discipline oneself into fits that recovery; the second movement does not — Johannes says philosophy should not give an account of faith.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Hannay reads the shopman/Abraham analogy as sharing only the double movement, not sacrifice or ethical breach, and reads both as allegory of an attitude to value (nothing has value merely because one values it; faith is the appendix that things have their value from God). That is Hannay, not Johannes.
- Whether Johannes is designed not to understand Abraham, or understands the merman genuinely and Abraham not at all, Hannay leaves open.
