# The Logic of Looks (Sellars)

> §§10–20 of [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|EPM]]: "*x* looks red to *S*" is not a triadic relation but a **report with the assertive endorsement withheld** — and the concept of *looking* green presupposes the concept of *being* green, which inverts the empiricist order of analysis and breaks logical atomism.

## The Target

Philosophers "have found it easy to suppose that such a sentence as 'The tomato looks red to Jones' says that a certain triadic relation, *looking* or *appearing*, obtains among a physical object, a person, and a quality" — assimilating "*x* looks φ to *S*" to "*x* is between *y* and *z*," of the general form "R(*x,y,z*)." Having supposed this, philosophers ask "Is this relation analyzable?"; sense-datum theorists answer yes, in terms of sense data; theorists of appearing answer that such facts are "ultimate and irreducible."

Sellars starts from "the simple but fundamental point that the sense of 'red' in which things **look** red is, on the face of it, the same as that in which things **are** red. When one glimpses an object and decides that it looks red … and wonders whether it really *is* red, one is surely wondering whether the colour — red — which it *looks* to have is the one it *really does* have." Hyphenating into an insoluble unity "looks-red" obscures this; the genuine insight buried in that manoeuvre is only "that *looks* is not a relation between a person, a thing, and a quality."

## The Necktie Story (§14)

John works in a necktie shop and has only ever seen ties in daylight. Electric light is installed. "Here is a handsome green one," he says; outside, Jim points out it is blue. John protests that it *was* green in the shop; Jim replies that neckties don't change colour with location, and that a change back in daylight "would be a queer kind of change, wouldn't it?"

John learns to suppress the report. Afterwards: "He doesn't *see* that it is blue, nor would he say that he sees it to be blue." Asked what he does see: "If I didn't know that the tie is blue … I would swear that I was seeing a green tie and seeing that it *is* green. It is *as though* I were seeing the necktie to be green."

The moral turns on the fact that sentences like "This is green" have "both a **fact-stating** and a **reporting** use." Once John stifles the report "This necktie is green," "there is no other report about colour and the necktie which he knows how to make." He now says "This necktie is blue," but "he is not making a reporting use of this sentence. He uses it as the conclusion of an inference."

Then John learns "This tie looks green." It is tempting to read this as his having noticed a *new kind of objective fact* — "a minimal fact, one which it is safer to report because one is less likely to be mistaken," and logically independent of the perceiver's beliefs and conceptual framework. **This is exactly the account Sellars rejects**; it is the Given re-entering as the givenness of *looks*.

## The Positive Account: Endorsement Withheld (§§16–17)

The experience of something's *looking* green is, *as an experience*, "obviously very much like that of seeing something to be green." But seeing is "not just an experience":

> To say that a certain experience is a *seeing that* something is the case, is to do more than describe the experience. It is to characterize it as, so to speak, making an assertion or claim, and — which is the point I wish to stress — to **endorse** that claim.

Hence the core suggestion:

> The statement "*X* looks green to Jones" differs from "*X* is green" in that whereas the latter … [ascribes the] propositional claim to Jones' experience and endorses it, the former ascribes the claim but does *not* endorse it.

"Two experiences may be identical *as experiences*, and yet the one be properly referred to as a case of something's *being* seen to be green, the other merely as a case of something's *looking* green." Saying "*X* merely looks green to *S*" *rejects* the claim; saying "*X* looks green to me now" reports "the fact that my experience is, so to speak, intrinsically, *as an experience*, indistinguishable from a veridical one of seeing that *x* is green," while raising, "so to speak in a higher court, the question 'to endorse or not to endorse.'" One says "I *see* that *x* is green" only when that question has come up.

Sellars borrows and amends [[Thinkers/Gilbert Ryle|Ryle]]: seeing that something is the case is an *achievement*, "see" an "achievement word." Sellars prefers "a 'so it is' or 'just so' word, for the root idea is that of **truth**." On the experience itself he turns a Kantian phrase: the propositional claim is "evoked or wrung from the perceiver by the object perceived. Here Nature — to turn Kant's simile … on its head — puts us to the question."

### Qualitative, existential, and generic looking (§17)
The account gives a unified treatment of three cases by varying *how much* of the claim is endorsed:
- **"The tree looks bent"** — endorsing the existence of the tree, withholding the rest (qualitative).
- **"There looks to be a bent tree over there"** — "refusing to endorse any but the most general aspect of the claim, namely, that there is an 'over there' as opposed to a 'here'" (existential).
- **Merely generic looks** — a necktie can look red without looking scarlet or crimson, because the propositional claim involved may be the more determinable one. "This … would be puzzling indeed if looking red were a natural as opposed to epistemic fact about objects." (Compare: *S* can believe Cleopatra's Needle is tall without believing it to be any determinate number of feet tall.)

## The Presupposition Thesis and the Break with Atomism (§§18–19)

> The concept of *looking* green, the ability to recognize that something looks green, **presupposes** the concept of *being* green, and … the latter concept involves the ability to tell what colours objects have by looking at them — which, in turn, involves knowing in what circumstances to place an object if one wishes to ascertain its colour by looking at it.

This preserves the necessary truth

> *x* is red ≡ *x* would look red to standard observers in standard conditions

but refuses it the status of a definition: it holds "not because the right-hand side is the definition of '*x* is red,' but because '**standard conditions**' means *conditions in which things look what they are*." Which conditions are standard "is, at the common-sense level, specified by a list of conditions which exhibit the vagueness and open texture characteristic of ordinary discourse." (In the shop: "Because it is blue, and blue objects look green in this kind of light"; in daylight: "Because the tie is green … in daylight things look what they are.")

Sellars sees exactly how disruptive this is: it is "at least prima facie, out of step with the basic presuppositions of **logical atomism**." As long as *looking* green was the notion to which *being* green reduces, one could claim "that fundamental concepts pertaining to observable fact have that logical independence of one another which is characteristic of the empiricist tradition." On his account they do not. The threatened regress — one cannot form the concept *green* without already knowing which circumstances are standard, which requires noticing colours — is blocked by denying that concepts come one at a time:

> One can have the concept of green only by having a whole **battery** of concepts of which it is one element.

It is not enough "to respond, when one is in point of fact in standard conditions, to green objects with the vocable 'This is green'"; for that response to be a *report* usable to determine colour by looking, "the subject must know that conditions of this sort are appropriate." This does not require having concepts before one has them, but it does make concept-acquisition holistic.

## Anticipated Objections (§§19–20)

Sellars stages two replies against himself:
- The **logical atomist**: physical-object space rests on a more fundamental logical space of *sense contents*, whose concepts do have the required independence — "until you have disposed … of the idea that there is a more fundamental logical space than that of physical objects in Space and Time … your incipient *Méditations Hegeliennes* are premature."
- The **sense-datum theorist**: grant that physical redness is not analyzed via *looking* red; why not analyze physical properties *directly* into the qualities of sense contents?

Sellars's answer is to press the question "How does the sense-datum theorist come by the framework of sense contents?" — since it is by asking us to reflect on *looking* that he makes the framework convincing, and the analysis of *looks* "has not … revealed any such items as sense-contents."

He also exposes the "red expanse" gambit — that seeing an object is seeing its facing surface, that a surface is a two-dimensional red expanse, and that this expanse is the common descriptive content of veridical and non-veridical experiences alike — as "a simple **equivocation** on the phrase 'having a red surface'": we slide from the familiar fact that an object may be one colour on the surface and another inside, to "surface" in the sense of a bulgy two-dimensional particular.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Reverses the order of analysis in [[Concepts/Sense-Data vs. Physical Objects (Russell)]]. For Russell, appearances are the epistemic starting point from which physical objects are inferred; for Sellars, "looks" is *parasitic* on "is" both conceptually and in the order of learning. Russell's simplicity-and-instinctive-belief realism survives; his foundational use of sense-data does not.
- > [!warning] Cuts against [[Concepts/Primary and Secondary Qualities (Locke)]] and [[Concepts/Impressions and Ideas - Copy Principle (Hume)]]: on Sellars's account you cannot build the concept *red* out of having red impressions, because recognizing an impression *as* red is already a conceptual, learned, framework-dependent achievement.
- The account leaves an explicit debt: what *is* "the intrinsic character of the common descriptive content" of seeing, looking, and there-looking-to-be? Sellars defers this to the second half of the essay ([[Concepts/Myth of Jones (Sellars)]]).
