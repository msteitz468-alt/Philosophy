# Pre-reflective Cogito (Sartre)
> Every positional consciousness of an object is at the same time a non-positional consciousness of itself — and that immediate self-consciousness, not Descartes's reflective "I think," is the starting point of ontology.

## Summary

In the Introduction to *Being and Nothingness* ("The Pursuit of Being," §§III–IV) Sartre refuses both Berkeley's *esse est percipi* and the "primacy of knowledge" that would make consciousness a knowledge of knowledge. If consciousness of consciousness were an *idea ideae* (Spinoza) or Alain's "to know is to know that one knows," we would face either an infinite regress of knower-known pairs or a final term that is not conscious of itself. "Consciousness of self is not dual. If we wish to avoid an infinite regress, there must be an immediate, non-cognitive relation of the self to itself" (BN liii).

That relation is the **pre-reflective cogito**. "Every positional consciousness of an object is at the same time a non-positional consciousness of itself" (BN liii). Counting cigarettes, I disclose an objective property of the collection — they are a dozen — and I may have no positional consciousness of counting. Children can add spontaneously without being able to explain how (Piaget). Yet if asked "What are you doing there?" I reply at once "I am counting." The reply aims not only at a present reflection but at "those fleeting consciousnesses which have passed without being reflected-on." Reflection therefore has no primacy: "it is the non-reflective consciousness which renders the reflection possible; there is a pre-reflective cogito which is the condition of the Cartesian cogito" (BN liii).

Barnes follows Sartre's later punctuation: English can drop the "of" that French syntax requires, and Sartre puts it in parentheses (*conscience (de) soi*) to mark that self-consciousness is not a knowledge of self. Pleasure is not first a psychic event which then receives the quality "conscious," nor is there first a consciousness which is later stained with pleasure. "Pleasure is the being of self-consciousness and this self-consciousness is the law of being of pleasure" (BN lv). Consciousness is "existence through and through," a non-substantial absolute: "pure 'appearance' in the sense that it exists only to the degree to which it appears," and "total emptiness (since the entire world is outside it)" (BN lvi).

Two further claims hang on this starting point. First, **existence implies essence** for consciousness: "its existence implies its essence" (BN lv); there is no law *of* consciousness, only consciousness of a law. Second, the same pre-reflective being of the *percipiens* yields Sartre's **ontological proof**: "consciousness is consciousness of something" means not that consciousness constitutes its object but that "transcendence is the constitutive structure of consciousness; that is, that consciousness is born supported by a being which is not itself" (BN lxi). A "pure" subjectivity disappears. This is closer to Descartes (being, not knowledge) than to Kant's refutation of problematical idealism, but it demotes Descartes's own *cogito* to a reflective act whose object is an earlier, non-reflective consciousness.

The 1936 essay *The Transcendence of the Ego* (which Barnes's introduction treats as the foundation BN presupposes) already made this move: the consciousness that says "I am" is not the consciousness that thinks. BN's Introduction writes the ontology that essay prepared.

## Key Claims / Positions

- Consciousness of consciousness is not a second, positional knowledge; it is the mode of existence of any consciousness of something.
- The Cartesian *cogito* is reflective and secondary; the pre-reflective cogito is its condition.
- No unconscious: a consciousness ignorant of itself is "absurd" (BN lii).
- Consciousness is a non-substantial absolute whose existence implies its essence.
- Intentionality is an ontological proof of transphenomenal being, not a constitution of objects.

### How Sartre Differs From Prior Thinkers in the Wiki

- **vs [[Concepts/Cogito (Descartes)]]**: Descartes's certainty is a reflective act ("I am aware that I doubt"). Sartre keeps the *cogito* as absolute starting point (the lecture will insist on this) but relocates it *before* reflection.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]]**: keeps "all consciousness is consciousness of something" and rejects *esse est percipi* for the noema; rejects *hyle* as a hybrid that is neither consciousness nor thing.
- **vs Spinoza** (*idea ideae*): self-consciousness is not a knowledge of knowledge.
- **vs the 1945/46 lecture** ([[Sources/Existentialism is a Humanism - Jean-Paul Sartre (1946)]]): the lecture still names the *cogito* as the one absolute and immediately adds that it attains the Other. BN's Introduction stops at the pre-reflective structure and the ontological proof of the in-itself; the Other is Part Three.
- **vs [[Concepts/Tacit Cogito (Merleau-Ponty)]]**: do not equate. Sartre's pre-reflective cogito is lucid, non-positional self-consciousness, the *condition* of the Cartesian cogito. MP's tacit cogito is anonymous, has only a fleeting hold, and "is only a *Cogito* when it has expressed itself."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] If pre-reflective consciousness is wholly lucid, repression cannot be a hidden region — see [[Contradictions/Sartre vs Freud - Lucidity vs the Unconscious]] and [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)]].
- The Introduction already rules out both realism (the in-itself cannot act on consciousness) and idealism (consciousness cannot constitute the in-itself). The rest of the book has to show another relation.
