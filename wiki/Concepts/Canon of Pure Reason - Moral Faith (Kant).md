# Canon of Pure Reason — Moral Faith (Kant)

> "All interest of my reason (the speculative as well as the practical) is united in the following three questions: 1. What can I know? 2. What should I do? 3. What may I hope?" (A 804–805 / B 832–833)

## Summary

The Doctrine of Method's **Canon** is "the sum total of the a priori principles of the correct use of certain cognitive faculties." There is no canon for *speculative* pure reason (it is dialectical); if any, it is **practical** (A 795–797 / B 823–825). The ultimate speculative objects — freedom of the will, immortality, God — have almost no speculative utility; their importance is practical (A 798–800 / B 826–828).

**Three questions.** What can I know? (answered by the Doctrine of Elements: possible experience). What should I do? (practical — this book only prepares it). What may I hope? = "If I do what I should, what may I then hope?" (A 805 / B 833).

**Highest good.** "Happiness in exact proportion with the morality of rational beings, through which they are worthy of it, alone constitutes the highest good of a world" (A 813–814 / B 841–842). This connection is not given in nature; it requires a highest reason as cause of nature. "Thus God and a future life are two presuppositions that are not to be separated from the obligation that pure reason imposes on us" (A 811 / B 839). Moral laws are *not* derived from God's will: "we will not hold actions to be obligatory because they are God's commands, but will rather regard them as divine commands because we are internally obligated to them" (A 819 / B 847).

**Opinion / belief / knowledge.** Opinion = subjectively *and* objectively insufficient; belief = subjectively sufficient, objectively insufficient; knowledge = both (A 822 / B 850). No opinion in pure mathematics or moral principles. **Moral belief** (*Glaube*) in God and a future world is inexorable if the moral law is one's maxim: "I will inexorably believe in the existence of God and a future life… the conviction is not logical but moral certainty… I must not even say 'It is morally certain that there is a God,' … but rather 'I am morally certain'" (A 828–829 / B 856–857).

This is *not* the *Groundwork*. There are no CI formulas, no autonomy-as-self-legislation, no kingdom of ends (only Leibniz's "realm of grace," A 812 / B 840). The Canon *prepares* practical philosophy. Practical freedom is here "proved through experience" as *arbitrium liberum* (A 802–803 / B 830–831) and must not be collapsed into the later autonomy doctrine.

**Discipline (context).** Philosophy cannot imitate mathematics (definitions, axioms, demonstrations) — "houses of cards" (A 727 / B 755). Reason's claim is only "the agreement of free citizens, each of whom must be able to express his reservations, indeed even his veto" (A 738–739 / B 766–767). Skepticism is "a resting-place… not a dwelling-place" (A 761 / B 789). "The critical path alone is still open" (A 856 / B 884).

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Groundwork (1785).** The Canon is the first outline of the argument that runs through all three Critiques: practical reason can justify metaphysical *beliefs* theoretical reason cannot *know*. The Groundwork then searches for the supreme principle of morality itself.
- **Utilitarianism.** Happiness is not the ground of the moral law; it is the second component of the highest good, proportioned to *worthiness*. See [[Contradictions/Utilitarian Consequentialism vs. Kantian Deontology]].
- **Popper.** Unrestricted freedom of criticism; hypotheses as weapons not foundations; skeptic as taskmaster; critique as determination of *boundaries*. See [[Concepts/Problem of Demarcation (Kant and Popper)]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] "I am morally certain" is not knowledge. Later readers (Jacobi, Hegel, Nietzsche) treat this as either a smuggled restoration of metaphysics or a confession that morality needs a fiction.
- > [!warning] Schopenhauer, *Parerga* I Fragments §13: after the Dialectic’s “death-knell,” Kant “had to try to soften its impression, thus to administer a sop to it as an anodyne.” The postulate has no theoretical objectivity; later writers wrongly treat it as dogmatic theism; it is valid “solely within morality… not a straw’s breadth further.” And “Kant’s moral theology also undermines morality” (virtue becomes long-sighted egoism). This is an enemy’s reading of the Canon, now primary. It does not replace A 828–829. See [[Sources/Parerga and Paralipomena Volume 1 - Schopenhauer (Cambridge 2014)]].
- > [!warning] *Parerga* II Ch. XV: Demopheles (not the author) equates religion with Kant’s moral theology as “hypotheses for a practical purpose.” The author’s voice keeps the demolition and refuses residual faith: philosophy concerns what can be *known*. See [[Concepts/Religion as Allegory (Schopenhauer)]].
