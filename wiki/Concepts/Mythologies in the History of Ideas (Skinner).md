# Mythologies in the History of Ideas (Skinner)

> Chapter 4 of [[Sources/Visions of Politics Volume 1 - Regarding Method - Quentin Skinner (2002)|*Regarding Method*]] — the revised version of Skinner's 1969 essay and the founding document of the **Cambridge School**. A diagnostic catalogue of the errors produced by reading canonical texts for "dateless wisdom": what results are "not histories but more appropriately as **mythologies**."

## The Target

The orthodoxy under attack holds that the classic texts contain "**dateless wisdom**" and "timeless elements" bearing on "abiding questions," and should be read "as though it were written by a contemporary." Skinner's stated aim is to "question, criticise and if possible discredit" it.

He is careful that the error is not simple carelessness but structural, and to that extent unavoidable:

> We must classify in order to understand, and we can only classify the unfamiliar in terms of the familiar.

He borrows the "priority of paradigms" from Gombrich and [[Thinkers/Thomas Kuhn|Kuhn]], and Collingwood's "constellations of absolute presuppositions," to explain why the historian's own mental set does the damage. The mythologies are what happens when that set goes unexamined.

## The Catalogue

### 1. The Mythology of Doctrines

Expecting each writer to "enunciate some doctrine on each of the topics regarded as constitutive of the subject." Three forms:

- **Scattered remarks promoted to doctrines.** Incidental comments are converted into an author's "doctrine of" something — Marsilius on the separation of powers, Coke on judicial review, Hooker's "social contract," Locke's "political trust," Harrington. The anachronism is in the question, not the answer.
- **Reification into unit-ideas.** In [[Thinkers/Arthur O. Lovejoy|Lovejoy]]-style histories the doctrine "is hypostasised into an entity," and "the fact that ideas presuppose agents readily disappears as the ideas get up to do battle on their own behalf." This generates the hunt for **anticipations** and interminable disputes about whether an idea was "really there."
- **The converse: blame for absence.** A writer who fails to produce the mandatory doctrine "is criticised for falling short of their proper task." Skinner's sharpest example is Strauss's demonological assignment of blame to Hobbes and Machiavelli for a "decided lowering of tone," and the practice of supplying writers with beliefs they never held: "**History then indeed becomes a pack of tricks we play on the dead.**"

The begged question underneath all three: "whether any of these writers ever intended, or could have intended, to do what they are castigated for not having done."

### 2. The Mythology of Coherence

Supplying texts "with the coherence they may appear to lack" — the interpreter's metaphors are of "effort and quest," and coherence gets displayed "at all costs." Two consequences:

- **Overriding the author's own statements.** Locke's early authoritarian writings, or Hobbes's Review and Conclusion, are discounted when they spoil the system. Skinner names Warrender, Hood, and A. E. Taylor for religious-whole readings of Hobbes that override what Hobbes said he was doing.
- **Resolving antinomies.** Apparent contradictions are assumed never to be real. Strauss's esotericism — "reading between the lines," from *Persecution and the Art of Writing* — is the limiting case, and Skinner's objection is that it rests on circular a priori "facts" about what the author must have meant.

### 3. The Mythology of Prolepsis

> the conflation of the asymmetry between the significance an observer may justifiably claim to find in a given historical episode and the meaning of that episode itself.

Petrarch's ascent of Mont Ventoux "inaugurating the Renaissance"; Plato as "totalitarian party-politician" (Popper); Rousseau blamed for totalitarianism (Talmon, Chapman, Cobban); Machiavelli "at the gateway of the modern world" (Cassirer); Locke as "liberal." The diagnostic sign: **"the episode has to await the future to learn its meaning."**

This is the near-relative of [[Thinkers/Herbert Butterfield|Butterfield's]] Whig interpretation — significance read backwards from an outcome the agent could not have had in view.

### 4. Parochialism

Skinner writes of "two particular forms of such parochialism" rather than naming a fourth mythology; the error is the observer's misuse of his own vantage point.

- **Spurious influence.** The genealogy Burke ← Bolingbroke ← Locke ← Hobbes ← Machiavelli. Skinner proposes three necessary conditions for an influence claim: that B studied A; that B could not have found the doctrine elsewhere; and that B could not have arrived at it independently. Most claims fail all three.
- **Misdescribing the sense**, so that "alien elements dissolve into a misleading familiarity" — the Levellers as "liberal democracy" (Brailsford); Locke's consent read as a modern theory of legitimacy.

## The Governing Constraint

No agent "meant or achieved something which they could never be brought to accept as a correct description" of what they meant or achieved. Questions violating this are, in Skinner's phrase, "**void for lack of reference**."

## The Positive Thesis

The meaning of what was said is not the same as what the writer meant by saying it, so reading the text "over and over" cannot deliver understanding — defeated by changing sense (Berkeley's "egoism" means solipsism), by irony (Defoe, Hoadly), and by problems of sincerity (Hobbes, Bayle). What is needed instead:

> we need to grasp not merely what people are saying but also what they are doing in saying it.

The method: delineate "the full range of communications that could have been conventionally performed on the given occasion," then decode which the author was performing. Social context is *subordinate* — a "court of appeal" for adjudicating rival ascriptions of intention, not a determinant of meaning. See [[Concepts/Illocutionary Force and Historical Meaning (Skinner)]].

The consequence for the canon is uncompromising: of any concept, "there is only a history of its various uses, and of the varying intentions with which it was used," and "**the only histories of ideas to be written are histories of their uses in argument.**"

## Why It Matters

> there are no perennial problems in philosophy. There are only individual answers to individual questions

(via Collingwood). What history delivers is "not the essential sameness but rather the variety of viable moral assumptions" — and finally "a lesson in self-knowledge." Demanding that the classic texts teach us lessons is, Skinner says, "not merely a methodological fallacy but something like a moral error."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Frontal collision with [[Thinkers/Arthur O. Lovejoy]] and [[Sources/The Great Chain of Being - Arthur O. Lovejoy (1936)]]. Lovejoy's method — tracking a **unit-idea** across centuries and disciplines — is named by Skinner as the paradigm of the mythology of doctrines: ideas "get up to do battle on their own behalf." The vault has held Lovejoy's method approvingly since that ingest; it now has a first-rate opponent. Worth noting the strongest Lovejovian reply: that Skinner's constraint (nothing an agent could not recognise as a description of what they meant) makes long-range intellectual history nearly impossible to write, which is a cost, not a refutation.
- > [!warning] Near-ally with [[Thinkers/Herbert Butterfield]]: the mythology of prolepsis restates the Whig-interpretation critique in the vocabulary of intention rather than of moral judgement. The two should be read together.
- Note on the text: this revised version foregrounds the **textualist** orthodoxy (the text as self-sufficient). The 1969 essay's symmetrical attack on the *contextualist* orthodoxy — social context as the determinant of meaning — survives here only in compressed form, where context is demoted to "the ultimate framework" for deciding what could conventionally have been meant. Anyone citing the 1969 essay's two-front structure should check the original.
