# Cogito (Descartes)

> "I am, I exist" (*ego sum, ego existo*)—the first indubitable truth reached in the *Second Meditation* after hyperbolic doubt. Whenever it is uttered by me or conceived in my mind, it is necessarily true. Even if an evil demon deceives me about everything else (including the existence of my body, the external world, and mathematical truths), he cannot bring it about that I am nothing so long as I think I am something. This is the Archimedean point from which Descartes begins to rebuild knowledge.

## Core Statement and Context

After supposing that everything he sees is false, that memory is deceitful, that he has no senses, no body, no extension, no place—nothing remains certain? Perhaps only that nothing is certain. But then: is there not some God (or whatever) that puts these thoughts into his mind? Could he himself be the source? But he has already denied senses and body. Did he also convince himself that he did not exist? No: if he convinced himself of something, he certainly existed. Even if a supremely powerful and cunning deceiver is deliberately deceiving him all the time, "Beyond doubt then, I also exist, if he is deceiving me; and he can deceive me all he likes, but he will never bring it about that I should be nothing as long as I think I am something."

"Having weighed all these considerations sufficiently and more than sufficiently, I can finally decide that this proposition, 'I am, I exist', whenever it is uttered by me, or conceived in the mind, is necessarily true."

He does not yet know *what* this 'I' is that exists—only *that* it exists. The rest of the Second Meditation is devoted to determining its nature (a thinking thing).

The cogito is not an inference from "I think" as a premise to "I exist" as conclusion in the manner of a syllogism (Descartes clarifies this in the Replies). It is the immediate, self-evident certainty that arises in the very act of thinking/doubting/deceiving: the act of thought cannot be separated from an existing thinker. It is performative and first-personal.

## First Publication: the *Discourse* (1637)

The formula appears in print for the first time not in the *Meditations* (1641) but four years earlier in Part IV of the [[Sources/Discourse on the Method - Descartes (1637)]], in the French *je pense, donc je suis* — Latinized in the same text as "COGITO ERGO SUM." The *Discourse* version compresses the reasoning: while trying to suppose everything false, "it was absolutely necessary that I, who thus thought, should be somewhat; and... this truth, I think, therefore I am, was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt... could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it." He immediately concludes that the 'I' is "a substance whose whole essence or nature consists only in thinking," wholly distinct from body, and that it is "more easily known than the latter." Notably, the *Discourse* presents it in the compressed "*therefore*" (inference) form that critics later seized on — prompting Descartes's clarification in the *Meditations*/Replies that it is not a syllogism but an immediate performative certainty (below). The *Meditations*' famous alternative phrasing is "I am, I exist" (*ego sum, ego existo*), presented precisely to avoid the appearance of inference.

## What the 'I' Is (and Is Not)

The Second Meditation strips away everything dubitable. The 'I' is not the body (supposed not to exist), not "rational animal" (too many further questions), not a subtle wind/fire/air infused in limbs. What remains: thought itself. "I am therefore, speaking precisely, only a thinking thing, that is, a mind, or a soul, or an intellect, or a reason."

A thinking thing is one that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wishes, does not wish, and also imagines and perceives by the senses (even if the objects of imagination or sensation do not exist or are deceptive—the *power* or mode of thinking exists).

The wax example then shows that even bodies are known by the mind/intellect (clear and distinct perception of extension etc.), not the senses, reinforcing that mind is known more easily and certainly than body.

## Key Clarifications from the Text and Replies

- Not dependent on any particular content of thought or on the existence of body/senses: "this knowledge, in the precise sense in question here, does not depend on things of which I do not yet know whether they exist."
- Imagination is not required for this knowledge (imagining is contemplating the image of a bodily thing; one can know the self without it).
- The proposition is certain only "whenever it is uttered by me, or conceived in the mind"—it is tied to the act of thinking. (Later secured more broadly once God is known.)
- Not "I think, therefore I am" as a learned or syllogistic inference from a general premise "whatever thinks exists." Descartes denies he presupposes such a major premise in the way critics claimed.

## Husserl: the *cogito* is kept and stripped (2026-08-12)

[[Sources/Cartesian Meditations - Edmund Husserl]] keeps *ego sum* / *sum cogitans* as the first apodictic basis ("I doubt" presupposes "I am," §9) and refuses what Descartes immediately loads onto it. The Ego remaining in epoché "is not a piece of the world"; "I exist, *ego cogito*" no longer means "I, this man, exist" (§11). Adequacy and apodicticity need not coincide: only the living present is given with "strict adequacy"; past, abilities, habitualities are a presumptive horizon. Descartes treats the *cogito* as an axiom for a nomological science and rescues "a little tag-end of the world" (*substantia cogitans*) — the failure to make the transcendental turn (§10). Husserl will not *deduce* the world from the *cogito*; he will *constitute* it, and only as intersubjective. See [[Concepts/Phenomenological Reduction and Epoché (Husserl)]], [[Concepts/Transcendental Ego and Constitution (Husserl)]].

## Sartre: the *cogito* is kept and is not solitary (2026-08-12)

[[Sources/Existentialism is a Humanism - Jean-Paul Sartre (1946)]] takes the *cogito* as the only possible point of departure — "the absolute truth of consciousness as it attains to itself" — because without an absolute, the merely probable crumbles. He then denies Descartes's (and Kant's) solitude: "when we say 'I think' we are attaining to ourselves in the presence of the other, and we are just as certain of the other as we are of ourselves." The Other is "indispensable to my existence, and equally so to any knowledge I can have of myself." The world of decision is **inter-subjectivity**. See [[Concepts/The Look and the Other (Sartre)]]. This is a use of the *cogito*, not a reading of the *Meditations*: Sartre needs the first-personal absolute and will not pay Descartes's solipsistic price.

*Being and Nothingness* (Introduction, 2026-08-12) is harder on Descartes than the lecture. The Cartesian *cogito* is a *reflective* act whose object is an earlier, non-reflective consciousness of doubting; "I think, therefore I am" is really "I am aware that I doubt; therefore I am." The condition of that reflection is the [[Concepts/Pre-reflective Cogito (Sartre)|pre-reflective cogito]]. Cartesian rationalism's ontological error is then to treat the absolute defined by the primacy of existence over essence as a *substance* (*res cogitans*). BN's "ontological proof" from intentionality is "on the ground of being, not of knowledge" and "closer to Descartes than to Kant" in that respect — consciousness implies a transphenomenal being that is not it — but it is not Descartes's proof of the self as thinking thing. See [[Sources/Being and Nothingness - Jean-Paul Sartre (Barnes trans.)]].

## Schopenhauer: the reverse of “The world is my representation” (*Parerga* I, 1851)

The Sketch of the ideal and the real reads *dubito, cogito, ergo sum* as the reverse of Schopenhauer’s own opening sentence. Descartes stresses “the exclusive certainty of the subjective consciousness”; “The world is my representation” stresses “the mediacy of the object.” “Both propositions express the same thing from different angles.” The *cogito* uncovers the chasm between ideal and real; God as guarantor of the external world is “barely adequate.” This is not Descartes’s own claim about representation, and it is not Kant’s restriction of the *I think*. See [[Concepts/Doctrine of the Ideal and the Real (Schopenhauer)]], [[Sources/Parerga and Paralipomena Volume 1 - Schopenhauer (Cambridge 2014)]].

*WWR* II Ch. I: Descartes is father of modern philosophy because he made the subjective the starting-point. Ch. IV: *cogito, ergo sum* is an analytical judgement; its worth is that only self-consciousness is immediate. See [[Sources/The World as Will and Representation Volume 2 - Schopenhauer (Payne, Dover 2012)]].

## Kant's Restriction (first *Critique*, 2026-08-12)

The B Deduction grants the *I think* as the vehicle of all representations (B 131–132) and even that it contains "I exist," but denies Descartes's inference to a thinking *substance*. "I am conscious of myself not as I appear to myself, nor as I am in myself, but only that I am" (B 157). The Paralogisms: "I think is thus the sole text of rational psychology" and yields only "a transcendental subject of thoughts = *x*" (A 343 / B 401; A 346 / B 404). Rational psychology is a *discipline*, not a doctrine (B 421). See [[Concepts/Paralogisms of Pure Reason (Kant)]], [[Concepts/Transcendental Deduction and Unity of Apperception (Kant)]].

## Dehaene: cortico ergo sum (2026-08-12)

[[Sources/Consciousness and the Brain - Stanislas Dehaene (2014)]] quotes the Second Meditation's "thing which thinks" as the epigraph of the signatures chapter and reduces that thing to an ignited prefronto-parietal web. In the clinic, Ropper's joke — "I have brain activation, therefore I am" — is rejected: willful, sustained workspace activity (Owen tennis/navigation; local–global P3b) *is* operational proof of a thinking thing. The cogito becomes a third-person assay. See [[Concepts/Signatures of Consciousness and Ignition (Dehaene)]].

## Damasio: we are, and then we think (2026-08-12)

[[Sources/Descartes' Error - Antonio Damasio (1994)]] quotes the *Discourse* IV *je pense, donc je suis* and the following essence-of-thought paragraph, then reverses the order: "in the beginning it was being, and only later was it thinking." The *cogito* taken as celebrating the separation of thinking from organism is [[Concepts/Descartes' Error (Damasio)|the named error]]. This is a cultural reading of the slogan, not a reply to the performative/non-syllogistic clarification already on this page.

## Merleau-Ponty: spoken / tacit cogito (2026-08-12)

[[Sources/Phenomenology of Perception - Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1945)]] (Part Three ch. I) treats the *Meditations* as a cultural object and a second-hand *Cogito*: grasped "through the medium of language," its true formula would be "'One thinks, one is.'" The split of doubtful things vs. indubitable *pensée de voir* is "untenable" — the thought that one is seeing is only certain if actual vision is. Beneath the spoken act, a [[Concepts/Tacit Cogito (Merleau-Ponty)|tacit cogito]] has only a fleeting hold and is a *cogito* only when expressed. Not Sartre's pre-reflective cogito.
