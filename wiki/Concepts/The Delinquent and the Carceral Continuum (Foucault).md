# The Delinquent and the Carceral Continuum (Foucault)
> The prison does not fail to reduce crime; it succeeds in producing delinquency — a specified, supervised, usable illegality — and in remaining the concentrated node of a carceral continuum that already runs through school, hospital, factory, and charity.

## Summary

Part Four of *Discipline and Punish* answers the question Part Two left open: why the prison won. The prison *form* antedates the new codes. It was already constituted outside the law by disciplinary procedures for distributing, classifying, extracting time and force, and producing a centralized knowledge of bodies. Detention's "humanity" is the moment those mechanisms **colonized the legal institution**. Its stubborn self-evidence is double: juridico-economic (liberty as the equal good; time as a wages-form of "paying one's debt") and technico-disciplinary (it only intensifies barracks, school, workshop). From the outset it is both deprivation of liberty *and* technical transformation of individuals. Prison "reform" is contemporary with the prison, not a later recognition of failure.

As Baltard's **complete and austere institution**, the prison must be exhaustive, "omni-disciplinary," uninterrupted, and despotic. Three schemata fill the **carceral excess** over mere detention — isolation/hierarchy (cell), compulsory work (workshop), cure/normalization (hospital). That excess *is* the penitentiary. Auburn (common work + silence: "society itself reduced to its bare essentials") versus Philadelphia/Cherry Hill ("life annihilated and begun again") debate only *how* isolation is used; the shared aim is coercive individualization. Penal labour's use is "not profit; nor even the formation of a useful skill; but the constitution of a power relation." Duration is adjusted to the inmate's "useful transformation" (Lucas's "mobile classification of moralities"): a **Declaration of Carceral Independence** in which penitentiary judgement claims a share of punitive sovereignty.

The convict received from justice is replaced by the **delinquent** — a life, a biography, a "dangerous" individual, a typology by **norm** not law. "The penitentiary technique and the delinquent are in a sense twin brothers." "Delinquency is the vengeance of the prison on justice."

Critique of the prison (no drop in crime, recidivism, production of a delinquent milieu, post-release branding, ruined families) is already fixed by 1820–45 and still repeated. The reply is always reactivation of the same seven maxims (correction, classification, modulation, work, education, technical staff, aftercare). Prison + failure + reform are not three stages but one fourfold carceral system. **Reverse the problem**: "Is not the supposed failure part of the functioning of the prison?" Penality does not check illegalities; it **differentiates** them. The juridical opposition is legality vs illegal practice; the **strategic** opposition is **illegalities vs delinquency**. "Prison has succeeded extremely well in producing delinquency, a specific type, a politically or economically less dangerous — and, on occasion, usable — form of illegality" (prostitution, trafficking, *agents provocateurs*; the *18th Brumaire* cited for 1848). **Police–prison–delinquency** is an uninterrupted circuit: "Police surveillance provides the prison with offenders, which the prison transforms into delinquents, the targets and auxiliaries of police supervisions."

**The carceral.** Foucault would date completion not to the 1810 Code but to 22 January 1840, the opening of Mettray — or to the unrecorded day a dying child there said "What a pity I left the colony so soon." Mettray concentrates cloister, prison, school, regiment; its staff are "technicians of behaviour," "orthopaedists of individuality"; its school is "the first training college in pure discipline." Extra-penal incarceration was never abandoned (article 66 acquittals, paternal correction). A **carceral archipelago** (colonies, orphanages, factory-convents, charities, workers' estates) becomes a **carceral continuum**: a homogenized gradation from slight irregularity to offence. "The delinquent is an institutional product." "There is no outside."

Six effects (IV.3): (1) a continuous gradation from disorder to offence, anomaly replacing the sovereign's adversary; (2) recruitment of major delinquents by "disciplinary careers"; (3) the power to punish made natural — the continuum is the "technical and real" counterpart of the contract's "fiction" of a subject granting the right to punish; (4) a new form of law, the **norm**, and the proliferation of "judges of normality" (teacher-, doctor-, educator-, social-worker-judges); (5) the carceral as armature of the human sciences ("Knowable man … is the object-effect of this analytical investment"); (6) the prison's inertia — rooted not in a state apparatus but in a general network of disciplines.

The live political issue "is not even whether we should have prison or something other than prison" but "the steep rise in the use of these mechanisms of normalization." The book ends as "a historical background to various studies of the power of normalization and the formation of knowledge in modern society," and with the instruction: "we must hear the distant roar of battle."

## Key Claims

- The prison form is older than the codes that adopted it; "humanization" is colonization of law by discipline.
- Delinquency is not the worst illegality but a carceral product: enclosed, pathologized, supervised, made instrumentally useful.
- The prison can be altered and is not "once and for all indispensable." Foucault does not call for abolition. He refuses the success→failure→reform chronology.
- The carceral network "takes back with one hand what it seems to exclude with the other. … It is unwilling to waste even what it has decided to disqualify."

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: *The Eighteenth Brumaire* is cited for the political use of delinquents (1848 / Louis Napoleon). The class split of illegalities (property vs rights) is already argued in Part Two; here delinquency is the form in which popular illegalities are colonized and made usable.
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Hobbes]]**: the theory of the contract answers how a new foundation was given to the right to punish; Foucault asks the reverse — how people were made to *tolerate* being punished. The continuum is the material counterpart of that "chimerical granting."
- **[[Concepts/History of Madness (Foucault)]]**: extra-penal confinement, the hospital, paternal correction, and the widening carceral circles are the same apparatus that *Histoire de la folie* tracked as the Great Confinement, now homogenized through the prison.
- **[[Concepts/Specific Intellectual (Foucault)]]**: the 1972–74 prison revolts (and the GIP) are the present that still gets answered by the same 150-year maxims.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- If there is "no outside," what is the status of the "distant roar of battle"? Resistance is asserted, not given a location outside the network.
- Historians have questioned both the suddenness of the carceral system's "completion" at Mettray and the tightness of the police–delinquency circuit as a general European pattern. Foucault notes (I.1 n.3) that he studies only the French penal system.

> [!warning] Do not write that Foucault says "the prison doesn't work." He reports that early critique, then reverses it: the supposed failure is part of the functioning; the prison succeeds at producing a usable delinquency.
