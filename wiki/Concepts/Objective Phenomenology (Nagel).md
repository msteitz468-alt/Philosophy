# Objective Phenomenology (Nagel)

> Nagel's 1974 closing proposal: invent concepts that describe the subjective character of experience in a form comprehensible to beings who cannot have those experiences — a phenomenology that does not depend on empathy or imagination.

## Summary

After arguing that every available reduction leaves the [[Concepts/Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)|subjective character]] out, Nagel refuses both "physicalism is false" and "stop here." The next step is methodological. We are "completely unequipped to think about the subjective character of experience without relying on the imagination — without taking up the point of view of the experiential subject." That should be treated as a challenge: form new concepts and a new method.

An **objective phenomenology** would describe, at least in part, what experiences are like in terms that do not require the describer to occupy the relevant point of view. It would not capture everything. The first applications Nagel names:

- Bat sonar — the case that made the problem vivid.
- Human cases first, as a proving ground: concepts that could explain to a person blind from birth what it is like to see. One would "reach a blank wall eventually," but more should be sayable, and more precisely, than at present.
- **Structural features of perception** are the plausible target. Loose intermodal analogies ("Red is like the sound of a trumpet") are useless to anyone who has both heard a trumpet and seen red.

A side benefit: concepts alternative to those learned in the first person might yield a kind of understanding of *our own* experience "denied us by the very ease of description and lack of distance that subjective concepts afford." Aspects that admitted this description would then be better candidates for familiar kinds of physical explanation. Whether or not that guess is right, "it seems unlikely that any physical theory of mind can be contemplated until more thought has been given to the general problem of subjective and objective."

The 2024 preface repeats the point against a common misreading: the essay does *not* place consciousness beyond science. It places it beyond the physical sciences' model of an objective external reality based on shared observation, and asks for an expansion of scientific ideas.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]]** ([[Schools/Phenomenology]]): Husserl's phenomenology is first-personal and proceeds by reduction and eidetic variation. Nagel's proposal runs the other way — toward descriptions usable *without* occupying the point of view. They share the refusal to replace experience with a functional substitute; they disagree on method.
- **[[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]]**: [[Concepts/Heterophenomenology (Dennett)]] can look like a rival "objective phenomenology," but it is not. Dennett's method takes verbal reports as data for a theorist's fiction and remains neutral on whether there are intrinsic phenomenal properties. Nagel's method presupposes those properties and tries to describe them better. Heterophenomenology is what you do if you think Nagel's residue is unearned; objective phenomenology is what you do if you think it is the topic.
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: the later program of psychophysical laws, structural coherence, and organizational invariance is one way of trying to give experience an objective theoretical shape. Nagel 1974 would count that as progress only if the concepts still pick out subjective character rather than awareness.
- **[[Thinkers/Andy Clark]]**: predictive processing offers a third-person structural description of perception and affect. On Nagel's test, the question is whether those structures are descriptions *of the experience* or only of its functional envelope.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Can there be an "objective character of an experience" at all, apart from the point of view? Nagel poses this as the unanswered prior question. If the answer is no, objective phenomenology has a built-in ceiling.
- Note 8 concedes that imagination may take us farther across species than the essay's official line allows (blind people using click-sonar; a continuum of partial understanding). The official claim is not "we cannot *know* what it is like to be a bat" but "even to form a *conception* of it one must take up the bat's point of view."
