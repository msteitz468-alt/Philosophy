# Being-for-itself and Being-in-itself (pour-soi / en-soi) (Sartre)
> Two regions of being: the in-itself that is what it is (*massif*, uncreated, *de trop*), and the for-itself that is what it is not and is not what it is — a non-substantial absolute born supported by a being it is not.

## Summary

The Introduction to *Being and Nothingness* ("The Pursuit of Being," §VI) closes by assigning three marks to the being of the phenomenon and by naming the opposed region that consciousness is. These are not Welten's hurricane metaphor; they are Sartre's own formulae.

**Being-in-itself (*être-en-soi*).** "Being is. Being is in-itself. Being is what it is" (BN lxvi). (1) *Being is*: it is neither derived from the possible nor reduced to the necessary; necessity binds ideal propositions, not existents. Uncreated, without reason, without connection with another being, it is *de trop* for eternity. Creation *ex nihilo* cannot explain it: even if God had placed it outside subjectivity, "being-in-itself would be inexplicable in terms of creation; for it assumes its being beyond the creation" (BN lxiv). It is not *causa sui* (that is consciousness's manner). It is beyond active and passive, beyond affirmation and negation. (2) *Being is in-itself*: it does not refer to itself as self-consciousness does. "It is opaque to itself precisely because it is filled with itself." "The in-itself has nothing secret; it is solid (*massif*)" (BN lxvi). (3) *Being is what it is*: a regional synthetic principle, not the empty law of identity. Transition and becoming are forbidden; it knows no otherness; it is not subject to temporality. When it gives way, "the full positivity of being is re-formed" — it was, and other beings are.

**Being-for-itself (*être-pour-soi*).** Consciousness is "radically different" (BN lxiii). Its formula, announced here and unpacked in Part Two, is the inverse: "the being of for-itself is defined, on the contrary, as being what it is not and not being what it is" (BN lxv). Beings who "have to be what they are" make the in-itself's self-identity a *contingent* regional principle rather than an axiom. Consciousness is a plenum of existence but not a substance: "the ontological error of Cartesian rationalism is not to have seen that if the absolute is defined by the primacy of existence over essence, it can not be conceived as a substance" (BN lvi). It is a **non-substantial absolute** — "total emptiness (since the entire world is outside it)" (BN lvi).

**Neither realism nor idealism.** Non-positional self-consciousness shows that the being of the phenomenon cannot act upon consciousness (realism out). The spontaneity of the non-reflective cogito shows that consciousness cannot get out of an initially given subjectivity to constitute a transcendent being (idealism out). "We have barred all doors and … are now condemned to regard transcendent being and consciousness as two closed totalities without possible communication. It will be necessary to show that the problem allows a solution other than realism or idealism" (BN lxiv). That is the book's commission. The concrete is not either region alone but "man within the world" — "that specific union of man with the world which Heidegger, for example, calls 'being-in-the-world'" (BN 3). Consciousness and the phenomenon are abstractions (Laporte / Husserl: what cannot exist in isolation); the work begins from the question as a human attitude in order to recover the synthesis.

The later drama — the for-itself as lack, as desire to be in-itself-for-itself, as **useless passion** and missing God — is prepared here (consciousness "has to be what it is") and stated in the Conclusion, not in the Introduction. See [[Concepts/Value as Lack and the Impossible Synthesis (Sartre)]].

### From Welten (2020) and the *Critique*

Welten's biography supplied the hurricane image ("a hole in being … like the eye in the storm") and the claim that the distinction is dialectical rather than a two-substance dualism. The primary text supports the second claim more carefully than the first: the Introduction *does* call the regions "absolutely separated" and "without communication," then assigns the book the job of recovering their connection in the concrete. In the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* Welten maps pour-soi → "practical organism" and en-soi → "practical-inert field." That mapping is not in BN.

## Key Claims / Positions

- In-itself: being is; being is in-itself; being is what it is — *massif*, uncreated, *de trop*, beyond becoming.
- For-itself: being what it is not and not being what it is; existence implies essence; non-substantial absolute.
- Realism and idealism are both barred; the concrete is man-in-the-world.
- "Useless passion" / *ens causa sui* is the Conclusion's name for the failed synthesis, not the Introduction's definition of the two regions.

### How Sartre Differs From Prior Thinkers in the Wiki

- **vs [[Thinkers/Martin Heidegger]]**: shares the ontic-ontological passage and the name "being-in-the-world," but starts from two regions of being and from a *lack*, not from the question of the meaning of Being via Dasein. Heidegger's Dasein is "a being such that in its being, its being is in question"; Sartre completes the formula: consciousness is a being such that in its being, its being is in question *in so far as this being implies a being other than itself* (BN lxii).
- **vs [[Thinkers/Hegel]]**: the for-itself / in-itself vocabulary is Hegelian; the Introduction withholds the synthesis. The Conclusion will call the real an *abortive* effort at *causa sui*.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Descartes]]**: against *res cogitans* as thinking substance; the for-itself is not a thing with the property of thought.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Berkeley]] / phenomenalism**: reducing the object to its appearances does not reduce its *being* to its modes of being; *esse* is not *percipi*.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- The Introduction's "two closed totalities" sits in tension with Welten's "no point in speaking of the en-soi without the pour-soi." BN itself says the *phenomenon* of the in-itself is an abstraction without consciousness, "but its being is not an abstraction" (Conclusion, BN 622). Keep both sentences.
- The pour-soi's craving to be an en-soi is, by the Conclusion, never realisable — see [[Concepts/Value as Lack and the Impossible Synthesis (Sartre)]] and [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)]].
