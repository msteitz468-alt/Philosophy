# Teleological Suspension of the Ethical (Kierkegaard)
> Abraham oversteps the ethical altogether for a *telos* outside it. If that cannot happen, he is a murderer, not the father of faith.

## Summary

Problema I of *Fear and Trembling* asks whether there is a teleological suspension of the ethical. The ethical as such is the universal: it applies to everyone at every moment, rests immanently in itself, and is the *telos* of everything outside it. The single individual's ethical task is to abrogate particularity and express himself in the universal. To assert particularity against the universal is to sin; the way back is repentance.

If that is the highest that can be said, Hegel is right in "Good and Conscience": the individual seen merely as the single individual is a "moral form of evil" to be annulled in the teleology of ethical life. **Where Hegel goes wrong is in still talking about faith** and leaving Abraham the father of faith. He should be remitted to a lower court as a murderer.

**Faith is the paradox** that the single individual is higher than the universal — not as leftover evil, but having been in the universal and then, by means of the universal, standing as the particular in an absolute relation to the absolute. The position cannot be mediated (all mediation is by virtue of the universal). Or else faith has never existed just because it has always existed, and Abraham is done for.

**Tragic hero vs Abraham.** Agamemnon (Iphigenia, for the fleet), Jephthah (his daughter, for the vow that saved Israel), Brutus (his sons, for the laws of Rome) stay *inside* the ethical. They let one ethical expression (father–child) have its *telos* in a higher ethical expression (nation, vow, law). The whole people can be privy to the pain and the deed. There is no teleological suspension of the ethical itself.

Abraham oversteps the ethical altogether. He has a higher *telos* outside it. He does not save a nation, uphold the State, or appease angry gods. If the deity is angry, it can only be with Abraham. The act is a purely private undertaking. The only ethical expression in his life is that the father shall love the son; the future "universal" is latent in Isaac's loins and would cry out with Isaac's mouth. He does it for God's sake and for his own (to produce the proof) — a unity the tradition calls a trial. **The temptation is the ethical itself**, which would keep him from God's will.

He cannot be mediated, which means he cannot speak. The moment he speaks he expresses the universal, and then his situation is one of temptation. The beholder cannot rest an eye on him. One weeps for Agamemnon; one approaches Abraham with a *horror religiosus*. Do not judge by the outcome. If Isaac had been sacrificed Abraham would not be less justified. "That's to be judged by the outcome" is the lecturers' sentence, people with a permanent position a millennium from the convulsions of existence. The hero becomes a hero by beginning, not by the result.

Mary is the parallel: not great because favoured, great because of the distress, the fear, the paradox. The knight of faith dares to say even to the noble weeper: "Do not weep for me, but weep for yourself."

So either, in the time before the outcome, Abraham was a murderer every minute, or we stay with the paradox. A tragic hero can become a human being by his own strength; the knight of faith cannot. Faith is a marvel, and no human being is excluded from it, because that in which all human life is united is passion, and faith is a passion.

## Key Claims / Positions

- Suspension here does not forfeit the ethical; it preserves it in a higher *telos*. That is what "teleological" marks.
- There is no analogy that stays inside the ethical. Analogies that make "everything the same" miss Abraham.
- Paganism has no such private relation to the divine; for the tragic hero the ethical *is* the divine.
- The priest who praises Abraham on Sunday and would call a congregant a monster on Monday for doing likewise has not thought the thought through.

### How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Hegel (as Johannes reads him)**: *Sittlichkeit* is the identification of the individual with the totality of social life. "The State in and by itself is the ethical whole" (Hannay cites *Philosophy of Right*). Abraham fails every implication. See [[Contradictions/Kierkegaard vs Hegel - Faith vs Sittlichkeit]].
- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]**: not named in Problema I, but the ethical-as-universal is also the shape of duty as universal law. An act with no universalizable maxim, done because God commands it, is exactly what the *Groundwork* bars. See [[Contradictions/Kierkegaard vs Kant - Absolute Duty vs Universal Law]].
- **[[Thinkers/Jean-Paul Sartre]]**: uses Abraham as the model of legislating without proof. Johannes's point is almost the opposite: Abraham is *not* legislating for mankind; he does nothing for the universal. Sartre's commander who interprets a general order is a tragic hero (the order is already the universal).
- **[[Thinkers/Aristotle]]**: Johannes uses the *Poetics* (*peripeteia*, *anagnorisis*) in Problema III, not here. The tragic hero of Problema I is closer to Hegel's ethical life than to Aristotle's *hamartia*.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Conflicts with Hegelian *Sittlichkeit* — see [[Contradictions/Kierkegaard vs Hegel - Faith vs Sittlichkeit]].
- > [!warning] Conflicts with Kantian universal law — see [[Contradictions/Kierkegaard vs Kant - Absolute Duty vs Universal Law]].
- Hannay: the story need not be taken as a literal description of what faith requires; it can symbolize a religious attitude to value. Johannes does not say that. He says: let the judgement fall on Abraham as on anyone else if faith cannot make murder a holy deed.
