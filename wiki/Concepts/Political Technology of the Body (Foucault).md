# Political Technology of the Body (Foucault)
> Power's hold on the body is not only violence or ideology: it is a calculated mastery of forces — a "political anatomy" that produces the modern soul as the prison of the body.

## Summary

In *Discipline and Punish* I.1 Foucault relocates the history of punishment onto a **political economy of the body**. Even when modern penality claims to address only the "secret souls" of criminals, "it is always the body that is at issue — the body and its forces, their utility and their docility." Historians already wrote the body as demography and pathology; Foucault adds that it is "directly involved in a political field." As a force of production the body is invested with domination, but it becomes useful only if it is *both* productive *and* subjected. That subjection can be physical, calculated, and non-violent: "there may be a 'knowledge' of the body that is not exactly the science of its functioning, and a mastery of its forces that is more than the ability to conquer them." Knowledge plus mastery = the **political technology of the body**.

This technology is diffuse — "bits and pieces," rarely a systematic discourse — and cannot be localized in a state apparatus. What apparatuses operate is a **micro-physics of power** situated between great functionings and material bodies. Power here is strategy, not property: "exercised rather than possessed"; transmitted through the dominated; specific, not homologous with law or class; reversible at innumerable local points. The model is "a perpetual battle rather than a contract."

From that micro-physics follows the book's most famous inversion. Kantorowitz's doubled King's Body (surplus power) has its inverse in the condemned man's "least body" (coded lack of power). Surplus power exercised on the subjected body produces another duplication: a "non-corporal" soul. That soul is not a theological remnant or an illusion. "It exists, it has a reality, it is produced permanently around, on, within the body by the functioning of a power that is exercised on those punished — and, in a more general way, on those one supervises, trains and corrects." Psyche, subjectivity, personality, consciousness are concepts carved out of this reality-reference. "The soul is the effect and instrument of a political anatomy; the soul is the prison of the body."

Part Three, chapter 1 ("Docile bodies") is the home chapter of the technology in its classical-age form. The soldier is no longer recognized by natural signs; he is *made* from "formless clay." La Mettrie's *L'Homme-machine* names the junction of the anatomico-metaphysical body and the technico-political body: **docility**. "A body is docile that may be subjected, used, transformed and improved." **Disciplines** are the methods that impose a relation of docility-utility at the level of gesture, time, space, and movement. They increase the body's forces as economic utility and diminish them as political obedience. Four techniques compose this anatomy of detail: the art of distributions (enclosure, partitioning, functional sites, rank); the control of activity (timetable, temporal elaboration of the act, body-gesture correlation, body-object articulation, exhaustive use); the organization of geneses (exercise as a political technology of duration); the composition of forces (tactics: bodies combined into a machine whose effect exceeds the sum of parts). Beside the jurists' contract there is a military dream of society: "not to the general will but to automatic docility."

## Key Claims

- Leniency is a technique of power, not a moral climate: it makes man, the soul, and the normal/abnormal duplicate crime as objects of intervention and knowledge.
- Discipline is distinct from slavery, service, vassalage, and monastic asceticism: it aims at utility, not appropriation or renunciation.
- "If economic exploitation separates the force and the product of labour, … disciplinary coercion establishes in the body the constricting link between an increased aptitude and an increased domination."
- Exercise, once an element in this technology, "does not culminate in a beyond, but tends towards a subjection that has never reached its limit."
- The man we are invited to free is already "the effect of a subjection much more profound than himself."

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: Foucault quotes *Capital* I on cooperation and military tactics, and accepts that the body is invested as labour-power. He adds a prior operation: discipline *links* increased aptitude to increased domination *inside* the body. Exploitation separates force from product; discipline makes the force itself usable. At large-scale industry one finds, "beneath the division of the production process, the individualizing fragmentation of labour power."
- **[[Thinkers/Descartes]]**: the anatomico-metaphysical register (intelligible body) is one half of the classical discovery; the other half is the army/school/hospital's technico-political body. La Mettrie joins them.
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Hobbes]]** / contract theory: the fundamental reference of discipline is "not to the state of nature" and "not to the primal social contract" but to "permanent coercions" and "automatic docility."
- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]]**: the genealogical frame (no origin, only a technology that produces the soul it then claims to discover) is Nietzschean; the book does not name him in I.1.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- The "soul is the prison of the body" inverts the Christian/Platonic picture ([[Thinkers/Plato]], [[Thinkers/Augustine]], [[Thinkers/Descartes]]) in which the body imprisons a prior soul. Foucault denies that the soul is an illusion — which blocks a simple materialist dismissal.
- Late Foucault's [[Concepts/Ethics of the Self and Care of the Self (Foucault)|ethics of the self]] looks for a practice not entirely determined by this technology; *Discipline and Punish* itself gives little room for it.

### From *The History of Sexuality* I (1976)

The political technology of the body is now the first pole of **bio-power**: an *anatomo-politics of the human body* (body as machine) paired with a *bio-politics of the population* (species body). Foucault refuses a "history of mentalities" that would take account of bodies only through how they have been perceived; he wants a "history of bodies" and of "what is most material and most vital in them" (p. 152). Sex is the *pivot* of the two poles — access both to the disciplined body and to the regulated species (p. 146). The "soul is the prison of the body" from 1975 is not withdrawn; it is resituated as one face of a technology whose other face is the calculated management of life.

> [!warning] Conflicts with [[Thinkers/Thomas Hobbes]] on the model of power: contract and sovereign appropriation vs. capillary strategy. The carceral continuum is later offered as the "technical and real" counterpart of the contract's "fiction" of a subject granting the right to punish — see [[Concepts/The Delinquent and the Carceral Continuum (Foucault)]].
