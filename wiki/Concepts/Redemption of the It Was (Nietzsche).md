# Redemption of the It Was (Nietzsche)
> To redeem the past is not to forgive it. It is to turn every "It was" into "Thus would I have it."

## Summary

Part II, "Redemption" (Common 1909), is the book's other load-bearing invention besides the Superman. Zarathustra walks among cripples and fragments — inverted cripples, "inverse cripples," great ears with too little man attached. Men are fragments and limbs on a battlefield. The soothsayer of the previous discourse has already sung: "All is empty, all is alike, all hath been!"

Then the formula:

"To redeem what is past, and to transform every 'It was' into 'Thus would I have it!'—that only do I call redemption!"

The Will emancipates — "Willing emancipateth" was already the Happy Isles. But the Will is a prisoner of the past. It cannot break time's stone. **Revenge** is named, not as a social passion only (that was the [[Concepts/The Last Man (Nietzsche)|tarantulas]]' equality), but as metaphysics: **"This, yea, this alone is REVENGE itself: the Will's antipathy to time, and its 'It was.'"** From that antipathy come penalty, eternal guilt, the madness of "eternally recurring deed and guilt."

The creating Will must learn to **will backwards**. Zarathustra names [[Concepts/Will to Power (Nietzsche)|Will to Power]] as what must do "something higher than all reconciliation" — and **breaks off in terror**. The Stillest Hour then says he *knows* it and will not speak. The thought he will not speak is the one Part III must bite: [[Concepts/Eternal Recurrence (Nietzsche)|eternal return]].

This is why recurrence in *Zarathustra* is not a bolt-on cosmology. Unless the "It was" can be willed, return is only the soothsayer's sameness or the dwarf's cheap circle. Redemption is the ethical task that makes the later "Once more" possible.

## Key Claims

- Redemption = "It was" → "Thus would I have it / Thus do I will it."
- Revenge = the Will's antipathy to time and its "It was" (not only ressentiment against persons).
- Creating Will must will backwards — named, then silenced.
- Foreshadow, not yet the teaching: "eternally recurring deed and guilt" appears here as *revenge-theology*, the unredeemed twin of return.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]]** — the hinge of Part II. Tarantulas: "FOR MAN TO BE REDEEMED FROM REVENGE—that is for me the bridge to the highest hope." Redemption then says what revenge *is*.
- **[[Concepts/Bad Conscience (Nietzsche)]] / [[Concepts/Ressentiment (Nietzsche)]]** — *Genealogy* will historicize guilt and imaginary revenge. This page is earlier and more formal: revenge against *time*.
- **[[Concepts/Amor Fati (Love of Fate)]]** — GS §276 is a vow to see the necessary as beautiful. The phrase is **absent** here. The weld (will the past, love eternity) is real in the corpus and in Part III's Seven Seals; it is not how this discourse names the task.
- **[[Concepts/Eternal Return as Selective Ontology (Deleuze-Nietzsche)]]** — Deleuze's first selection (eliminate half-willing and the "little poisons of revenge") is closest to this page. His second selection (reactive forces do not return) is not.

## 1888: Nietzsche names this the life-task (*Ecce Homo*)

The *Zarathustra* self-review closes on this discourse, and it is the strongest evidence in the vault that Nietzsche regarded it — not eternal return, not the Superman — as the book's practical demand. §8 quotes it back:

> "To redeem all the past and to transform every 'it was' into 'thus would I have it' — **that alone would be my salvation**!"

He then supplies the consequence the 1885 text left implicit: man is "**not a subject for love nor yet for pity**" but raw stone for the sculptor, and "The command '**become hard!**', the deep conviction that all creators are hard, is the really distinctive sign of a **Dionysian** nature." Re-willing the past is thus filed under hardness rather than reconciliation — which is the point most likely to be softened when this doctrine is compared to Stoic acceptance or to Holiday's "fuel." See [[Sources/Ecce Homo - Nietzsche (Collection 2013)]], [[Concepts/Amor Fati (Love of Fate)]].

> [!note] The same chapter's §6 poses the psychological problem this discourse answers: how "the most affirmative of all spirits" can also contradict "with every word that he utters," having thought the abysmal thought without taking it as an objection to existence. *Amor fati* is absent from that chapter (verified); the redemption formula does its work.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Do not read Christian redemption into the word. No Saviour, no wiping of guilt. The Will *re-wills* the past.
- > [!warning] The discourse breaks off. Do not write Part III's animals' hurdy-gurdy back into this silence as if Zarathustra had already taught return.
