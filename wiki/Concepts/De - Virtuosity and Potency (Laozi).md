# De — Virtuosity and Potency (Laozi)

> In Ziporyn's *Daodejing*, *de* is usually **virtuosity** (skill attained by practicing a course) and, when modified, **power** or **potency**. The text ironically reverses it: true *de* is not acquired by study but is what things already do well without effort — heartbeat, digestion, the "life" of them — "ironic virtuosity attained from the ironic course."

## The ironic reversal

Ziporyn: the simplest relation of *dao* to *de* in the primary (pre-ironic) sense is that of a course of study to its result. Take a course in archery, attain virtuosity in archery. Ancient lexicons gloss *de* by the homonym "attainment." In Confucian usage *de* is ethical virtue plus the charisma that can order a state without punishments.

The *Daodejing* keeps the attainment-talk and empties the practice: this virtuosity is "the result not of purposive activity, but of nonpurposive process, of nondoing." Because it is not one's own effort, it feels like a gift from the course; because it was always already there, the attainment is a nonattainment. Hence also "power," "potency," "the life force."

The binome *xuande* (chs. 10, 51, 65) is double-translated: "the obscure power of remaining obscure" / "the unseen power, the power of the unseen."

## Load-bearing claims in the text

- **Ch. 10.** Birthing and rearing that "never possesses what it produces, never expecting anything from its deeds, growing things but never controlling them — the obscure power of remaining obscure."
- **Ch. 21.** Those of "grand powers of virtuosity" follow only the course; the course itself is "confusion, pure indistinction," yet an image and a "life-giving kernel" appear in the dimness.
- **Ch. 23.** Those who endeavor on the course merge with the course; those who gain virtuosity merge with virtuosity; those who lose merge with the loss — and there is "joy in the gain of the loss."
- **Ch. 38 (the general statement of the *de* half).** "The highest virtuosity is not grasped as virtuosity. That is why it has such virtuosity. Lower virtuosity never loses its 'virtuosity.' That is why it so lacks virtuosity. Highest virtuosity *does* nothing, for it harbors no motives or purposes. Lower virtuosity is something done — always with some motive or purpose." Then the descent: higher humankindness (done, no specific motive) → higher duty (done, specific motive) → higher ritual (done, and when others fail to respond, "out comes the muscle to coerce compliance"). "Virtuosity appears when the course is lost. Humankindness appears when virtuosity is lost. Duty appears when humankindness is lost. Ritual propriety appears when duty is lost. Ritual propriety is the thinning out of loyalty and trust, the beginning of disorder."
- **Ch. 49.** The sage has "no intentions of his own." "I regard the good as good, but I also regard the not-good as good. For virtuosity is to be good at finding them all good." Same for trust / the untrustworthy.
- **Ch. 51.** "Whatever the course generates is reared and raised by virtuosity… The honor they give to the course, the esteem they give to virtuosity, happen of their own accord without being imposed. … Producing them but not possessing them, making them but not requiring anything of them, growing them but not controlling them. Such is their obscure power, the power of their obscurity."
- **Ch. 54–55.** Virtuosity cultivated in the self becomes genuine, then ubiquitous through family, community, nation, world. Deep virtuosity is like a newborn — soft, potent, harmonious. Augmenting life, or the mind controlling vital energy, is "ominous," "strong-arming," "going off course."
- **Ch. 79.** Having virtuosity, one's concern is the agreement; lacking it, the payment. Sages as creditors "demand nothing from the other party."

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Vs. Confucian *de*** (now primary, Chin): Ziporyn's Note flagged *Analects* 2.1 and 2.3. Chin: "To rule by virtue is like the way the North Star rules" (2.1); statutes yield evasion without shame, *de* + *li* yield shame and self-reform (2.3). Some Han readers take 2.1 as "doing nothing"; others as doing with "a semblance of doing nothing." Ch. 38 keeps the "effortless" and discards the humankindness/duty/ritual stack as a decay series. Confucius keeps the stack: without *ren*, rites are useless (3.3); returning to rites *is* the way to *ren* (12.1). See [[Concepts/Ren - Humaneness (Confucius)]], [[Contradictions/Confucius vs Laozi - Humaneness and Rites vs Course Lost]].
- **Vs. [[Concepts/Eudaimonia]] / Aristotelian virtue**: Aristotle's virtues are hexeis acquired by habituation, crowned by *theōria*. Laozi's highest *de* is precisely *not* acquired and not an activity; lower *de* that "never loses its 'virtuosity'" is the counterfeit.
- **Vs. [[Concepts/Duty (Kant)]]**: Kantian moral worth requires acting *from* duty, i.e. from a motive. Ch. 38's highest virtuosity "harbors no motives or purposes"; duty is two steps down the decay ladder.
- **Vs. [[Concepts/Conatus (Spinoza)]]**: both name an intrinsic power of persisting. Spinoza's *conatus* is the essence of each thing as it strives to persevere. Laozi's *de* rears what the course generates and *refuses* to control it; striving ("strong-arming" the life in oneself, ch. 55) is how one goes off course.
- **Vs. Nietzschean virtue / mastery**: Nietzsche's noble *de* (in the ordinary sense of excellence) is pathos of distance and self-legislation. Laozi's *de* finds the not-good good (ch. 49) and prefers rocks to jade (ch. 39).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Ch. 38 can be read as a historical decline (golden age → ritualized late Zhou) or as a structural ranking (any time you can point to your virtuosity, you have already lost it). The text does not decide.
- "Foreknowledge is the ostentatious flowering of the course, the beginning of foolishness" (ch. 38) sits against [[Concepts/Foreknowledge and the Use of Spies (Sun Tzu)]], where foreknowledge is *the* enabler of victory. Same Chinese cultural world; opposite valuation of knowing in advance.
