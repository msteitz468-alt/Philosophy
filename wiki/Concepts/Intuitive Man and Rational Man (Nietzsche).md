# Intuitive Man and Rational Man (Nietzsche)
> The metaphor-drive cannot be killed by concepts. It escapes into myth and art.

## Summary

Part 2 of *On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense* (1873; Breazeale) is not an appendix to the definition of truth. Once language and science have built “this great columbarium of concepts, the graveyard of perceptions,” the **drive toward the formation of metaphors** — “the fundamental human drive, which one cannot for a single instant dispense with in thought, for one would thereby dispense with man himself” — is only imprisoned, not vanquished. It “seeks a new realm and another channel” and finds it in *myth* and in *art* generally. It confuses the conceptual cells, wanting a world “as colorful, irregular, lacking in results and coherence, charming, and eternally new as the world of dreams.” Art tears the web of concepts; the waking man then thinks he is dreaming. Pascal’s repeated dream (workman/king) is endorsed. The waking life of a mythically inspired people — “the ancient Greeks, for instance” — “more closely resembles a dream than it does the waking world of a scientifically disenchanted thinker.”

So long as it deceives *without injuring*, the intellect is free and “celebrates its Saturnalia.” The free intellect smashes the framework of concepts, “pairing the most alien things and separating the closest,” and is guided by intuitions. “There exists no word for these intuitions; when man sees them he grows dumb, or else he speaks only in forbidden metaphors.”

Then the typology. “There are ages in which the **rational man** and the **intuitive man** stand side by side, the one in fear of intuition, the other with scorn for abstraction. The latter is just as irrational as the former is inartistic.” Both want to rule life: the first by foresight, prudence, regularity; the second by disregarding need and, as an “overjoyed hero,” counting as real “only that life which has been disguised as illusion and beauty.” When the intuitive man wins (Greece), “art’s mastery over life can be established”: house, gait, clothes, clay jugs do not look invented from pressing need; “Olympian cloudlessness,” “a playing with seriousness.”

The conceptual man “only succeeds by such means in warding off misfortune, without ever gaining any happiness for himself from these abstractions.” The intuitive man reaps “illumination, cheer, and redemption” — and “suffers more intensely, when he suffers”; he “keeps falling over and over again into the same ditch.”

The closing figure is the **stoical man**, who “learns from experience and governs himself by concepts.” He “seeks nothing but sincerity, truth, freedom from deception” — and in misfortune “executes a masterpiece of deception”: a mask with “dignified, symmetrical features,” no cry, no change of voice. “When a real storm cloud thunders above him, he wraps himself in his cloak, and with slow steps he walks from beneath it.”

Do not rename this pair Apollo/Dionysus. Those names are not in this essay. Kin to *The Birth of Tragedy*’s artist vs Socratic man, not identity. Do not file the stoical man as a reading of [[Thinkers/Epictetus]] or [[Thinkers/Marcus Aurelius]].

## Key Claims

- Metaphor-drive is fundamental; concepts are its own products used as a prison.
- Myth and art are the drive’s escape, not ornaments on science.
- Rational man / intuitive man: fear of intuition vs scorn for abstraction; both want to rule life.
- Greek culture as possible victory of the intuitive type: art’s mastery over life.
- Conceptual life wards off pain and does not yield happiness.
- The stoic’s sincerity in misfortune is itself a masterpiece of deception.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]] (1873)** — unpublished continuation of the 1872 artist-metaphysics into an epistemology of types.
- **[[Concepts/Tragedy from the Spirit of Music (Nietzsche)]] / [[Concepts/Socratism and the Theoretical Man (Nietzsche)]].** BT: Dionysian chorus vs theoretical man. 1873: intuitive vs rational, without those names. The stoic-mask is new here.
- **[[Concepts/The Great Health (Nietzsche)]].** 1887 Saturnalia of a convalescent is a later weather. 1873 Saturnalia is the intellect freed to deceive without injuring. Same word, different job.
- **[[Thinkers/Blaise Pascal]]** — the king/workman dream is quoted. Pascal is not a vault thinker from this citation alone.
- **Stoics in the vault.** The 1873 “stoical man” is a type of *conceptual* self-government whose masterpiece is a mask. It is not a reading of the *Enchiridion* or the *Meditations*. Flag against [[Concepts/Dichotomy of Control (Epictetus)]] and Holiday’s *amor fati*: do not feed this mask to those pages as their source.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Part 2 can be read as still preferring the intuitive / Greek type (as BT prefers tragedy). It can also be read as diagnosing both types (the intuitive is “just as irrational”). Do not close that from this short text.
- > [!warning] “Deceive without injuring” is the condition of the intellect’s Saturnalia. It is not the 1888 *décadence* diagnosis and not a utilitarian harm principle.
