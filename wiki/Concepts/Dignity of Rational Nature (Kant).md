# Dignity of Rational Nature (Kant)

> "In the kingdom of ends everything has either a *price* or a *dignity*. What has a price can be replaced by something else as its *equivalent*; what on the other hand is raised above all price and therefore admits of no equivalent has a dignity." (4:434–435)

## Summary

In §II of the [[Sources/Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals - Immanuel Kant (Gregor, Cambridge 1998)|*Groundwork*]] Kant distinguishes two kinds of worth. Whatever answers to needs or taste has a **price** (*Preis*) — a **market price** if it serves inclinations, a **fancy price** if it answers mere taste without need — and can be replaced by an equivalent. What "admits of no equivalent" has **dignity** (*Würde*).

"Morality, and humanity insofar as it is capable of morality, is that which alone has dignity" (4:435). The ground is not a special feeling for persons, nor their usefulness, nor happiness: "**Autonomy** is therefore the ground of the dignity of human nature and of every rational nature" (4:436). A rational being has dignity because it can give universal law through its maxims and so is fit to be a member of a possible [[Concepts/Kingdom of Ends (Kant)|kingdom of ends]]. Korsgaard quotes the same stretch: dignity is "nothing less than the share it affords a rational being in the giving of universal laws" (4:435).

This is the worth-theoretic companion of the [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)|Formula of Humanity]]. Persons are ends in themselves *because* they have dignity, not price. Things (*Sachen*) have price and may be used as mere means. Treating a person as a mere means is treating what has dignity as if it had only a price.

The same stretch explains the *sublimity* of duty. What is sublime is not mere subjection to the law but the agent's status as *lawgiving* member (4:440). Respect (*Achtung*) is the consciousness of this worth; it is "self-wrought by means of a rational concept," not a received feeling (4:401n).

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]**: dignity is *Würde*, incomparable inner worth, grounded in autonomy. It is not a natural right derived from creation or contract, and not a rank among talents. It belongs to every rational nature as such.
- **[[Thinkers/John Stuart Mill]]**: persons are counted in the [[Concepts/Greatest Happiness Principle (Mill)|greatest-happiness]] calculus as bearers of pleasure and pain. Kant's price/dignity cut is the explicit refusal of that reduction: happiness has (at most) a price; humanity capable of morality does not. See [[Contradictions/Utilitarian Consequentialism vs. Kantian Deontology]].
- **[[Thinkers/Aristotle]]**: honor (*timē*) and the magnificent/magnanimous man mark *comparative* worth inside the polis. Kantian dignity is not comparative and does not wait on recognition.
- **[[Thinkers/Locke]]**: natural rights attach to persons as God's workmanship and as self-owners. Kant relocates the same non-instrumental status in the will's capacity to legislate, without theological workmanship.
- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche]]**: "Tartuffery of old Kant" and the attack on the "Good in Itself" read dignity as a priestly/egalitarian fiction. The kingdom of ends is, for Nietzsche, Platonism for the people.
- **[[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]]**: treats the categorical imperative as the *Grundnorm* of Kantian ethics and criticizes Kant for excluding love and compassion from moral grounding — a pressure on whether dignity can be purely legislative.
- **Christine Korsgaard** (this edition's introduction): dignity = legislative membership. The constructivist gloss — we *confer* value by taking ourselves to be important in pursuing ends — is her frame, not a separate Kant text.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] If dignity is grounded only in *capacity* for morality, the status of children, the severely impaired, and non-rational animals is underspecified in the *Groundwork*. The 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals* is where Kant later sorts duties regarding them; that book is not yet in the vault.
- Formula-equivalence (Korsgaard n.10): if FH and autonomy are *stronger* than FUL, dignity may be doing more work than the universal-law test can support.
- Price is not *immoral* — talents, gifts of fortune, and happiness are good *with* a good will (4:393–394). Dignity does not abolish relative value; it ranks it.
