# Epiphenomenal Qualia (Jackson)

> Certain properties of certain mental states — the hurtfulness of pain, the look of red — can be such that their presence or absence makes **no difference to the physical world**. That is a "perfectly possible" view, and the usual reasons for refusing it have no real force.

## Summary

§IV of [[Sources/Epiphenomenal Qualia - Frank Jackson (1982)]] ("The Bogey of Epiphenomenalism") is not a further proof that qualia exist. The [[Arguments/Knowledge Argument (Jackson)|knowledge argument]] is supposed to have shown that. The section answers the objection that stops people from *admitting* qualia: they would have to cause things in the brain, "and it is hard to do this without sounding like someone who believes in fairies."

Jackson's thesis is narrower than the classical epiphenomenalist package:

1. He does **not** claim that mental *states* are inefficacious with respect to the physical world — only that certain *properties* of them (qualia) may be.
2. He does **not** claim that the mental is totally causally inefficacious. "It may be that you have to hold that the instantiation of qualia makes a difference to other mental states though not to anything physical." Awareness of qualia "suggest[s] such a position."

So the view is: brain events cause both behaviour and qualia; qualia do not cause anything physical; they may cause other mental states; the subject is aware of them.

Three standard reasons for insisting that (e.g.) the hurtfulness of pain must sometimes make a difference in the brain:

**(i) It is just obvious** that hurtfulness is partly responsible for avoiding pain and saying "It hurts." Reply: "to reverse Hume, anything can fail to cause anything." Regular succession, however obvious, can be overturned by an over-arching theory on which A and B are distinct effects of a common cause. On the screen, Lee Marvin's fist "causes" John Wayne's head to move; the projector and the film are the common cause. The epiphenomenalist says the same of hurtfulness and behaviour: certain brain happenings cause both.

**(ii) Darwin.** Qualia evolved (we have them; the earliest forms of life do not); traits that evolve are conducive to physical survival; they could hardly help us survive if they do nothing to the physical world. Reply: polar bears have thick, warm coats, which are conducive to survival in the Arctic, and therefore also *heavy* coats, which slow the animal down and are not conducive to survival. Evolution is not refuted. "All we can extract from Darwin's theory is that we should expect any evolved characteristic to be either conducive to survival or a by-product of one that is so conducive." Qualia are in the latter category: by-products of brain processes that *are* highly conducive to survival.

**(iii) Other minds.** We infer other minds from behaviour. How can behaviour be evidence of qualia unless it is their outcome? Man Friday's footprint was evidence of Man Friday because footprints are causal outcomes of feet. Reply: reading in *The Times* that Spurs won is excellent evidence that *The Telegraph* has also reported the win, even though (one trusts) *The Telegraph* does not get the results from *The Times*. Argue from one effect back to a common cause and out again to another effect. The epiphenomenalist allows that qualia are effects of what goes on in the brain; so one can argue from others' behaviour back to their brains and out again to their qualia. The chain is more dubious than the newspaper case — other minds is a major problem, other newspaper reports is not — but there is "no special problem of Epiphenomenalism as opposed to, say, Interactionism here."

The leftover complaint is granted: they are an "excrescence. They do nothing, they explain nothing, they serve merely to soothe the intuitions of dualists, and it is left a total mystery how they fit into the world view of science." That is "perfectly true; but is no objection to qualia." It rests on "an overly optimistic view of the human animal." We understand what we need to survive. Epiphenomenal qualia are "totally irrelevant to survival." At no stage did selection favour those who could make sense of how they are caused, the laws governing them, "or in fact why they exist at all. And that is why we can't." Physicalism is "an extremely optimistic view of our powers": if it is true, then "in principle we have it all." The antecedent probability that everything in the Universe is of a kind relevant to the survival of *Homo sapiens* is "very low surely."

The **sea-slugist** parable: intelligent slugs on the ocean floor whose successful restricted sciences tempt their "tough-minded slugists" to hold that those terms suffice for everything. They correctly note that no slugist has ever spelled out how the mysterious residue fits the science. Super-beings might stand to us as we stand to the slugs. "We cannot adopt the perspective of these super beings, because we are not them, but the possibility of such a perspective is, I think, an antidote to excessive optimism."

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Frank Jackson]]**: the 1982 defence. The 1986 sequel does not re-argue it; it only needs leftover *truths*, not a causal story.
- **[[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]]** ([[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]]): distinguishes Huxley's weak sense (nonfunctional by-products — "trivially physicalist") from a strong sense in which qualia are undetectable even by the subject's introspective machinery, then runs the **parity / gremlins** argument against the strong sense. Jackson's actual view is neither: qualia are not "trivially physicalist" (the knowledge argument has already denied that), and they are *not* undetectable by the subject — awareness of them is why he allows mental-to-mental efficacy. Dennett's incoherence charge therefore has to be retargeted: at a property that the subject knows and that may cause other mental states, but that never makes a physical difference, including to the utterance "I see red."
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: treats epiphenomenalism as the cost of combining causal closure with the failure of logical supervenience — the [[Concepts/Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment (Chalmers)]]. He calls the view "epiphenomenalistic to a first approximation" and looks for a Russellian escape (phenomenal or protophenomenal properties as the intrinsic natures physics is silent about) rather than Jackson's evolutionary shrug. Huxley is named as the historical precedent.
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]]** (2023): the [[Concepts/Psychophysical Nexus and Neutral Monism (Nagel)|hoped-for monism]] is explicitly **hostage** to consciousness doing causal work. If a closed physical story of behaviour is available, "property dualism plus epiphenomenal psychophysical laws" becomes equally live — Jackson's combination. Nagel will not take that option; Jackson's §IV is written to make it livable.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Dennett's parity argument vs Jackson's common-cause story: if the report "I have qualia" is caused entirely by the brain, what makes the quale the thing the report is *about*? Jackson answers other-minds (the *Times*/*Telegraph* pattern) and grants mystery about the how and why. Dennett treats that mystery as evidence the posit is empty. See [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]].
- > [!warning] Nagel 2023 vs Jackson 1982 on whether leftover properties may be idle. Nagel needs them not to be; Jackson argues they may.
- Open in this source: how awareness of a physically inefficacious property is itself to be understood, if awareness is (in part) a physical or functional state. Jackson flags the issue ("general considerations … suggest" mental-to-mental efficacy) and does not solve it.
