# Rhetorical Redescription and Paradiastole (Skinner)

> Chapter 10, the *Retrospect*: the technique the later Skinner puts at the centre of conceptual change. **Paradiastole** — redescribing an action with a "neighbouring" term, so that prodigality becomes liberality and recklessness becomes courage. This chapter is also where Skinner says most plainly what he now thinks he got wrong.

## The Technique

Recovered from the classical rhetoricians — Quintilian (*Institutio* IV.ii, VIII.vi), Cicero (*De Partitione*), Aristotle (*Rhetoric* II). The device exploits the *neighbourliness* of vices and virtues:

> prodigality must be more leniently redescribed as liberality, avarice as carefulness, negligence as simplicity of mind (Quintilian)

> slander can pass for frankness, recklessness for courage, extravagance for copiousness (Aristotle, via Quintilian)

The move works in both directions: a virtue can be denigrated as its neighbouring vice just as readily as a vice can be excused as its neighbouring virtue.

## Applications

- **Machiavelli**, *Il Principe* 16–17: liberality redescribed as *suntuosità*, clemency as *troppa pietà*, Scipio's indulgence as *natura facile*.
- **Castiglione's *sprezzatura***, and Hoby's 1561 English rendering of it as "recklessness" — a redescription visible in the act of translation.
- **[[Thinkers/Nietzsche|Nietzsche]]**, *Genealogy of Morals* I, read as an unrecognised exercise in *paradiastole*: slave morality redescribing what the strong called vices as virtues, and vice versa. Skinner's claim is that Nietzsche was doing rhetoric without naming it.
- **[[Thinkers/Ian Hacking|Hacking]]** on child abuse: "inventing new descriptions, providing new ways to see old acts."

## What Skinner Concedes

The chapter is unusually explicit about revision, and the concessions are load-bearing for anyone using his earlier work.

**On his own chapter 4.** He now regards chapters 8–9 as "an advance (or at least a change of mind)" over the 1969 manifesto, where he had wrongly assumed that morals and politics possess "distinct and relatively stable vocabularies" — the assumption underwriting Weldon's *Vocabulary of Politics* and Hare's *Language of Morals*.

**On stable meanings.** In the early 1970s he assumed "for every evaluative term there will at any one time be a standard and accepted meaning and use," picturing the innovating ideologist as manipulating that standard by sleights of hand. After immersion in the classical rhetoricians:

> it makes little sense to speak of evaluative terms as having accepted denotations… all attempts to legislate about the 'correct' use of normative terms must be regarded as equally ideological in character.

**On the "almost paradoxical" status of his own thesis.** Strictly, these are not changes in concepts but "transformations in the applications of the terms by which our concepts are expressed" — indeed "if the disputants are genuinely arguing, they must have the same concept." Yet the long-run effect is genuine conceptual change: the concept acquires "new prominence and a new salience."

**On Time.** He accepts Palonen's charge that he neglects Time as internal to the meaning of concepts: "I do indeed neglect this possibility, but only because I cannot make sense of it."

## Against Unit Ideas — and With Koselleck

The chapter restates the anti-Lovejoy thesis in its final form:

> there cannot be a history of unit ideas as such, but only a history of the various uses to which they have been put by different agents at different times… their history is the only history of ideas to be written

And "there is nothing lying beneath or behind such uses."

On **Begriffsgeschichte**, Skinner corrects a persistent misreading: he never aimed to discredit Koselleck's programme, having simply had "no knowledge of Koselleck's research programme" in the 1960s–70s, learning of it through Melvin Richter. Both treat concepts as "tools and weapons of ideological debate"; both are influenced by Foucault. The genuine difference is **tempo and scale** — Koselleck's *longue durée* against Skinner's "pointillist study of sudden conceptual shifts." Skinner pleads a lack of talent for social history and a suspicion (with Dunn) of theories in which Time is an agent. He ends by accepting Palonen's placement of his own work inside Koselleck's larger one:

> Koselleck is interested in nothing less than the entire process of conceptual change: I am chiefly interested in one of the techniques by which it takes place.

He also declares an allegiance: to a **Nietzsche–Weber** tradition in which concepts afford only changing perspectives, and against neo-Kantian and ideal-speech-situation attempts "to halt the flux of politics."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] **Read this chapter before citing chapter 4.** [[Concepts/Mythologies in the History of Ideas (Skinner)]] rests on an assumption of stable normative vocabularies that Skinner here withdraws. The mythologies survive as diagnoses; the picture of a fixed lexicon being manipulated does not.
- > [!warning] Frontal restatement against [[Thinkers/Arthur O. Lovejoy]] and [[Sources/The Great Chain of Being - Arthur O. Lovejoy (1936)]]: "there is nothing lying beneath or behind such uses." The vault now holds both the unit-idea method and its most developed refutation.
- > [!warning] Sits interestingly beside [[Concepts/Perspectivism (Nietzsche)]] and [[Concepts/Belief in Antitheses of Values (Nietzsche)]] — Skinner claims the *Genealogy*'s central operation is a rhetorical technique with a classical name, which makes Nietzsche's transvaluation a special case of something Renaissance humanists practised deliberately.
- Open: whether "same concept, different applications" is a stable position. Skinner himself calls it "almost paradoxical," and it is the pressure point where critics (and Koselleck's followers) push hardest.
