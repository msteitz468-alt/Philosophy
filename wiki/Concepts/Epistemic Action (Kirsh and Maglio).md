# Epistemic Action (Kirsh and Maglio)
> Some actions change the world in order to change what we can know — and those actions are already part of thinking.

## Summary

Clark and Chalmers import the term **epistemic action** from David Kirsh and Paul Maglio, "On distinguishing epistemic from pragmatic action," *Cognitive Science* 18 (1994): 513–49. It is the empirical hinge of §2 of [[Sources/The Extended Mind - Andy Clark and David Chalmers (1998)]].

In Tetris, falling shapes must be slotted into an emerging structure. A rotation button is available. Kirsh and Maglio time a physical 90° rotation at about 100 milliseconds, plus about 200 milliseconds to select the button; the same result by mental rotation takes about 1000 milliseconds. More importantly, physical rotation is used **not just to position** a shape but **to determine whether shape and slot are compatible**. That second use is epistemic.

**Definition (as Clark and Chalmers report it):**

- **Epistemic actions** "alter the world so as to aid and augment cognitive processes such as recognition and search."
- **Pragmatic actions**, by contrast, "alter the world because some physical change is desirable for its own sake" (cement into a hole in a dam).

The philosophical move is that epistemic action "demands spread of epistemic credit." If rotating the piece on screen is doing the work mental rotation would do, the [[Concepts/Parity Principle (Clark and Chalmers)|parity principle]] counts the button-press as part of the cognitive process. The same pattern covers pen-and-paper long multiplication, Scrabble-tile rearrangement (Kirsh 1995), and the nautical slide rule (Hutchins 1995). In each case "the re-arrangement… is not part of action; it is part of thought."

## Later Use in the Vault

Clark's *Experience Machine* (2023) keeps the Kirsh–Maglio Tetris study and Feynman's "You have to work on paper" as paradigm epistemic actions, and claims [[Concepts/Predictive Processing (Clark)|predictive processing]] unifies them with practical action under one quantity: minimizing expected future prediction error. That unification is **not** in the 1998 paper. In 1998 the distinction is borrowed, not derived.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Kirsh and Maglio (1994)**: the empirical distinction, from Tetris expertise.
- **[[Thinkers/Andy Clark]] and [[Thinkers/David Chalmers]] (1998)**: epistemic credit must spread; this is the on-ramp to extended cognition.
- **[[Thinkers/Andy Clark]] (2023)**: same examples, now as what a prediction machine is "ideally poised to discover."
- **[[Thinkers/Hubert Dreyfus]]**: would recognize the phenomenon as skilled coping with equipment, but would not automatically reclassify the action as a *mental state*. See [[Concepts/Skilled Coping and the Five Stages of Expertise (Dreyfus)]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- The distinction assumes we can tell when a world-change is "for knowledge" rather than "for its own sake." Many actions (rotating a piece *and* dropping it) do both. Clark and Chalmers need only that *some* uses are epistemic.
- Open in 1998: no account of *why* brains recruit such actions. That is the "recruitment puzzle" Clark later says the original paper lacked.
