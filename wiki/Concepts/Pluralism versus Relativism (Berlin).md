# Pluralism versus Relativism (Berlin)

> Many objective ends, some incompatible, inside a finite human range — **not** “I prefer coffee, you prefer champagne.” Berlin’s load-bearing distinction, aimed first at readings of Vico and Herder, then at himself.

## Summary

[[Thinkers/Isaiah Berlin]] spends “The Pursuit of the Ideal” and especially “Alleged Relativism in Eighteenth-Century European Thought” (1980) refusing a collapse he says he once “perpetrated” himself (Momigliano’s charge; Berlin recants at p. 80).

**Relativism** (of values): a judgement is “simply what it is, with no objective correlate which determines its truth or falsehood.” Taste-difference. Cultures as “impenetrable bubble” (Spengler). Nothing more to be said.

**Pluralism:** “There are many objective ends, ultimate values, some incompatible with others.” They can be sought by men still “fully rational, fully men.” Communication is possible only because “what makes men human is common.” We can imaginatively **enter** other outlooks; we are not required to **accept** them. Vico can damn Homeric brutality; Herder can denounce Alexander.

> “I repeat, pluralism – the incommensurability and, at times, incompatibility of objective ends – is not relativism.”

True modern relativism of ends is mainly a **nineteenth-century** legacy (Romantic irrationalism, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, anthropology, Marx, Freud, Pareto, Mannheim). Eighteenth-century *philosophes* are not relativists of ends (Montesquieu, Hume, Diderot, Locke vary *means*). Marginal exceptions: Sade, Deschamps.

## The Human Horizon

Berlin’s working test: a finite variety of intelligible human ends. Beyond communication we speak of **derangement** or **incomplete humanity**. “We are called upon to exercise our imaginative powers to the utmost; but not to go beyond them; not to accept as authentic values anything that we cannot understand, imaginatively ‘enter’ into.” Outlooks we can enter vs behaviour we can only describe “physicalistically.”

The later slogan **human horizon** is used in the second-edition appendix (McKinney reply; letter to Polanowska-Sygulska). In the 1980 essay the working phrases are **limits of humanity** and **enter**. Herder is quoted on Nature restricting man to a horizon — Berlin treats that closed-circle remark as **exceptional**, not Herder’s general view.

## What They Are Not Relativist About

Vico and Herder are **historicists** (Meinecke/Troeltsch/Croce sense: thought intelligible only in context) **not** Popper’s historicism, and **not** ideology-determinism. They are **monists about facts**: “there is only one truth, not many.” *Certum* vs *verum*; fable is poetically not literally true. Ideology / false consciousness waits for Hegel and the early Marx.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Giambattista Vico]] / [[Thinkers/Johann Gottfried Herder]]:** Berlin’s type-cases. Not Spengler, not Westermarck. “What men have made, other men can understand.”
- **[[Concepts/Value Pluralism (Berlin)]]:** the positive doctrine. This page is the *distinction* that keeps it from collapsing into taste.
- **[[Thinkers/William James]] / [[Concepts/The One and the Many (James)]]:** metaphysical pluralism (the world is many) is a cousin, not this distinction. James sides against the Absolute; Berlin sides against a single moral pattern.
- **[[Thinkers/Paul Feyerabend]] / [[Concepts/Proliferation and Tenacity (Feyerabend)]]:** theoretical pluralism in science, not value pluralism.
- **[[Thinkers/Ludwig Wittgenstein]]:** family resemblance of cultures is used in the Polanowska-Sygulska letter (appendix), not in the 1980 essay.
- **John Gray** (named in the 2013 reception, not here): pluralism undercuts any unique liberal ranking. Berlin in these essays still defends a liberal *equilibrium* without claiming a deduction from pluralism to liberalism.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Incommensurability does **not** entail relativism in Berlin’s usage. Do not import Kuhn/Feyerabend “incommensurable paradigms” as if they settled this page.
- > [!warning] Essay 3 still has Herder hoping for harmony without conflict; essay 4 treats him as a pluralist of uncombinable peaks. Keep the tension.
- Does pluralism *entail* liberalism? Crowder (outside this book) says yes; Gray says no. This book states the distinction and the politics of equilibrium; it does not close that debate.
