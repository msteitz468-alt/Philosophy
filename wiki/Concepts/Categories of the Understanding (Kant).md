# Categories of the Understanding (Kant)

> Twelve pure concepts of an object in general, derived from the logical functions of judgment. Without corresponding intuition they are empty; without them, intuitions are blind.

## Summary

Cognition needs both stems: sensibility (receptivity / intuitions) and understanding (spontaneity / concepts). "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (A 51 / B 75). **General logic** abstracts from all content and treats only the form of thinking. **Transcendental logic** concerns the origin and a priori objective validity of pure concepts of objects (A 55–57 / B 80–82). Used as a mere organon, general logic becomes dialectic (logic of illusion). The transcendental *analytic* is a "logic of truth"; the *dialectic* critiques hyperphysical misuse of the categories beyond experience (A 57–64 / B 82–88).

The understanding is a discursive faculty of concepts, hence of **judging**: "the understanding in general can be represented as a faculty for judging" (A 69 / B 94). The same function that unites representations in a judgment unites the synthesis of a manifold in an intuition (A 79 / B 104–105). This "clue" (named the **metaphysical deduction** at B 159) yields twelve **categories**:

| Heading | Categories |
|---|---|
| **Quantity** | Unity, Plurality, Totality |
| **Quality** | Reality, Negation, Limitation |
| **Relation** | Inherence and Subsistence (*substantia et accidens*); Causality and Dependence (cause and effect); Community (reciprocity) |
| **Modality** | Possibility–Impossibility; Existence–Non-existence; Necessity–Contingency |

The first two classes are mathematical; the last two dynamical. The third in each class is the combination of the first two, yet still an ancestral concept (B §11). Kant "deliberately spare[s]" definitions here (A 82–83 / B 108–109). Official definition: categories are "concepts of an object in general, by means of which its intuition is regarded as determined with regard to one of the logical functions for judgments" (B 128).

That we *have* these concepts a priori does not yet show they apply to objects of experience. That is the job of the [[Concepts/Transcendental Deduction and Unity of Apperception (Kant)|transcendental deduction]]. That they apply *determinately* to spatiotemporal objects is the job of [[Concepts/Schematism and Principles of Pure Understanding (Kant)|schematism and the principles]]. Without schemata "the categories are only functions of the understanding for concepts, but do not represent any object" (A 147 / B 186–187). Ontology "must give way to… a mere analytic of pure understanding" (A 247 / B 303).

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Aristotle.** Kant takes the name and the ambition of a complete table of fundamental concepts, but derives his from *judgment-forms*, not from a survey of being.
- **Locke.** Locke's "physiology" of the understanding is *quaestio facti* (how we came by the concepts). Kant's question is *quid juris*: by what right they apply to objects (A 84–87 / B 116–119).
- **Hume.** Causation cannot be abstracted from custom: necessity and strict universality are marks of the a priori (A 90–92 / B 122–124). The category is the rule of synthesis that makes an objective sequence possible.
- **Hegel.** The table is a fixed formal inventory; Hegel's logic will treat categories as a self-developing sequence.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The claim that there are *exactly* twelve logical functions and twelve corresponding categories has been controversial since 1781 (Guyer/Wood). The vault records the table as Kant states it.
- This is the theoretical half of the vault's Kant: the *Groundwork* then asks how a *will* can be a law to itself. The categories legislate for *nature*; the categorical imperative legislates for *freedom*.
