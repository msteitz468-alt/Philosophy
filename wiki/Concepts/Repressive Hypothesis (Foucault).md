# Repressive Hypothesis (Foucault)
> The familiar story that modernity silenced sex, and that speaking it is liberation, is not denied as a set of local facts — it is denied the right to be the constitutive principle of a history of sexuality.

## Summary

Part One of *La Volonté de savoir* (1976; Hurley, *The History of Sexuality* I) names and reconstructs what Foucault terms the **repressive hypothesis**. "For a long time, the story goes, we supported a Victorian regime, and we continue to be dominated by it even today" (p. 3). After a relatively frank seventeenth century, the bourgeoisie confined sex to the home and the procreative couple; silence became the rule; the parents' bedroom was the only acknowledged locus. What the story *attributes* to repression (as distinct from penal prohibition) is a fourfold operation: "a sentence to disappear… an injunction to silence, an affirmation of nonexistence, and, by implication, an admission that there was nothing to say about such things, nothing to see, and nothing to know" (p. 4). Illegitimate sexualities are conceded only as coded, insular places of profit — brothel and asylum, Steven Marcus's "other Victorians" (p. 4).

The story is easy to uphold because it is aligned with capitalism: repression is dated to the seventeenth century so as to coincide with the bourgeois order, and is explained by a **work-imperative** — "if sex is so rigorously repressed, this is because it is incompatible with a general and intensive work imperative" (p. 6). A further motive is the **speaker's benefit**: if sex is condemned to prohibition, nonexistence, and silence, "the mere fact that one is speaking about it has the appearance of a deliberate transgression" (p. 6). Statement of oppression and sermon-form reinforce each other: "Today it is sex that serves as a support for the ancient form… of preaching. A great sexual sermon" (p. 7). Freud's couch and "the obvious timidity underlying Reich's vehemence" (p. 5) are the story's own cautious first lights.

Foucault's question is not "Why are we repressed?" but "Why do we say, with so much passion and so much resentment… that we are repressed?" (pp. 8–9). Three doubts:

1. **Historical.** Is seventeenth-century sexual repression an established fact?
2. **Historico-theoretical.** Do power's workings belong primarily to prohibition, censorship, and denial?
3. **Historico-political.** Is the critique of repression a rupture, or "part of the same historical network as the thing it denounces" (p. 10)?

The aim is not the symmetrical counter-claim of unchanging liberty. "I do not maintain that the prohibition of sex is a ruse; but it is a ruse to make prohibition into the basic and constitutive element from which one would be able to write the history of what has been said concerning sex" (p. 12). Negative elements have a local and tactical role inside a mechanism of incitement, implantation, and a will to knowledge.

Part Two then supplies the counter-history. At the level of authorized vocabulary there is a "restrictive economy"; at the level of discourses, "a veritable discursive explosion" (p. 17). The **confession of the flesh** expands after Trent (pp. 18–21); sex becomes a "police" matter of ordered maximization, not taboo (pp. 23–25); **population** emerges with sex at its heart (p. 25); children's sex is put under a new *regime* of discourses, not consigned to silence (pp. 27–30). Chapter 2 rejects the objection that all this talk served only to expel non-reproductive sex: the nineteenth century is an age of *multiplication* — specification of individuals, "the homosexual was now a species" (p. 43), spirals of power and pleasure, devices of sexual saturation. Closing verdict: "We must therefore abandon the hypothesis that modern industrial societies ushered in an age of increased sexual repression" (p. 49).

Part Four relocates both "liberation" (power as external hold on desire) and "you are always-already trapped" (law constitutive of desire) inside the same **juridico-discursive** representation of power (pp. 82–83). Reich's interwar antirepressive critique is "a tactical shift and reversal in the great deployment of sexuality," not a grid for its history or dismantling (p. 131). The book's last sentence: "The irony of this deployment is in having us believe that our 'liberation' is in the balance" (p. 159).

## Key Claims

- Prohibition, censorship, and denial exist; they are not the constitutive principle of the modern history of sex.
- The speaker's benefit recruits the critic of repression as an agent of the very incitement the hypothesis cannot see.
- The capitalist/work-imperative version of the hypothesis (sex repressed because incompatible with labor-capacity) is the story's political guarantee, not Foucault's explanation. Part Four explicitly abandons that "diffuse energetics" (p. 114).
- Antirepressive struggle (Reich) is a tactical shift *inside* the deployment, not an exit from it.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Freud / Reich** (no thinker pages): named as the story's cautious first lights (p. 5) and, later, as relays. Freud placed sex "at one of the critical points marked out for it since the eighteenth century" and gave "a new impetus to the secular injunction to study sex and transform it into discourse" (p. 159). Reich's "sexual revolution" is internal to the deployment (p. 131).
- **[[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: the hypothesis's *capitalist* version is stated in Part One as what makes the discourse easy to uphold, then refused in IV.4 as the wrong object (labor-capacity energetics; *Spätkapitalismus* / "hyper-repressive desublimation").
- **[[Thinkers/Jean-Paul Sartre]]**: Sartre vs Freud is a fight over lucidity vs the unconscious. Foucault's third pole: the question is not whether repression is real in the psyche but whether "repression" is the right name for how power works. See [[Contradictions/Sartre vs Freud - Lucidity vs the Unconscious]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Foucault does not deny that sex has been "prohibited or barred or masked" (p. 12). Readers who take the book as a simple inversion ("we were never repressed") miss the third doubt: the critique of repression may be part of the same network.
- The later ethics of the self ([[Concepts/Ethics of the Self and Care of the Self (Foucault)]]) looks for a practice not exhausted by this analysis; Volume 1 itself ends at "bodies and pleasures," not at *souci de soi*.

> [!warning] Conflicts with the vault's Marx-adjacent picture of ideology as *hiding* a truth that liberation would speak. For Foucault, the will to speak the truth of sex is an instrument of the deployment, not an escape from it. See [[Thinkers/Karl Marx]], [[Concepts/Power-Knowledge (Foucault)]].
