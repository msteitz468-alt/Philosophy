# Explaining Beliefs Without Truth (Skinner)

> Chapter 3 of [[Sources/Visions of Politics Volume 1 - Regarding Method - Quentin Skinner (2002)|*Regarding Method*]]: "**the concept of truth is irrelevant to the enterprise of explaining beliefs**." What the historian must ask is not whether a belief was true but whether it was rational for that agent, by the standards then prevailing, to hold it true.

## The Argument

Skinner does not ask historians to suspend judgement. Noticing that a belief strikes us as false is a *good* place to start: bizarre beliefs "cry out to be explained." What he denies is that the truth-value belongs in the explanation.

The target is the widespread assumption that false beliefs indicate failures of reasoning, so that establishing truth or falsity is the first step in explaining why a belief was held:

> It is I think nothing less than fatal to good historical practice to introduce the question of truth into social explanation in this way.

The replacement question concerns **rationality**, deliberately deflationary and non-algorithmic: a rational belief is one reached "by some accredited process of reasoning" under the prevailing norms of epistemic rationality. "A rational agent will thus be someone who… believes what he or she ought to believe." Verifiability and falsifiability both fail as criteria.

Crucially, rationality is not self-explanatory. Against Hollis, Skinner insists there remains a gap between showing a belief rational and explaining why it was held: "the attainment of rationality will always be an achievement," and achievements need causal explanation.

## Charity, and Its Limits

Skinner accepts Davidson's charity as a strategy for *identifying subject-matter*, not as a requirement that beliefs about that subject-matter be mostly true — one can identify Bodin's beliefs about witches while judging every one of them false. Against Davidson's over-application by Macdonald and Pettit, he insists truth and falsity do not determine what *kind* of explanation is owed.

Three working precepts follow:
1. Assume a convention of truthfulness (Lewis).
2. Take utterances at face value — against Durkheimian symbolic readings and Freudian displacement readings. His objection is sharp: "the only criterion we are offered for distinguishing those propositions we are to take literally from those we are to take symbolically is our own cognitive discomfort."
3. Recover the network of beliefs that made the utterance rational to hold true.

But charity is not "boundless." His two case studies cut both ways: Ladurie on Languedoc witchcraft is criticised at length for pre-judging irrationality and thereby foreclosing the available explanation (inference from biblical premises); while Machiavelli's reports of the battles of Anghiari and Molinella are a case where the historian *must* judge a belief less than rational.

He accepts Bloor's **impartiality** requirement — all beliefs get causal explanation, and reasons can be causes — while rejecting the **symmetry** requirement, because "the causes of someone's following what are taken to be the relevant norms of reasoning will nevertheless be of a different order from the causes of their violating them."

## Holism and Translation

Against Hollis's "bridgehead" of shared beliefs, Skinner argues holistically: concepts mediate perception, so translatability is not a condition of intelligibility. The historian's job is to learn the alien terms — and, following [[Thinkers/Ian Hacking|Hacking]], the alien **styles of reasoning** — rather than to find a common core.

> our concepts are not forced upon us by the world, but represent what we bring to the world in order to understand it

This licenses a rule about vocabulary: revising the subjects' own terms is legitimate when *explaining* or narrating (Danto's later descriptions; a superior explanation may use terms unavailable to the agent), but generally fatal when *identifying* beliefs. Machiavelli had no *diritti* or *interessi*; "to revise those terms will be to talk about a different set of beliefs."

## The Relativism Charge

Skinner's defence is precise about what is being relativised:

> I am not in general talking about truth; I am talking about what different peoples at different times may have had good reasons by their lights for holding true.

What is relativised is *holding true*, not truth. He adds two arguments: conceptual relativism is self-refuting, and intellectual history presupposes shared norms of consistency — following Quine, without non-contradiction, contradictory beliefs would simply be unreportable.

The chapter ends on a note of deliberate modesty: "To look for complete intelligibility is to adopt an unduly optimistic view of what we can hope to bring back from the foreign lands of the past."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Instructive contrast with [[Concepts/Space of Reasons (Sellars)]]. Both Skinner and [[Thinkers/Wilfrid Sellars]] insist that a normative status cannot be read off a bare description, and both make justification relative to a framework of other commitments. But Sellars is doing epistemology — what makes a claim *justified* — while Skinner is doing historical explanation — why an agent *held* it. Skinner's "rational acceptability, not truth" and Sellars's placement of knowings in the space of reasons are cousins arriving from opposite directions.
- > [!warning] Bears on [[Concepts/Incommensurability (Kuhn)]] and [[Concepts/Observation as a Skill (Hacking)]]. Skinner defends [[Thinkers/Thomas Kuhn]] as "widely, if mistakenly" read as a relativist and borrows Hacking's styles of reasoning — placing the vault's philosophy-of-science material and its history-of-ideas material on the same footing about theory-laden perception.
- > [!warning] Against the vault's Popper material: Skinner explicitly rejects both verifiability and falsifiability as criteria of rational belief-holding — not as criteria of *science* (which is Popper's claim in [[Concepts/Falsifiability as Criterion of Demarcation (Popper)]]) but as standards for judging historical agents. The two claims are compatible, but the vault should not let Popper's demarcation criterion migrate into historical explanation.
