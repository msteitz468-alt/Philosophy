# Myth of the Given (Sellars)

> The empiricist demand that some episodes be **both** epistemically independent of everything else known **and** capable of justifying the rest of empirical knowledge. Sellars's claim is that nothing can be both — and that this, not sense-data specifically, is the framework error running through "both 'dogmatic rationalism' and 'skeptical empiricism.'"

## What the Myth Is Not

Sellars opens by fencing off the obvious misreading:

> I presume that no philosopher who has attacked the philosophical idea of givenness or, to use the Hegelian term, **immediacy**, has intended to deny that there is a difference between *inferring* that something is the case and, for example, *seeing* it to be the case.

If "the given" meant merely what is observed as being observed, "the existence of 'data' would be as non-controversial as the existence of philosophical perplexities." It does not. "The phrase 'the given' as a piece of professional — epistemological — shoptalk carries a substantial theoretical commitment."

Nor is the Myth simply the belief in inner episodes. "Many who attack the idea of the given seem to have thought that the central mistake embedded in this idea is exactly the idea that there *are* inner episodes, whether thoughts or so-called 'immediate experiences,' to which each of us has privileged access. I shall argue that this is just not so, and that the Myth of the Given can be dispelled without resorting to the crude verificationisms or operationalisms characteristic of the more dogmatic forms of recent empiricism." Nor is it identical with foundationalism, though that is "the most widespread form of the Myth": "it is far from constituting its essence. Everything hinges on *why* these philosophers reject it."

## The Scope of the Framework

The Given is a *framework*, not a doctrine about one kind of item. "Many things have been said to be 'given': sense contents, material objects, universals, propositions, real connections, first principles, even givenness itself." Sellars stresses that attacking sense-data is only the entry point — "if, however, I begin my argument with an attack on sense-datum theories, it is only as a first step in a general critique of the entire framework of givenness." Philosophers who reject sense-data on the ground that language-learning is a public process governed by public sanctions are immune to one form of the myth but "have no defense against the myth in the form of the givenness of such facts as that *physical object x looks red to person S at time t*." (Hence [[Concepts/The Logic of Looks (Sellars)]].)

The framework has been so pervasive that "few, if any, philosophers have been altogether free of it; certainly not Kant, and, I would argue, not even Hegel, that great foe of 'immediacy.'"

## The Core Argument (§§32–38)

Sellars states the foundationalist thesis precisely: there is "a structure of particular matter of fact such that (a) each fact can not only be non-inferentially known to be the case, but presupposes no other knowledge either of particular matter of fact, or of general truths; and (b) such that the non-inferential knowledge of facts belonging to this structure constitutes the ultimate court of appeals for all factual claims — particular and general — about the world."

The internal tension: "Knowledge pertaining to this level is non-inferential, yet it is, after all, knowledge. It is ultimate, yet it has authority." Statements at this level "must not only be *made*, but, so to speak, must be worthy of being made, *credible*… For where there is no connection between the making of a statement and authority, the assertion may express *conviction*, but it can scarcely be said to express *knowledge*." And that authority cannot come from other statements, "for in that case all knowledge pertaining to this level would have to be inferential."

Sellars sets up two "ultimate modes of credibility": the intrinsic credibility of analytic sentence *types* (flowing from type to token), and the credibility of tokens that "express observations," which "flows from tokens to types." The question is how the second works.

**The dilemma.** If observation reports are construed as *actions* whose correctness is the correctness of an action, and their authority as a matter of "following a rule" in the proper sense:

> then we are face to face with givenness in its most straightforward form. For these stipulations commit one to the idea that the authority of *Konstatierungen* rests on nonverbal episodes of awareness — awareness *that* something is the case, e.g. that this is green — which nonverbal episodes have an intrinsic authority (they are, so to speak, 'self-authenticating') which the verbal performances (the *Konstatierungen*) properly performed 'express.'

The image Sellars gives this: "These self-authenticating episodes would constitute **the tortoise on which stands the elephant on which rests the edifice of empirical knowledge**." He notes the view is indifferent to whether the self-authenticating item is "the awareness that a certain sense content is green" or "the awareness that a certain physical object looks to someone to be green" — the second escapes sense-data and keeps the myth.

**The alternative and its two hurdles.** Suppose instead a token of "This is green" is a *reliable symptom* of the presence of a green object — a thermometer-like "acquired causal characteristic of the language user." Two hurdles:

1. A report expresses *knowledge* only if it has authority. On this account the only candidate authority is that one can *infer* the presence of a green object from the fact that someone makes the report.
2. Decisively: that authority "must not only be *possessed* but be *recognized* by the person whose report it is." Hence "not only must it be a symptom or sign of the presence of a green object in standard conditions, but **the perceiver must know** that tokens of 'This is green' are symptoms of the presence of green objects in conditions which are standard for visual perception."

Sellars accepts this apparently absurd consequence: "one couldn't have observational knowledge of *any* fact unless one knew *many other things* as well."

**The regress objection and the answer.** Doesn't observational knowledge at *t* then presuppose knowledge of the form "X is a reliable symptom of Y," which presupposes prior observational knowledge, and so on? The objection "rests on too simple, indeed a radically mistaken, conception of what one is saying about Jones when one says that he *knows* that *p*":

> The essential point is that in characterizing an episode or a state as that of *knowing*, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in **the logical space of reasons**, of justifying and being able to justify what one says. (§36)

See [[Concepts/Space of Reasons (Sellars)]]. The regress dissolves because what is required is only that it be correct *now* to say that those earlier utterances were reliable indicators — not that anyone knew it *then*. "It does not require that any episode in this prior time be characterizeable as expressing knowledge."

## The Verdict (§38)

> The idea that observation "strictly and properly so-called" is constituted by certain self-authenticating nonverbal episodes, the authority of which is transmitted to verbal and quasi-verbal performances when these performances are made "in conformity with the semantical rules of the language," is, of course, the heart of the Myth of the Given. For the given, in epistemological tradition, is what is taken by these self-authenticating episodes. These "of the given" could be, e.g., sense contents, material objects, universals, propositions, real connections, first principles, even givenness itself. **They are the unmoved movers of empirical knowledge**, the "knowings in presence" which are presupposed by all other knowledge, both the knowledge of general truths and the knowledge "in absence" of other particular matters of fact.

## What Replaces It

Sellars is emphatic that he is *not* saying empirical knowledge lacks a foundation in every sense — "to put it this way is to suggest that it is really 'empirical knowledge so-called,' and to put it in a box with rumors and hoaxes. There is clearly *some* point to the picture of human knowledge as resting on a level of propositions — observation reports — which do not rest on other propositions in the same way as other propositions rest on them."

What he denies is the *one-directionality*: "the metaphor of 'foundation' is misleading in that it keeps us from seeing that if there is a logical dimension in which other empirical propositions rest on observation reports, there is another logical dimension in which the latter rest on the former."

The famous conclusion (§38, p. 79):

> Above all, the picture is misleading because of its static character. One seems forced to choose between the picture of an elephant which rests on a tortoise (What supports the tortoise?) and the picture of a great Hegelian serpent of knowledge with its tail in its mouth (Where does it begin?). Neither will do. For **empirical knowledge, like its sophisticated extension, science, is rational, not because it has a *foundation* but because it is a self-correcting enterprise which can put *any* claim in jeopardy, though not *all* at once.**

## How Other Vault Thinkers Bear On This

- **[[Thinkers/W.V.O. Quine]]** — the complementary demolition. Rorty's division of labor: Quine's [[Concepts/Analytic-Synthetic Distinction (Quine)|"Two Dogmas"]] "helped destroy the distinction between analytic and synthetic truths," while EPM "helped destroy the empiricist form of foundationalism by attacking the distinction between what is 'given to the mind' and what is 'added by the mind.'" Sellars's "self-correcting enterprise… though not *all* at once" is a near-relative of [[Concepts/Confirmation Holism (Duhem-Quine Thesis)|confirmation holism]] — but note the difference: Quine's holism is about *confirmation* across a web of belief; Sellars's point is about the *normative status* of a knowing.
- **[[Thinkers/Karl Popper]]** — [[Concepts/Basic Statements and the Empirical Basis (Popper)]] reaches a strikingly similar verdict from philosophy of science: basic statements are accepted by *decision*, are intersubjectively testable, and there is no incorrigible given. [[Concepts/No Authoritative Sources of Knowledge (Popper)]] is the same denial of an "ultimate court of appeals." The two arrive independently and by different routes — Popper via the logic of testing, Sellars via the logic of *knows*.
- **[[Thinkers/Bertrand Russell]]** — the primary casualty. See [[Arguments/Inconsistent Triad in Sense-Datum Theories (Sellars)]].
- **[[Thinkers/A.J. Ayer]]** — [[Sources/Language, Truth and Logic - A.J. Ayer (1936)]] is the canonical target; Ayer is named for the "another language" defense of sense-data.
- **[[Thinkers/Ian Hacking]]** — [[Concepts/Observation as a Skill (Hacking)]] supplies the empirical case study for Sellars's a priori point: observing is a trained, theory-laden competence, not a passive reception.
- **[[Thinkers/John McDowell]]** — *Mind and World* accepts the Myth of the Given and then worries that Sellars's own position risks leaving experience unable to *rationally constrain* belief at all (coherentism, "frictionless spinning in a void"); McDowell's fix is that experience is already conceptual.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Tension with [[Concepts/Sense-Data vs. Physical Objects (Russell)]] and [[Concepts/Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description (Russell)]] — Russell's whole constructive epistemology rests on acquaintance as inference-free awareness that grounds description. Sellars's argument, if sound, removes the epistemic load-bearing role while leaving the act/object distinction Russell used against Berkeley intact.
- > [!warning] Cuts against [[Concepts/Impressions and Ideas - Copy Principle (Hume)]]. Hume's meaning-test ("from what impression?") presupposes that one can be aware of a determinate repeatable simply by having the impression — precisely what [[Concepts/Psychological Nominalism (Sellars)|psychological nominalism]] denies.
- **The open question Sellars leaves**: if experience does not justify by being given, what *does* the relation between sensory episodes and empirical belief come to? Sellars's answer is the [[Concepts/Myth of Jones (Sellars)|Myth of Jones]]; whether it succeeds is the debate McDowell and Brandom inherit.
