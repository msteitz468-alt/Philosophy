# Perception as Causal Inference (Hohwy)
> Perception is the brain inferring hidden worldly causes from the only thing it has — their effects on the senses — by Bayes, hierarchically, and without ever sensing the objects themselves.

## Summary

Chapter 1 of [[Sources/The Predictive Mind - Jakob Hohwy (2013)|*The Predictive Mind*]] states the problem of perception as a problem of **causal inference**. "Objects and processes in the world are the causes of the sensory input. The problem of perception is the problem of using the effects — that is, the sensory data that is all the brain has access to — to figure out the causes" (p. 13). It is hard because the same cause can produce very different effects (a bicycle seen vs touched, from different angles) and different causes can produce the same effects. There is no one–one map. The brain cannot "help itself to the answer by going beyond the perspective of the skull-bound brain" (p. 15).

Additional constraints on the inference must be *normative*, not "brute biases." The ingredients are those of Bayes' rule: **likelihood** P(e|h), **prior** P(h), **posterior** P(h|e). "The brain infers the causes of its sensory input using Bayes' rule — that is the way it perceives" (p. 18). The inference is Helmholtzian and unconscious: from "the observed effect on our senses" we arrive at an idea of the cause, though we "sense the effects, never the external objects" (Helmholtz 1867: 430, at p. 17).

**Binocular rivalry** is Hohwy's demonstration that perception is not stimulus-driven feature detection. The world-stimulus is constant (incompatible images to each eye) while percepts alternate. So "some manner of inferential power" is required (pp. 19–20). Helmholtz already called rivalry a "wonderful theatre."

Inference is **hierarchical**. The cortex "recapitulates" nested causal regularities at different timescales. This yields both variant (perspectival) and invariant perception — not mere labelling (pp. 27–30). Priors come from higher levels (**empirical Bayes**); idle circularity is broken because prediction error is an "objective corrective caused by the objects in the world" (pp. 33–34). Message passing runs both ways. Chapter 2 then shows that [[Concepts/Prediction Error Minimization (Hohwy)|minimizing prediction error]] *is* this inference, implemented so the generative model need not be separately inverted.

## Key Claims / Positions

- **Normative, not brute.** Whatever extra constraints the brain uses to invert the many–one mapping must be evaluable as better or worse inferences, not wired-in quirks.
- **Unconscious perceptual inference.** Neural machinery performs the Bayes, not a homunculus.
- **Rivalry as existence proof.** Constant stimulus, alternating percept ⇒ inference, not detection.
- **Hierarchy does phenomenology.** Variant vs invariant, coarse-to-fine, "woods before the trees" (Clark's later phrase) are products of nested timescales, not extra Gestalt principles.
- **The world is the truth.** Supervision is by the statistical regularities the world actually causes in us (p. 49).

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Helmholtz**: the historical source. Hypothesis-testing as the escape from "the world of the sensations of our own nervous system."
- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]**: the mind's prior conceptions (forms of intuition, categories) organize the sensory manifold. Hohwy quotes A51/B75. Helmholtz is Kant made answerable to the world.
- **[[Thinkers/Hume]]**, Lewis, Woodward, **[[Thinkers/Judea Pearl]]**: causation as extracting regularities *and* modelling intervention. Both enter unconscious perceptual inference (p. 6).
- **Ibn al-Haytham**: "many visible properties are perceived by judgement and inference" (II.3.16), a millennium earlier (p. 5).
- **Fodor / Pylyshyn**: accept low-level unconscious inference; resist high-to-low cognitive penetrability. Hohwy takes the Helmholtzian core and later (ch. 6) gives a precision/uncertainty verdict on the rest — see [[Concepts/Cognitive Penetrability (Hohwy)]].
- **[[Thinkers/Andy Clark]]**: same Helmholtzian inversion of the "smart camera." Clark's emphasis is construction ("controlled hallucination"); Hohwy's is the inverse problem and the skull-bound constraint.
