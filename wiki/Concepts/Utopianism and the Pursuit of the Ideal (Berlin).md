# Utopianism and the Pursuit of the Ideal (Berlin)

> The West’s oldest moral assumption — one true answer to every genuine question, a path to it, and compatibility of all true answers — makes a perfect society look possible. Berlin’s claim is stronger: the perfect whole is **conceptually incoherent**, and the search for it is “a recipe for bloodshed.”

## Summary

In “The Pursuit of the Ideal” (1988) and “The Decline of Utopian Ideas in the West” (1978), [[Thinkers/Isaiah Berlin]] treats **utopia** as the political form of **monism**. The “Platonic ideal” shared by Greeks, the monotheisms, seventeenth-century rationalists, Enlightenment empiricists, Condorcet, and (after historicising) Hegel and Marx is a **three-legged stool**:

1. Every genuine question has **one true answer** (the rest are errors).
2. A **dependable path** to those truths exists (even if only Adam, angels, or God now walk it).
3. The true answers, when found, **must be compatible** and form “a single whole.”

Utopias built on this stool are typically **static**: perfection needs no change. They presuppose a **fixed human nature** with identical essential goals. The myth of a broken primal unity and its restoration is “a central strand in the whole of Western thought.” Socrates/Plato’s “virtue is knowledge” underwrites Renaissance and later Utopias.

Berlin’s counter is not that perfection is hard. “The notion of the perfect whole… is not merely unattainable — that is a truism — but conceptually incoherent.” Some **Great Goods cannot live together**. That is why Kant’s line, used as argument and not only as the book’s title, matters: “Out of the crooked timber of humanity no straight thing was ever made.” To force people into “the neat uniforms demanded by dogmatically believed-in schemes is almost always the road to inhumanity.”

## How Berlin Uses This

- **Omelette politics.** If a “final solution” exists, there is “surely no limit to the number of eggs that should be broken” — “the faith of Lenin, of Trotsky, of Mao, for all I know of Pol Pot.” Herzen (*From the Other Shore*) is enlisted against sacrificing the living on “altars of abstractions.”
- **Why utopia died as a credible idea.** Reformation-era national lawyers break “one world, one universal law.” Then the German backlash to French cultural hegemony. [[Thinkers/Johann Gottfried Herder]]: a single perfect society of all mankind is “internally self-contradictory” (“Valhalla of the Germans” ≠ the French ideal). “This… is the beginning of the modern attack on the notion of Utopia, Utopia as such.”
- **Romantics.** Values are “not discovered, they are created.” Class- and nation-generated values destroy the single moral universe. Hegel and Marx then restore a “happy ending.” Huxley, Orwell, Zamyatin, Tocqueville, Mill protest “uniformitarian despotism.”
- **What replaces it.** If Great Goods collide, “What is to be done?” has “no clear reply.” Collisions can be “softened.” “The first public obligation is to avoid extremes of suffering.” A “precarious” / “uneasy equilibrium” is “the first requirement for a decent society.” There is at least “a minimum without which societies could scarcely survive.”
- **Two modern views, irreconcilable.** Eternal values plus eventual “true human history” vs “open texture,” Mill’s “experiments in living,” friction under rules. If some ultimates are incompatible, “no perfect solution is, not merely in practice, but in principle, possible.”

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Plato]]:** ancestor of one-answer politics, hierarchical Utopia, virtue=knowledge. Berlin’s target is the *assumption*, not only the *Republic*.
- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]:** not the author of a utopia here. The 1784 *Idee* sentence is used to block perfect solutions **in principle**. Keep this apart from Berlin’s 1958 slide argument about Kantian self-legislation (reception: Baum/Nichols).
- **[[Thinkers/Hegel]] / [[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]:** historicise conflict but keep a happy ending / “true history.” Not pluralists.
- **[[Thinkers/John Stuart Mill]]:** ally — experiments in living; with Tocqueville, against uniformitarian despotism.
- **[[Thinkers/Robert Nozick]] / [[Concepts/Framework for Utopia (Nozick)]]:** a *different* word. Nozick’s framework is a meta-utopia of many communities. Berlin’s “Utopia” is one static pattern. Do not identify them. Berlin’s many cultures **refute** one perfect city; they are not a framework-utopia.
- **[[Thinkers/Karl Popper]]:** anti-utopian kinship (piecemeal vs holist engineering) if the vault’s Popper pages are used this way; this book does not ingest Popper.
- **[[Concepts/Value Pluralism (Berlin)]]:** the positive doctrine that makes utopia incoherent. See also [[Concepts/Pluralism versus Relativism (Berlin)]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Romantic “values are created” (Decline) sits next to essay 1’s “world of objective values.” Berlin uses the former as history (why utopia died) and the latter as his own view. Do not collapse them.
- > [!warning] This is **not** “Two Concepts of Liberty.” Negative/positive liberty is not the machinery. The thesis is incompatibility of ends.
- The 1958 lecture’s “final solution” slogan (as Baum/Nichols quote it) is the same family; this book is the history-of-ideas working-out.
