# Rigid Designation (Kripke)

> "Let's call something a *rigid designator* if in every possible world it designates the same object, a *nonrigid* or *accidental designator* if that is not the case." Ordinary proper names are rigid; typical definite descriptions are not.

## Summary

The load-bearing device of [[Sources/Naming and Necessity - Saul Kripke (1980)]]. A designator is rigid when, once a reading is fixed, the truth-conditions of simple sentences containing it, *in counterfactual situations*, still turn on that same object. "Aristotle was fond of dogs" is true of a counterfactual situation iff *that man* would have been fond of dogs — not whoever would then have been the last great philosopher of antiquity (Preface, pp. 6–7; Lecture I, p. 48).

Names are rigid: "no one other than Nixon might have been Nixon" (Lecture I, p. 48). "The President of the U.S. in 1970" is not. A designator is **strongly rigid** if its object is a necessary existent. Names need not designate in every world — "suppose Hitler had never been born" (Lecture II, p. 78) — but where we speak of a counterfactual situation in *our* language, the name still picks out that man. Demonstratives and free variables are also rigid (Lecture I, n. 16). The 1980 preface adds that names are rigid **de jure** (reference stipulated to be that object in every situation); the lectures often state only the weaker rigidity claim (Preface, p. 21 n. 21).

Possible worlds are **stipulated**, "not discovered by powerful telescopes" (Lecture I, p. 44). The preface's dice: thirty-six possible states of a pair of dice are miniature possible worlds; they are abstract states, not phantom dice-pairs, and they are not given purely qualitatively — or there would be twenty-one possibilities, not thirty-six (Preface, pp. 16–18). There is no special problem of "transworld identification." Lewis's counterpart theory is rejected: Humphrey would not care about a counterpart's victory.

Rigidity is not a scope convention. "Aristotle was fond of dogs" is a simple sentence; no modal operator, no room for scope (Preface, pp. 10–12). Dummett's reduction of rigidity to wide-scope reading of names is "a technical error." Homonymy (Aristotle the philosopher vs Onassis) is irrelevant once a reading is fixed.

If `a` and `b` are rigid and `a = b` is true, the identity is necessary (Preface, pp. 3–4; Lecture II, pp. 104–105; Lecture III, pp. 108–109). That is the engine of [[Concepts/Necessary A Posteriori and Contingent A Priori (Kripke)]] and of [[Arguments/Identity Thesis and Pain (Kripke)]].

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Bertrand Russell]]**: ordinary names are disguised non-rigid descriptions. Counterfactually, someone else being the last great philosopher of antiquity would then settle whether "Aristotle was fond of dogs." Kripke treats this as incompatible with the direct intuition of rigidity.
- **Mill** (no page): names have denotation, not connotation — partly recovered, but Kripke refuses Millian universal substitutivity in epistemic contexts (Preface, p. 20).
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: later *rigidifies* the secondary intension. That is Chalmers's two-dimensional apparatus, not this book's vocabulary. See [[Concepts/Logical Supervenience and Reductive Explanation (Chalmers)]].
- **Kaplan** (reported): rigidity of demonstratives; addendum (f) credits a formal "logic of demonstratives" (`Dthat`) in which "a good deal of the argument of this paper can be given a formal representation."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Preface: no official doctrine on what propositions codesignative names express in *epistemic* contexts. Rigidity implies interchangeability in *modal* contexts (nonexistence caveat). Do not fill the belief hole from later literature.
- > [!warning] De jure vs de facto rigidity is stated in the preface and not developed in the lectures.
