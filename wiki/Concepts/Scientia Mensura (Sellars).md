# Scientia Mensura (Sellars)

> §41 of [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|EPM]]: "in the dimension of describing and explaining the world, **science is the measure of all things, of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not**" — Sellars's scientific realism, and the reason the Myth of the Given matters for philosophy of science and not only for epistemology.

## The Claim

Sellars is willing to say "the more paradoxical thing" (§41): that there are no such things as the coloured objects of the common-sense world of physical objects in Space and Time. Not because common-sense discourse is meaningless, but because, *in the dimension of describing and explaining*, the scientific framework is authoritative over the common-sense one. The Protagorean formula is deliberately inverted: not man but *science* is the measure.

## The Argument Against the "Positivistic" Conception of Science

The target is the view Sellars calls "(in an extended sense) the **positivistic** conception of science": that the framework of theoretical objects — molecules, electromagnetic fields — is "an *auxiliary* framework," and that theoretical objects are "calculational devices" whose function is systematizing empirical laws, having no genuine ontological standing.

His diagnosis is that this conception rests on **two mistakes**:

1. **The Myth of the Given.** "A misunderstanding (which I have already exposed) of the *ostensive* element in the learning and use of a language." As long as one thinks there is a framework — physical objects or sense contents — "the absolute authenticity of which is guaranteed by the fact that the learning of this framework involves an 'ostensive step,'" one will be tempted to treat theoretical discourse as merely derivative. Remove the Given and that guarantee evaporates. This is the essay's central link between the epistemology and the philosophy of science: **the same error that props up foundationalism props up instrumentalism.**
2. **A reification of a methodological distinction** — turning the working contrast between theoretical and observational vocabulary into an ontological one.

Sellars presses the point with a dilemma about instrumentalist idiom: to say the objects of theoretical discourse "behave *as if*" they consisted of Y's "makes sense only by contrast with" the claim that X behaves as it does *because* it does consist of Y's — whereas the instrumentalist's contention "is exactly that where the X's are scientific objects, no such contrast makes sense."

The philosophers he opposes are "united in the conviction that what is called the 'ostensive tie' between our fundamental descriptive vocabulary and the world rules out of court as utterly absurd any notion that there are no such things as this framework talks about." Their unity survives their disagreement about *what* the fundamental framework is (sense contents for some, physical objects for others) — which is exactly Sellars's point about the Given as a *framework* error rather than a doctrine about a particular kind of item.

## Continuity, Not Two Worlds

The claim is not that science and common sense are alien. "Science is continuous with common sense, and the ways in which the scientist seeks to explain empirical phenomena are refinements of the ways in which plain men, however crudely and schematically, have attempted to understand their environment and their fellow men since the dawn of intelligence" (§51). "What we call the scientific enterprise is the flowering of a dimension of discourse which already exists in what historians call the 'prescientific stage.'"

This is why the [[Concepts/Myth of Jones (Sellars)|Myth of Jones]] can work at all: Jones is a *plain man theorizing*, not a modern scientist, and the postulational move that introduces thoughts and impressions is the same move that later introduces molecules.

Sellars also uses the section (§§39–40) to complain about the professional fragmentation that hides this: philosophy of science had become a specialty, encouraging "the temptation to leave it to the specialists," and the atomistic conception of analysis as "making little ones out of big ones" made such fencing-off seem harmless. It is not, "for 'analysis' no longer means the defining of terms, but rather the clarification of the logical structure — in the broadest sense — of discourse."

## Relation to the Rest of the Essay

The scientia mensura is what lets Sellars close the loop in §61(5) and §62. Having denied that sense contents can be found by analysis of ordinary discourse, he can still allow the sense-datum theorist a partial vindication *in a different place*: the particulars of a future micro-theory of sentient organisms "are the solid core of the sense contents and sense fields of the sense-datum theorist." Sellars sketches such organisms as "Space-Time worms characterized by variable[s]… peculiar to sentient things." What was wrong was not positing such items but supposing they were *given* — antecedent objects of knowledge already in the framework from the beginning.

He is careful to flag this as speculation: it would be a mistake "to treat what is really a speculative critique of the common-sense framework… as a matter of distinctions which can be drawn *within* the framework itself."

## How This Sits With the Vault

- **[[Thinkers/Karl Popper]]** — a strong ally here. Popper's scientific realism/conjecturalism against essentialism *and* instrumentalism is the same target; both reject "calculational device" readings of theory. Compare [[Concepts/Three Views Concerning Human Knowledge (Popper)]], which stages exactly this three-way contest.
- **[[Thinkers/Ian Hacking]]** — [[Concepts/Observation as a Skill (Hacking)]] and the experimental turn supply a different route to realism (manipulability rather than explanatory measure), and a useful check on Sellars's heavy weighting of *describing and explaining*.
- **[[Thinkers/A.J. Ayer]]** — [[Sources/Language, Truth and Logic - A.J. Ayer (1936)]] is the positivism whose verificationist scruples make theoretical entities auxiliary. Sellars's §41 is the direct reversal.
- **[[Thinkers/W.V.O. Quine]]** — an interesting near-miss. Quine's [[Concepts/Naturalized Epistemology and the Web of Belief (Quine)|"physical objects as pragmatic posits epistemologically on a par with the gods of Homer"]] levels the field between framework and theory; Sellars instead ranks them, giving science authority over common sense in the descriptive dimension. Both reject the observational/theoretical divide as ontologically fundamental; they differ on whether anything wins.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Sharp tension with the vault's phenomenological and common-sense-realist pages — e.g. [[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]] and [[Thinkers/Martin Heidegger]], for whom the lived world is not a deficient version of the scientific one. Sellars's "there are no such things" about coloured common-sense objects is precisely the move [[Schools/Phenomenology]] refuses.
- > [!warning] Tension with [[Concepts/Sense-Data vs. Physical Objects (Russell)]]: Russell's structural realism holds we know matter's *relations* but not its intrinsic nature; Sellars's scientia mensura is more confident, treating the scientific framework as ontologically authoritative rather than as structure-only.
- Open, and much debated since: whether the "dimension of describing and explaining" qualifier is a genuine restriction (leaving normative, practical, and personal discourse untouched) or a concession that quietly undoes the boldness of the thesis. Sellars's later "manifest image / scientific image" essay (1962) is where he develops it; that work is **not** in this volume and no vault page should attribute its detail to EPM.
