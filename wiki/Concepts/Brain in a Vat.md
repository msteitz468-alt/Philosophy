# Brain in a Vat
> A skeptical scenario: a brain, envatted and stimulated so that its experience matches a normal world, cannot tell from the inside that it has no body and no environment. Putnam's use of it is semantic and requires *lifelong* computer envattment; the ordinary skeptical use does not.

## Summary

Pritchard and Ranalli (2016) treat the BIV as "a familiar way of arguing for radical skepticism." The hypothesis is "by [its] nature indistinguishable from normal experience," so one cannot know it is false. Everyday propositions (that one has hands) are known to be inconsistent with it (BIVs don't have hands). A **closure** principle then yields the skeptical conclusion: if one cannot know one is not a BIV, one cannot know those everyday propositions either.

That is the **epistemological** use. Putnam's 1981 use is different (see [[Arguments/Putnam Brain in a Vat Argument]]). He stipulates that the brain has **always** been envatted and that stimulation is (and always has been) by **supercomputers**, so there is "no route, whether direct or indirect," by which the BIV could become causally related to trees and the rest of the ordinary furniture. Recent envattment would leave prior causal connections intact; Putnam's semantic result does not apply to it. Pritchard and Ranalli set "interesting questions" about whether a recently envatted brain eventually loses the capacity to think ordinary thoughts (citing Boghossian 1989 on Twin Earth) to one side.

[[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]] (1986, as they report him) treats the stipulated BIV as a **template**: "The traditional skeptical possibilities that we can imagine stand for limitless possibilities that we can’t imagine." Even a successful reply to this one scenario leaves ineffable skeptical hypotheses.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Ordinary BIV skepticism** (as Pritchard and Ranalli reconstruct it): indistinguishability + closure. Replies include denying closure ([[Thinkers/Robert Nozick]] 1981; Dretske 1970) or claiming one can know the denials of skeptical hypotheses (Pritchard's own cited papers).
- **[[Thinkers/Hilary Putnam]]** (1981b, reception only): not a claim that one can know one is not a BIV, but that one cannot *truly think* that thought. Requires the lifelong-computer stipulation. Engine: [[Concepts/Semantic Externalism (Putnam)]].
- **[[Thinkers/Descartes]]**: the ancestor is the evil demon of Meditation I, which alone among the three demolition arguments (illusion, dream, demon) supports *global* skepticism. The BIV is the technological rewrite.
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]** (*Reality+*): the lineage is demon → Putnam's BIV → **simulation hypothesis**. Putnam's semantic argument "works (if at all) against 'brain in a vat,' not 'computer simulation'"; Sim Putnam really is in a simulation and can say so. Chalmers grants one cannot *know* one is not simulated and denies that this is a skeptical result ([[Concepts/Simulation Realism (Chalmers)]]).
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]]** (1986, reported): the scenario's intelligibility is evidence *against* causal theories of reference; the leftover thought ("perhaps I cannot even think what I am") *is* skepticism. See [[Contradictions/Putnam vs Nagel - Semantic Anti-Skepticism vs Intelligible Skepticism]].
- **[[Thinkers/Nick Bostrom]]**: the simulation argument is not a BIV argument. It is a disjunction about the fraction of observer-moments that are simulated; it does not turn on undetectable envattment of a biological brain.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Putnam's semantic use vs Nagel's claim that the scenario is intelligibly possible — see [[Contradictions/Putnam vs Nagel - Semantic Anti-Skepticism vs Intelligible Skepticism]].
- The stipulated lifelong-computer BIV, a recently envatted brain, a demon world, and a computer simulation are **not** the same hypothesis. Arguments that hit one can miss the others. Chalmers and Pritchard/Ranalli both insist on this; the vault should too.
- Putnam 1981 remains outstanding. This page is written from the 2016 reconstruction plus the existing Chalmers reception.
