# Value as Lack and the Impossible Synthesis (Sartre)
> Value is the lack in relation to which the for-itself determines its being; the ideal filling of that lack would be the *ens causa sui* — in-itself-for-itself, God — and the real is an abortive effort to attain it.

## Summary

Part Two, Chapter One derives value from the for-itself's structure as lack; the Conclusion and Part Four state the whole-work result.

**Derivation (Immediate Structures).** The in-itself is identity; consciousness is a *decompression*. Of all internal negations, "this negation is lack" (BN 86). "Human reality … must be itself a lack" (BN 87). What it lacks is itself *as in-itself* — "the impossible synthesis of the for-itself and the in-itself" (BN 90), Hegel's unhappy consciousness "with no possibility of surpassing its unhappy state." "The being of the self: it is value" — "value is 'the lacked;' it is not 'the lacking'" (BN 92–93). Facticity: consciousness founds its nothingness but not its being; one is totally responsible *and* totally unjustifiable, *de trop* (BN 83–84). Part Four then names the a priori structure: "man fundamentally is the desire to be God" (BN 566).

**Ontology cannot write ethics.** "Ontology itself can not formulate ethical precepts. It is concerned solely with what is, and we can not possibly derive imperatives from ontology's indicatives" (BN 625–626). It can, however, "catch a glimpse of what sort of ethics will assume its responsibilities when confronted with a human reality in situation." What it has revealed is "the origin and the nature of value": "value is the lack in relation to which the for-itself determines its being as a lack. By the very fact that the for-itself exists … value arises to haunt its being-for-itself" (BN 626).

**The missing synthesis.** Every task of the for-itself aims at "producing the missing synthesis of consciousness and being in the form of value or self-cause." "Man makes himself man in order to be God" (BN 626). There is no common measure between human reality and the self-cause it wants to be, so one could as well say that man *loses* himself in order that the self-cause may exist. All human existence is a passion; self-interest is only one freely chosen way to realize it. The Introduction had already defined the in-itself as *not* *causa sui* and the for-itself as the being that "has to be what it is." The Conclusion names the ideal totality: "the in-itself founded by the for-itself and identical with the for-itself which founds it — i.e., the *ens causa sui*." "But precisely because we adopt the point of view of this ideal being … we must establish that the real is an abortive effort to attain to the dignity of the self-cause. Everything happens as if the world, man, and man-in-the-world succeeded in realizing only a missing God" (BN 623). The in-itself and for-itself present themselves "in a state of disintegration in relation to an ideal synthesis" that "is always indicated and always impossible."

**Spirit of seriousness.** Existential psychoanalysis (see [[Concepts/Existential Psychoanalysis and the Original Choice (Sartre)]]) is "moral description" because it releases the ethical meaning of projects. Its principal result must be to make us "repudiate the spirit of seriousness." That spirit has two marks: it takes values as "transcendent givens independent of human subjectivity," and it transfers desirability from the ontological structure of things to their material constitution (bread is desirable because one must live, and because bread is nourishing). The result "rules the world": "Objects are mute demands, and he is nothing in himself but the passive obedience to these demands" (BN 626). This is already "on the moral plane but concurrently on that of bad faith," an ethics "ashamed of itself" that has "obscured all its goals in order to free itself from anguish."

**Equivalence and the promised ethics.** If one still believes the mission of effecting the in-itself-for-itself is written in things, one is "condemned to despair": "all human activities are equivalent (for they all tend to sacrifice man in order that the self-cause may arise) and … all are on principle doomed to failure. Thus it amounts to the same thing whether one gets drunk alone or is a leader of nations" (BN 627). Precedence, if any, belongs to the degree of consciousness of the *ideal* goal — in which case "the quietism of the solitary drunkard … will take precedence over the vain agitation of the leader of nations." Ontology and existential psychoanalysis must then reveal to the moral agent "that he is the being by whom values exist." Freedom "will reveal itself in anguish as the unique source of value and the nothingness by which the world exists" (BN 627). Whether freedom can "take itself for a value as the source of all value," or must remain defined by a transcendent value that haunts it, and whether a freedom that wills itself freedom is another bad faith or "another fundamental attitude," is deferred: "We shall devote to them a future work" (BN 628). That work was never published as a treatise.

Barnes's Key to Special Terminology (BN 629) glosses **absurd** as the meaninglessness of existence whose contingency finds no external justification, and of projects directed toward an unattainable goal ("the desire to become God"). The phrase **"useless passion"** (*passion inutile*) is the book's name for this structure.

## Key Claims / Positions

- Value = the lack that haunts the for-itself; not a transcendent given.
- Ideal being = *ens causa sui* / in-itself-for-itself / God; the real is a missing God.
- Spirit of seriousness = values written in things; it is bad faith at the scale of an ethics.
- Ontology yields no imperatives; the ethics of a freedom that takes itself as value is promised, not written.

### How Sartre Differs From Prior Thinkers in the Wiki

- **vs [[Thinkers/Spinoza]]**: *causa sui* is the dignity being cannot attain; it is not the definition of substance.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Hegel]]**: the synthesis that would justify the terms is "on principle out of reach"; the real is a "decapitated notion in perpetual disintegration" (BN 623–624), not a completed *Aufhebung*.
- **vs [[Thinkers/Kant]]**: agrees that one cannot derive *ought* from ontology's *is*; the glimpse of ethics is not a categorical imperative but the question whether freedom can will itself.
- **vs the 1945/46 lecture**: the lecture condemns cowards and scum and names good faith as consistency in willing freedom. The Conclusion's equivalence thesis (drunkard = leader of nations) and the withheld ethics sit in tension with that public verdict. See [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)]], [[Concepts/Existential Humanism (Sartre)]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The Conclusion's "all activities are equivalent" conflicts with the lecture's moral names (cowards, scum) and with any ranking of projects except by degree of lucidity. Do not silently reconcile.
- The promised ethics is an internal hole: BN shows value's origin and then refuses to say what a freedom that takes itself as value would live like.
