# Gorilla Problem (Russell)

> Russell's name (Ch. 5) for the species-level control problem: humans may lose "supremacy and autonomy" to substantially smarter machines, as gorillas have lost them to us. Ban and retreat are neither politically likely nor enforceable. The solution, if there is one, is not to remain the smartest species. It is to keep machines out of conflict with us by solving King Midas — not putting a definite, incomplete purpose into the machine.

## Summary

The gorilla problem is not "robots will hate us." It is a fact about comparative competence. Gorillas cannot switch humans off. If we create entities "far more powerful than humans," the live question of Ch. 1 is: "How do we ensure that they never, ever have power over us?"

Two responses fail.

**Ban / retreat.** Whiteboard mathematics cannot be uninvented. Tool-AI research spills into general techniques. "We cannot insure against future catastrophe simply by betting against human ingenuity." Kevin Kelly's "no single IQ dimension" does not help: chimps outrank us on some axes; "smarter than" still decides who survives.

**Resign.** Moravec-style cession of the future is a mistake. Value is "defined primarily by conscious human experience."

The remaining path is design. The gorilla problem is produced by the [[Concepts/Standard Model of AI (Russell)|standard model]] plus capability. A machine with a definite objective and enough competence will treat humans as obstacles or resources. Solving King Midas — abandoning the fixed objective — is what keeps machines out of conflict with humans, and so solves the gorilla problem without requiring us to remain supreme in raw intelligence.

Do not confuse this slogan with the Google Photos incident in Ch. 1 (equal-cost loss labeled people as gorillas). That is a King Midas vignette. The *gorilla problem* is the species analogy in Ch. 5.

## Key Claims

- Loss of control is a comparative-competence problem, not a Hollywood-hatred problem. "It's competence, not consciousness, that matters."
- Superintelligent AI is likened to incoming aliens we ourselves build. The email exchange in Ch. 1 (aliens: we arrive in 30–50 years; humanity: out of office) is the gorilla problem as current policy.
- Control, not prohibition. Tribal pro-/anti-AI camps block the work that would actually keep power.
- Ch. 10: even if loss-of-control is designed away, enfeeblement can still make us passengers. That is a second gorilla problem — cultural, not technical. Children cannot switch their parents off; we will be able to switch the machines off only if Ch. 8 holds, and we may choose not to.

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **Bostrom**: The sparrows' owl is the gorilla problem as fable. Decisive strategic advantage and singleton are the same comparative-competence fact at civilizational scale. Bostrom's default is catastrophe unless motivation selection works; Russell's default is the same unless the standard model is abandoned.
- **Wiener**: The *machine à gouverner* is humans using machines to dominate humans — a gorilla problem *among* humans. Russell's gorilla is the machine side of the same gradient.
- **Kurzweil**: Merger as escape from the gorilla problem (become the smarter species). Russell lists merger among the Ch. 6 non-solutions: it does not specify whose values survive the merge.
- **Mitchell / Ng / AI100**: "Too soon" and "not even possible" are refusals to see a gorilla problem. "The reasons for concern are not predicated on imminence."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] If solving Midas solves the gorilla problem, then a beneficial superintelligence can be arbitrarily smarter than us. That is a large empirical bet on Ch. 8's theorems surviving "us" (Ch. 9) and Dr. Evil (Ch. 10).
- Enfeeblement reopens the gorilla problem from the human side: we hand over the trillion person-years of civilizational learning and cannot get them back.
- Multipolar gorilla: many slightly-superhuman systems vs. one global wireless entity (Ch. 3's architecture). Russell's default picture is already one connected system.
