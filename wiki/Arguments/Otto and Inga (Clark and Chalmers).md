# Otto and Inga (Clark and Chalmers)
> If Inga's standing memory is a belief, Otto's notebook entry is a belief — and then a belief is not in the head.

## Summary

The **Otto and Inga** thought experiment is the step from extended *cognition* to extended *mind* in §4 of [[Sources/The Extended Mind - Andy Clark and David Chalmers (1998)]]. Processing in the environment might be cheap; "truly mental states" might still be internal. Experiences, they concede, "may be determined internally." Beliefs, they argue, need not be.

**Inga.** She hears there is an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art, thinks for a moment, recalls that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks there. She believed this *before* consulting memory. It was not occurrent, "but then neither are most of our beliefs. The belief was somewhere in memory, waiting to be accessed."

**Otto.** He has Alzheimer's and "relies on information in the environment to help structure his life." He carries a notebook everywhere, writes new information, looks up old. The notebook "plays the role usually played by a biological memory." He hears of the same exhibition, consults the notebook (53rd Street), and walks there. "Just as Inga had her belief even before she consulted her memory, it seems reasonable to say that Otto believed the museum was on 53rd Street even before consulting his notebook." "In relevant respects the cases are entirely analogous."

The alternative — Otto has no belief until he looks, or only the higher-order belief that the museum is at the address written in the notebook — "seems to miss the big picture in just the same way as saying that Inga's beliefs disappear as soon as she is no longer conscious of them." Both stores are "reliably there when needed, available to consciousness and available to guide action, in just the way that we expect a belief to be."

**Explanatory simplicity.** Beliefs and desires are characterized by their explanatory roles. The causal dynamics "mirror each other precisely." Explaining Otto via desire + belief-that-the-address-is-in-the-notebook + the accessible fact that the notebook says 53rd Street "complicates the explanation unnecessarily" and must be repeated for every notebook-involving action. "To explain things this way is to take one step too many." Pointing to the notebook in every explanation would be as redundant as pointing to Inga's memory. "In an explanation, simplicity is power."

**Twin Otto.** He is a physical duplicate of Otto from the skin in, but earlier wrote 51st Street by mistake. He walks to 51st. Otto believes 53rd; Twin Otto believes 51st. "In these cases, a belief is simply not in the head." Unlike Putnam/Burge twins, these twins behave *differently*; the external difference is dynamically relevant. Footnote 4: they differ in **notional** (primary-intension) content, because the cognitive system now includes the notebook.

**Usage vs natural kind.** An opponent may simply refuse to call Otto's state a "belief." They "do not intend to debate what is standard usage." The broader notion "picks out something more akin to a natural kind" and is "more useful in explanation." To make the case solely on location "would be to beg the question."

## Objections Treated as Shallow

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| The notebook can be stolen; memory is safer | Inga's brain can be tampered with or fogged by drink. Constancy matters; Otto's access is "very reliable" though not perfect. A one-off guidebook would *not* yield a standing belief. |
| Access comes and goes (shower, darkness) | Inga asleep or intoxicated does not lose the belief. What counts is availability "when the subject needs it." |
| Lower bandwidth / perceptual access | Lucy, with a low-grade biological memory link, still believes. Struggle or a psychotherapist would start to undermine ascription; Otto is not there. From the coupled-system standpoint the notebook–brain flow "is not perceptual at all." |
| Different phenomenology | Why should that decide *belief*? The Terminator recalls by visual display; standing-belief status is unaffected. |
| Only occurrent beliefs are real | Then Inga is out too, and so are most everyday ascriptions. "Once dispositional beliefs are let in the door," Otto's notebook has "all the relevant dispositions." |

## Four Features of the Central Case

From §5, not as necessary and sufficient conditions but as what makes the ascription "so clearly applicable":

1. The notebook is a **constant** — Otto rarely takes relevant action without consulting it.
2. The information is **directly available without difficulty**.
3. Upon retrieving it he **automatically endorses** it.
4. It was **consciously endorsed in the past** and is there as a consequence. (Feature 4 is "arguable" — subliminal perception, memory tampering.)

The 1998 Internet typically fails several of these; certain computer files may pass. Socially extended cases (partner, waiter, secretary) pass when trust, reliance, and accessibility are high.

## How Later Sources Use the Case

- **Clark 2023**: updates the notebook to the smartphone; keeps the parity structure; adds a predictive-processing account of why recruitment is effortless.
- **Chalmers 2022**: Ishi/Omar and the Sydney Opera House; Ned Block's "false then, true now"; AR as a superior extender; stealing a phone becomes "more akin to assault" — the 1998 moral consequence made concrete.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The argument extends **belief**, not experience. Collapsing Otto into a claim about consciousness extending is a misreading the paper warns against.
- Open: feature (4) and tampering. If someone writes a false address in the notebook overnight, does Otto believe it? They list the question and refuse a categorical answer.
- Open: whether "simplicity is power" is a metaphysical argument or only a plea for a better explanatory kind. The Twin Otto case is meant to do metaphysical work the simplicity claim alone cannot.
