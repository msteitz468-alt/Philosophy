# Zombie Argument (Chalmers)
> A phenomenal zombie — physically and functionally identical to you, but with no experience, "all dark inside" — is conceptually coherent; therefore consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical, and reductive explanation must fail.

## Summary

*The Conscious Mind* ch. 3 marshals five converging arguments against the logical supervenience of consciousness:

1. **Zombies**: a physical duplicate without experience is conceivable — "There is nothing it is like to be a zombie." Mere conceptual coherence (not empirical possibility) defeats logical supervenience; the burden lies on those alleging a hidden contradiction. Bolstered by nonstandard realizations (Block's China-nation, silicon isomorphs).
2. **Inverted spectrum**: a replica with red/blue-inverted qualia is coherent; even the *character* of experience is unexplained.
3. **Epistemic asymmetry**: knowledge of consciousness derives only from the first-person case. There is a problem of other minds, but no problem of "other lives" or "other economies" — those supervene logically.
4. **Knowledge argument**: Jackson's Mary (with Nagel's bat) — complete physical knowledge does not yield knowledge of what it is like to see red. Now primary as [[Arguments/Knowledge Argument (Jackson)]] (1982 and 1986): Fred first, then Mary. Jackson himself ranked this argument as *polemically stronger* than the modal/zombie argument, whose intuition he notes is disputed — and his version is purely epistemic, concluding only that some truths escape the physicalist story, where Chalmers converts it into a modal claim about supervenience.
5. **Absence of analysis**: any a priori entailment would need a functional analysis of consciousness, and all "fail miserably" — "One might as well define 'world peace' as 'a ham sandwich.'"

Result: Levine's **explanatory gap**. The vitalism analogy fails as a rejoinder: vitalist doubts concerned *functions* and dissolved when the functions were explained; the consciousness problem survives complete functional explanation. Surveyed reductive programs all explain awareness, not experience: Baars's global workspace, Dennett's models, Crick & Koch's 40-Hz binding, Edelman's re-entrant circuits, Penrose–Hameroff quantum microtubules ("from structure and dynamics, we can only get more structure and dynamics"), and evolutionary accounts ("evolution cannot distinguish between me and my zombie twin"). Baars's program now has a vault primary in [[Thinkers/Stanislas Dehaene]] ([[Concepts/Global Neuronal Workspace (Dehaene)]]); Dehaene's Ch. 7 reply is that the leftover is vitalism and that Chalmers swapped the easy/hard labels. See [[Contradictions/Dehaene vs Chalmers - Access Consciousness vs the Hard Problem]].

The argument's modal machinery (primary intensions, conceivability→possibility) and the a posteriori-necessity objections are handled in [[Concepts/Logical Supervenience and Reductive Explanation (Chalmers)]] and [[Concepts/Naturalistic Dualism (Chalmers)]].

The named ancestor is [[Arguments/Identity Thesis and Pain (Kripke)]] (*Naming and Necessity* Lecture III, now primary). Kripke has no "zombie" and no full-world physical duplicate: he argues both C-fiber stimulation without pain *and* pain without C-fiber stimulation, and that pain is not picked out by an accidental property the way heat is. Chalmers generalizes the leftover to a physically identical world "all dark inside" and then uses 2-D semantics to keep Kripkean a posteriori necessities from blocking that world. That last step is not in Kripke.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] [[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]] (2023 lecture) uses the same conceivability argument — a physical organism "behaviorally, physiologically, and chemically indistinguishable from you or me, but which nevertheless has no inner, subjective mental life" — and then *refuses* to infer metaphysical dualism. Conceptual dualism is real; the hoped-for truth is [[Concepts/Psychophysical Nexus and Neutral Monism (Nagel)|monism]]. See [[Sources/What Is It Like to Be a Bat - Thomas Nagel (1974)]].
- > [!warning] [[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]] ([[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]]) denies zombies are coherently conceivable — anyone who conceives one has failed to imagine the full functional story. See [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]].
- > [!warning] [[Thinkers/Frank Jackson]] (1982 §II) accepts the modal intuition as a *consequence* of the knowledge argument, not as its ground, and treats it as weaker because it is disputed (Kirk recanted). The knowledge argument is the one he is willing to rest on.
- > [!warning] The argument's success saddles Chalmers with the [[Concepts/Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment (Chalmers)]]: his zombie twin writes the same book for the same functional reasons.
