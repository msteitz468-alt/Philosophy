# Wilt Chamberlain Argument (Nozick)
> Start from any patterned just distribution; let people pay Wilt a quarter to watch him play; the new distribution is just if the old one was, and no patterned principle can survive that liberty.

## Summary

The Wilt Chamberlain argument is the load-bearing thought experiment of *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* Ch. 7, section “HOW LIBERTY UPSETS PATTERNS.” It is the step from the form of [[Concepts/Entitlement Theory (Nozick)|entitlement theory]] to the claim that no more extensive state can be justified by distributive justice.

**Setup.** Let *D*1 be a distribution that your favorite patterned or end-state principle of justice already ranks as just — equality, a Rawlsian difference-principle profile, to-each-according-to-need, whatever. People are entitled to their *D*1 shares. Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract: twenty-five cents from the price of each home-game ticket goes to him. One million people attend. Wilt has $250,000. Call this *D*2.

**The claim.** If *D*1 was just, and people voluntarily moved from it to *D*2, transferring resources they were entitled to, then *D*2 is just. Third parties still have their *D*1 shares; no one else’s holdings were seized. To restore *D*1 (or any other pattern) the state must continuously interfere: forbid the payments, tax them back, or prevent the contract. “No end-state principle or distributional patterned principle of justice can be continuously realized without continuous interference with people’s lives.”

**The socialist analog.** “The socialist society would have to forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults.”

**What the argument is supposed to show.** Liberty upsets patterns. A principle that looks only at who has what (or at a time-sequence of who-has-what profiles) cannot be maintained unless people are stopped from doing what they choose with what they justly hold. Entitlement is historical but *not* patterned; that is why it can survive voluntary moves.

**What it does not show.** It does not specify justice in original acquisition. It assumes *D*1 is just, including that people are entitled to transfer their shares. Some “very weak patterns,” Nozick concedes, may not be liberty-thwarted. Rectification of past injustice is a different topic: if *D*1 itself is stained, Chamberlain’s $250,000 inherits the stain.

## How This Relates to Prior Thinkers

- **Rawls**: the difference principle is an end-state / patterned principle. If *D*1 is the difference-principle profile, *D*2 violates it, and restoring it requires continuous interference. See [[Contradictions/Nozick vs Rawls - Historical Entitlement vs Patterned Justice]].
- **[[Thinkers/John Stuart Mill]]**: utilitarianism is the type-case of a time-slice principle the argument defeats. Mill’s [[Concepts/Harm Principle (Mill)|harm principle]] would not, by itself, forbid the quarter — unless “harm” is read as pattern-disruption.
- **[[Thinkers/Karl Marx]]**: “to each according to…” is the patterned slogan; the socialist analog is aimed here. The labor-theory attack is Ch. 8, not this argument.
- **[[Thinkers/Locke]]**: the transfers are Lockean use of property. The argument does not settle first acquisition.
- **[[Thinkers/Kant]]** / [[Concepts/Side Constraints (Nozick)]]: blocking the quarters to preserve a pattern uses Wilt and the ticket-buyers as means to an end-state.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] The argument is decisive only if justice in transfer is as unconstrained as Nozick claims. Patterned theorists will deny that *D*1 included an unlimited right to transfer. That is the contradiction page, not a slip in the setup.
- Does a gift or a bequest get the same treatment as a ticket? Nozick thinks yes; the “continuous interference” line is meant to cover families, not just sports.
