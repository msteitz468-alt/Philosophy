# Knowledge Argument (Jackson)

> Complete physical information is not complete information: Fred's extra colour, and then Mary's release from the black-and-white room, show that something — a quale — is left out of the physicalist story, and therefore that Physicalism is false.

## Summary

The argument is first stated in [[Sources/Epiphenomenal Qualia - Frank Jackson (1982)]] §I, as the polemically strongest replacement for the "unargued intuition" that nothing physical captures the smell of a rose. It is restated and defended against Paul Churchland in [[Sources/What Mary Didn't Know - Frank Jackson (1986)]].

**Physical information**, in the 1982 usage, is what the physical, chemical, and biological sciences provide, plus what "automatically comes along with it" — including the **functional role** of neural states. Jackson does not rest the case on a precise definition of the physical.

**Fred** (1982 only, as a rhetorical first pass). He sorts ripe tomatoes into two groups no one else can make, consistently, and reports two colours, red₁ and red₂, as different as yellow from blue. Physiology can in principle explain the extra discrimination. We should admit he sees at least one more colour; we are to him as the red-green colour-blind person is to us; to refuse is the mistake of Wells's "Country of the Blind." Yet all the physical information about his body, behaviour, dispositions, physiology, and history does not tell us what the new colour is like. If we later made our optics like his, people would say "at last we will know"; so we did not know all along everything the physicalist scheme contains.

**Mary** (1982, canonical in 1986). A brilliant scientist confined to a black-and-white room, educated through black-and-white books and a black-and-white monitor, acquires *all* the physical information about colour vision — wavelengths, retina, CNS, even the contraction of the vocal chords that produces "The sky is blue." (If this could not in principle be learned from black-and-white television, "the Open University would of necessity need to use colour television.") On release, or when given a colour monitor, "it seems just obvious that she will learn something about the world and our visual experience of it." Then her previous knowledge was incomplete; she had all the physical information; Physicalism is false. The same style covers taste, hearing, bodily sensations, "raw feels, phenomenal features or qualia."

The 1986 paper adds three clarifications and a more precise target. Physicalism is "the challenging thesis that [the actual world] is **entirely** physical," so complete physical knowledge must be complete knowledge *simpliciter*. The knowledge Mary lacked that matters is **knowledge about the experiences of others**, not a new fact about her own first red experience (there were no such facts beforehand). The argument is not about imagination, and not about the intensionality of knowledge. Churchland's Leibniz's-Law reconstruction — Mary knows everything about brain states, not everything about sensations, therefore sensations ≠ brain states — "is convenient but **not** the knowledge argument." The accurate display is: she knows everything *physical* about other people; she does not know everything about them; therefore some truths about them escape the physicalist story.

Jackson distinguishes the argument from two neighbours in 1982:

- **Modal / zombie argument** (§II): physical replicas with no conscious life. He accepts the intuition but treats it as disputed (Kirk recanted) and therefore weaker polemically. Acceptance of it can be a *consequence* of already having the knowledge argument, not its ground. See [[Arguments/Zombie Argument (Chalmers)]].
- **Nagel's "what it is like"** (§III): a complaint about occupying a point of view, *de se* knowledge, and imaginative extrapolation. Jackson's complaint is that a **property of the experience** is left out. Even after we learn the quale we will not know what it is like to *be* Fred. Physicalism "makes no special claims about the imaginative or extrapolative powers of human beings." See [[Concepts/Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)]].

He concedes (1986) that he has "no *proof*" Mary acquires factual knowledge as well as abilities; the argument is "valid … from highly plausible, though admittedly not demonstrable, premises," which is "about as good an objection as one could expect in this area of philosophy."

## How Different Thinkers Use This

- **[[Thinkers/Frank Jackson]]**: the author. 1982 states it and pairs it with [[Concepts/Epiphenomenal Qualia (Jackson)]]; 1986 defends the epistemology against Churchland. Later (not in the vault) he recanted the conclusion.
- **[[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]]** ([[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]]): if Mary *really* has all physical information she already knows every reactive disposition; the intuition that she learns something comes from imagining her with *less* than all the information. Blue-banana continuation: she is not naively surprised. This denies Jackson's "just obvious" premise rather than the inference.
- **[[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]**: argument 4 of [[Arguments/Zombie Argument (Chalmers)]]; Mary "gains factual knowledge"; the Lewis–Nemirow ability analysis is "deeply implausible" ([[Concepts/Naturalistic Dualism (Chalmers)]]). Embedded in a modal framework Jackson himself treated as weaker.
- **[[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]]**: cited by Jackson 1986 as a fellow manifestation; distinguished from it in 1982 §III. Nagel refuses the step to "Physicalism is false."
- **Lewis / Nemirow** (no thinker pages yet): the serious physicalist reply — what Mary gains is *knowledge how* (imaginative / representational abilities), not *knowledge that*. Jackson's 1986 answer is the other-minds-skepticism case: her abilities are "a known constant throughout" the episode she agonizes over. See [[Concepts/Ability Analysis of Phenomenal Knowledge (Lewis-Nemirow)]].
