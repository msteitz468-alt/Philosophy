# Inconsistent Triad in Sense-Datum Theories (Sellars)

> §§1–7 of [[Sources/Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)|*Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*]]: the classical sense datum must be both an unlearned sensing of a *particular* and a *knowing of a fact*. Sellars shows the two requirements form an inconsistent triad, and that no branch of it can be given up without abandoning the epistemological point of the Given.

## The Setup

Sense-datum theories distinguish the *act* (sensing) from its *object* (the colour patch). "Being a sense datum, or sensum, is a **relational property** of the item that is sensed" — hence the need for a neutral term for the item itself, and *sense content* is "perhaps, as neutral a term as any."

The trouble appears the moment we recall what the Given is *for*: "the point of the epistemological category of the given is, presumably, to explicate the idea that empirical knowledge rests on a 'foundation' of non-inferential knowledge of matter of fact." But **what is known is always a fact, never a particular** — "even in non-inferential knowledge, is facts rather than particulars, items of the form *something's being thus-and-so* or *something's standing in a certain relation to something else*." So the sensing of sense contents cannot by itself constitute knowledge.

The sense-datum theorist therefore faces a choice:

> (a) It is *particulars* which are sensed. Sensing is not knowing. The existence of sense data does not logically imply the existence of knowledge.
>
> (b) Sensing is a form of *knowing*. It is *facts* rather than particulars which are sensed.

Classically he insists on **both** — that sensing is a knowing *and* that particulars are sensed — which he can do without outright contradiction only by using "know" and "given" in two senses: a sense content is *sensed as being* red (a fact non-inferentially known), and then said to be *sensed* (full stop), and then said to be *known*. Sellars notes that this stipulated sense of "know" "would receive aid and comfort from the fact that there is, in ordinary usage, a sense of *know*" that takes a direct object — "Do you know John?", "Do you know the President?" — from which "the phrase **knowledge by acquaintance** recommends itself as a useful metaphor for this stipulated sense of *know* and, like other useful metaphors, has congealed into a technical term." (Compare [[Concepts/Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description (Russell)]].)

## The Triad

Sellars states the three propositions of "the inconsistent triad":

> **A.** *x* senses red sense content *s* entails *x* non-inferentially knows that *s* is red.
> **B.** The ability to sense sense contents is unacquired.
> **C.** The ability to know facts of the form *x* is φ is acquired.

"A and B together entail not-C; B and C entail not-A; A and C entail not-B."

Each escape route has a price:

1. **Abandon A** — sensing becomes "a noncognitive fact … which may be a necessary condition, even a logically necessary condition, of non-inferential knowledge, but a fact, nevertheless, which cannot *constitute* this knowledge." The Given loses its epistemic office.
2. **Abandon B** — he must "pay the price of cutting off the concept of a sense datum from its connection with our ordinary talk about sensations, feelings, afterimages, tickles and itches," i.e. from the common-sense counterparts that made it plausible.
3. **Abandon C** — "to do violence to the predominantly nominalistic proclivities of the empiricist tradition." (This is the branch Sellars himself attacks head-on with [[Concepts/Psychological Nominalism (Sellars)|psychological nominalism]]: all classificatory consciousness "involves learning, concept formation, even the use of symbols.")

## The Diagnosis: A Mongrel

"The classical concept of a sense datum [is] a mongrel resulting from a crossbreeding of two ideas":

1. **Inner episodes as causal-explanatory posits** — "sensations of red or of C♯ which can occur to human beings (and brutes) without any prior process of learning or concept formation; and without which it would — in *some* sense — be impossible to *see*, for example, that the facing surface of a physical object is red and triangular." This idea "arises in the attempt to explain the facts of sense perception in scientific style," and, so far as it goes, gives no reason to think that having a sensation "is a *cognitive* or *epistemic* fact"; it could be "a fact *sui generis*, neither epistemic nor physical, having its own logical grammar."
2. **Inner episodes as the epistemic foundation** — "certain inner episodes which *are* the non-inferential knowings that certain items are, for example, red or C♯; and … these episodes are the necessary conditions of empirical knowledge as providing the evidence for all other empirical propositions."

The second idea is driven by an argument from illusion: seeing is "a *veridical* member of a class of experiences — let us call them 'ostensible seeings' — some of the members of which are non-veridical; and there is no inspectible hallmark which guarantees that any such experience is veridical," so founding knowledge on those that "*happen* to be veridical" would "open the door to skepticism by making a mockery of the word *knowledge*." Sense contents are then imported to supply an infallible substitute — the blending Sellars is out to break.

He isolates the grammatical accident that greased the blend: "the grammatical similarity of 'sensation of a red triangle' to 'thought of a celestial city'" invites the presupposition "that sensations belong in the same general pigeonhole as thoughts — in short, are cognitive facts." Sellars's rule: it "would be a mistake" to infer from what may properly be flown as a flag about elephants to what may be flown about sense data; "if it makes sense to speak of an experience as veridical it must correspondingly make sense to speak of it as unveridical."

The upshot: "a sensation of a red triangle is the very paradigm of empirical knowledge" — an idea that "leads straight to the orthodox type of sense-datum theory and accounts for the perplexities which arise when one tries to think it through."

## The "Another Language" Variant (§§8–10)

Sellars then closes the side exit taken by [[Thinkers/A.J. Ayer|Ayer]] and others: that sense-datum discourse is "another language," a contrived notation for what the plain man says with "Now the book looks green to me." He develops this with an extended analogy to a **code** (ship's flags): if sense-datum sentences are code flags, "sense-datum talk neither clarifies nor explains facts of the form *x looks φ to S* or *x is φ*." The apparent logical relations among sense-datum sentences would be borrowed, not independent.

The "another language" view survives, Sellars argues, only by equivocating between two incompatible models: a *code* (which has "no surplus value," and so can illuminate ordinary discourse) and a *theory* (which does have surplus value, and so can explain). "These two characteristics are incompatible; for it is just because theories have *surplus value* that they can provide explanations." One cannot have both. Note that the possibility of treating sense contents as genuinely *theoretical* entities is "not obviously absurd" — Sellars sets it aside here and returns to it in the [[Concepts/Myth of Jones (Sellars)|Myth of Jones]].

## Significance

This is the *first* instance of the general critique, not the whole of it — "if, however, I begin my argument with an attack on sense-datum theories, it is only as a first step in a general critique of the entire framework of givenness." The general form is developed as the [[Concepts/Myth of the Given (Sellars)]].

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Direct attack on [[Concepts/Sense-Data vs. Physical Objects (Russell)]] and [[Concepts/Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description (Russell)]]. Russell's acquaintance is "direct, inference-free awareness" of sense data, and his "every proposition we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted" is precisely the doctrine that psychological nominalism denies. Sellars accepts the act/object distinction Russell used against Berkeley; he denies that the *object* side can carry epistemic weight.
- Sellars explicitly does **not** deny inner episodes, nor the difference between seeing and inferring. Read the argument as concerning what such episodes can *justify*, not whether they occur.
