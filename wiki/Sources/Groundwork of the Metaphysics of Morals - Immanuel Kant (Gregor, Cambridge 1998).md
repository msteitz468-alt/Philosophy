# Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals — Immanuel Kant (Gregor, Cambridge 1998)

> The search for and establishment of the supreme principle of morality: a pure a priori law that binds every rational will, independent of inclination, happiness, or anthropology.

**Full citation**: Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, trans. and ed. Mary Gregor, introduction by Christine M. Korsgaard (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, Cambridge University Press, 1998; 11th printing 2006). German original: *Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten* (1785). Academy pagination in the margins (4:387–463).

This is a **second English edition** of a work already in the vault via Allen W. Wood's Yale translation ([[Sources/Metaphysics of Morals - Immanuel Kant]] — that file is the 1785 *Groundwork*, not the 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*). The Cambridge volume adds Gregor's standard English, Academy references, a selected glossary, and Korsgaard's interpretive map. Existing concept pages are updated, not duplicated. New pages isolate [[Concepts/Dignity of Rational Nature (Kant)|dignity]] and [[Concepts/Perfect and Imperfect Duties (Kant)|perfect/imperfect duties]], which the Wood ingest left scattered.

## Argument Spine

The book is "nothing more than the search for and establishment of the supreme principle of morality" (4:392). Method: *analytic* from common moral cognition to the principle, then *synthetic* from the principle back to common use. A metaphysics of morals must be "completely cleansed of everything that may be only empirical" (4:389): a law that is to hold morally must carry *absolute necessity* and bind all rational beings, so its ground is a priori in pure reason, not in human nature or the circumstances of the world. Conformity with the law is not enough; the action must also be done *for the sake of the law* (4:390).

Korsgaard's frame: Kant's Copernican turn applied to morals — the rational order is not discovered in the world but *imposed* through the construction of knowledge and, differently, through action. Central themes: every human being is an end, never a mere means; respect for one's own humanity is respect for others'; "morality is freedom, and evil a form of enslavement."

## Summary

**Korsgaard's Introduction** treats the book as "a single, continuous argument." §§I–II are analytic: they identify *what* the supreme principle is and what would have to be shown if there is such a thing as morality. §III is the synthetic deduction: how a categorical imperative is possible. She rejects the common misreading that actions have moral worth "only if they are done reluctantly." She flags as contested Kant's claim that the three main formulas are equivalent — Formula of Humanity and Autonomy/Kingdom of Ends have been read as stronger than Formula of Universal Law.

**Preface (4:387–392).** Ancient philosophy divides into logic, physics (laws of nature), and ethics (laws of freedom). Ethics has an empirical part (practical anthropology) and a rational part (morals). Wolff's "universal practical philosophy" treats volition generally and so is not a metaphysics of a *pure* will. Kant issues this groundwork in advance of a later *Metaphysics of Morals* and distinguishes it from a full *Critique of Practical Reason*, because moral reason already works well in common understanding and because a complete critique would have to show the unity of practical and speculative reason.

**Section I (4:393–405).** "It is impossible to think of anything at all in the world, or indeed even beyond it, that could be considered good without limitation except a [[Concepts/Good Will (Kant)|good will]]" (4:393). Even fruitless, it "would still shine by itself, as something that has its full worth in itself" (4:394). Reason's vocation is not happiness (instinct would serve that better) but a will good in itself. [[Concepts/Duty (Kant)|Duty]] is that good will under hindrances: an action has moral worth only if done *from* duty, and the worth lies in the *maxim*, not the purpose. "Duty is the necessity of an action from respect for law" (4:400). After all material incentives are stripped, only universal lawfulness remains: "I ought never to act except in such a way that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law" (4:402). A false-promise maxim "would have to destroy itself" if universalized (4:403). Common reason already has this compass; a "natural dialectic" of inclination then drives it into philosophy.

**Section II (4:406–445).** Experience cannot "make out with complete certainty a single case" of action from pure duty (4:407). The will is practical reason; imperfect wills meet objective laws as *imperatives*. [[Concepts/Hypothetical and Categorical Imperatives (Kant)|Hypothetical]] imperatives command an action as means (skill / prudence); the [[Concepts/Categorical Imperative - Universal Law Formulation (Kant)|categorical]] commands an action as "objectively necessary of itself" (4:414). Hypotheticals are analytic; the CI is "an a priori synthetic practical proposition" (4:420). There is "only a single categorical imperative": "act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (4:421), equivalently as if it were a universal *law of nature*. Four illustrations yield a 2×2 of [[Concepts/Perfect and Imperfect Duties (Kant)|perfect/imperfect duties to self and others]], via contradiction in *conception* vs contradiction in the *will*. The [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)|Formula of Humanity]] recasts the same four: "So act that you use humanity… always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (4:429). [[Concepts/Autonomy and Heteronomy of the Will (Kant)|Autonomy]] is "the property of the will by which it is a law to itself" (4:440); every other principle is heteronomy (happiness, moral feeling, perfection, divine will) and "must necessarily miscarry." The [[Concepts/Kingdom of Ends (Kant)|kingdom of ends]] is "a systematic union of various rational beings through common laws" — "admittedly only an ideal" (4:433). [[Concepts/Dignity of Rational Nature (Kant)|Dignity]] (*Würde*) is "raised above all price" (4:434–435); "autonomy is therefore the ground of the dignity of human nature" (4:436). §§I–II remain analytic; that morality is no phantom requires the synthetic use of pure practical reason.

**Section III (4:446–463).** [[Concepts/Freedom as the Key to Autonomy (Kant)|Freedom]] is the key. Negative freedom (independence of alien causes) yields positive freedom = autonomy. "Hence a free will and a will under moral laws are one and the same" (4:447). Every rational being with a will must act *under the idea of freedom* (4:448). A suspected circle (freedom assumed to get morality, morality inferred from freedom) is broken by the [[Concepts/Two Standpoints: Sensible World and Intelligible World (Kant)|two standpoints]]: as appearance in the world of sense (natural necessity, heteronomy) and as intelligence in the intelligible world (autonomy). The moral *ought* is the intelligible self's necessary *will*, called "ought" only for the sensible self (4:455). Interest / moral feeling is the law's *effect*, not its ground (4:460–461). How pure reason can be practical is the same as how freedom is possible: **incomprehensible**. "We do not indeed comprehend the practical unconditional necessity of the moral imperative, but we nevertheless comprehend its *incomprehensibility*" (4:463).

## Key Claims / Positions

- A moral law must carry absolute necessity and bind all rational beings; any partly empirical precept is a practical rule, never a moral law (4:389).
- Only a good will is good without limitation; its worth is in willing, not effects (4:393–394).
- Moral worth requires acting *from* duty (respect for law), not mere conformity (4:397–400). Korsgaard: this isolates the motive; it does not require reluctance.
- There is one CI, expressed as universal law, law of nature, humanity as end, autonomy, and kingdom of ends (4:421–436). Equivalence of the formulas is asserted by Kant and contested by later readers (Korsgaard n.10).
- Autonomy is the sole supreme principle; all prior ethics is heteronomy (4:440–444). Happiness as moral ground is "most objectionable" (4:442).
- Freedom and morality are reciprocal; the two standpoints make a CI possible without theoretical knowledge of freedom (4:447–455).
- The limit of the inquiry: we comprehend only the incomprehensibility of how pure reason is practical (4:463).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Happiness as moral ground is the explicit target of §II's heteronomy table — the vault's other pole is [[Contradictions/Utilitarian Consequentialism vs. Kantian Deontology]] / [[Concepts/Greatest Happiness Principle (Mill)]].
- > [!warning] Freedom as first cause vs Spinoza's necessitarianism: [[Contradictions/Free Will - Spinozan Necessity vs Kantian Autonomy]]. Kant keeps *both* standpoints "absolutely necessary."
- > [!warning] Two worlds as two *standpoints*, thinkable only formally — the seed of [[Contradictions/Hegelian Monism vs. Kantian Dualism]].
- Korsgaard records the formula-equivalence dispute (FUL vs stronger FH/autonomy). The Wood/Yale essays already flag related interpretive fights (rigorism, emptiness of FUL, "fact of reason" in the later *Critique of Practical Reason*).
- The 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals* (Doctrine of Right + Doctrine of Virtue) is still not in the vault. Kant himself treats this book as preparatory to that system.

## Whole-Work Assessment

The Gregor/Korsgaard Cambridge edition does not change *what* the vault already held from Wood's *Groundwork*. It changes the *citation standard* and the *interpretive frame*. Academy numbers (4:387–463) now sit on every Kant ethics page. Gregor's wording of the load-bearing formulas (FUL 4:421, FH 4:429, dignity 4:434–436, free will = will under moral laws 4:447) is now the vault's English. Korsgaard supplies the autonomy-first road map that the Wood volume's four essays (Schneewind, Baron, Kagan, Wood) do not: §§I–II identify what must be proved; §III is the deduction; the "reluctant duty" reading is a misreading; dignity is legislative membership in a possible kingdom of ends; morality is "metaphysics in practice." Two concepts the first ingest left implicit — dignity vs price, and the perfect/imperfect duty table — now have their own pages. The outstanding-sources claim that the vault had "the later *Metaphysics of Morals* only" is corrected: both English editions are the 1785 *Groundwork*. The 1797 system and the first *Critique* remain the next Kant holes.
