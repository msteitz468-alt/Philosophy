# What Mary Didn't Know - Frank Jackson (1986)

> Frank Jackson's five-page reply to Paul Churchland: the knowledge argument is not about imagination, not about the intensionality of knowledge, and not about Mary's own experiences — it is about the **facts she lacks concerning other people**, and it survives all three of Churchland's objections.

**Full citation**: Frank Jackson, "What Mary Didn't Know," *The Journal of Philosophy* 83, no. 5 (May 1986): 291–295. A reply to Paul M. Churchland, "Reduction, Qualia, and the Direct Introspection of Brain States," *The Journal of Philosophy* 82, no. 1 (January 1985): 8–28. Jackson thanks David Lewis and Robert Pargetter.

## Summary

The paper restates the Mary case in its canonical short form. Mary is confined to a black-and-white room, educated through black-and-white books and lectures relayed on black-and-white television, and in this way learns **everything there is to know about the physical nature of the world** — everything in "completed physics, chemistry, and neurophysiology," plus all causal and relational facts consequent on it, "including of course functional roles." If physicalism is true she thereby knows all there is to know. On release she learns what it is like to see red: "This is rightly described as *learning* — she will not say 'ho, hum.'" Hence physicalism is false. This is the [[Arguments/Knowledge Argument (Jackson)|knowledge argument]] "in one of its manifestations"; the original is Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982), with [[Concepts/Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)|Nagel's bat]] and Howard Robinson's *Matter and Sense* cited alongside.

Before that, Jackson defines the target. Physicalism is "not the noncontroversial thesis that the actual world is largely physical, but the challenging thesis that it is **entirely** physical," which is why physicalists "must hold that complete physical knowledge is complete knowledge *simpliciter*." The supporting move is the world-comparison: if complete physical knowledge were not complete, our world would have to differ from a world *W(P)* for which it *is* complete, and since the two agree in all matters physical the difference would be nonphysical — so physicalism would be false at our world (though only contingently, since it holds at *W(P)*). A footnote fends off Terence Horgan: the claim is not that only what is said in explicitly physical language counts as knowledge, but that if physicalism is true, knowing everything expressible in explicitly physical language is knowing everything. See [[Concepts/Physicalism as Complete Physical Knowledge (Jackson)]].

**Three clarifications** (§I) — each removes a standard misreading:

1. **Not imagination.** The argument does not claim that one logically cannot imagine sensing red without having sensed it. "Powers of imagination are not to the point." The claim is that as a matter of fact Mary *would not know*; if physicalism were true she would, and no great imaginative powers would be needed. "Imagination is a faculty that those who *lack* knowledge need to fall back on."
2. **Not the intensionality of knowledge.** The argument does not assume that if *S* knows *a* is *F* and *a* = *b* then *S* knows *b* is *F*. The question is whether Mary's *total body* of knowledge before release is complete. A failure of logical alertness would be no threat to physicalism — but it is "very hard to believe" that her gap could be closed by drawing out further logical consequences. "On being let out, she will not say 'I could have worked all this out before by making some more purely logical inferences.'"
3. **Not her own experiences.** The knowledge she lacked that matters is **knowledge about the experiences of others**. Before release there were no facts about her own experience of red to know — "that physicalist and nonphysicalist alike can agree on" — and physicalism can happily grant that her brain states and their functional roles change afterwards. The trouble is that after seeing her first ripe tomato she realizes "how impoverished her conception of the mental life of *others* has been all along": their experiences had a feature conspicuous to them but hidden from her "(in fact, not in logic)," while she knew all the physical facts about them. So what she did not know is not a physical fact about their experiences, but it is a fact about them.

**Churchland's three objections and Jackson's replies** (§II):

*(i) Equivocation on 'knows about'.* Churchland's "conveniently tightened version" runs: (1) Mary knows everything there is to know about brain states and their properties; (2) Mary does not know everything there is to know about sensations and their properties; therefore, by Leibniz's Law, (3) sensations ≠ brain states. Churchland is right that this equivocates between knowledge by description and knowledge by acquaintance, and that it makes "a highly dubious use of Leibniz's law." Jackson's reply: that is convenient but **not the knowledge argument**. Premise 1 is false by the argument's own lights — Mary does *not* know everything about brain states, because she does not know the qualia associated with them. What is complete is her knowledge *of matters physical*. The accurate display is (1)′ Mary knows everything **physical** there is to know about other people; (2)′ Mary does not know everything there is to know about other people (she learns something about them on release); therefore (3)′ there are truths about other people (and herself) that escape the physicalist story. "What is immediately to the point is not the kind, manner, or type of knowledge Mary has, but *what* she knows."

The real challenge in the vicinity concerns the support for (2)′: the **Lewis–Nemirow ability analysis** — what Mary gains is a representational or imaginative ability, *knowledge how* rather than *knowledge that*, so a physicalist can grant that she acquires something significant without granting that her prior factual knowledge was defective. Jackson concedes she acquires abilities (to imagine seeing red, to remember it, to understand why her friends thought her deprived — "something which, until her release, had always mystified her") but denies that is *all*. The **other-minds skeptic** counter-case: suppose Mary heard a lecture on skepticism about other minds while incarcerated. On release she first says she now knows more about others' experiences, then remembers the lecture and worries whether she is generalizing wildly from one case, then decides she does know and that skepticism is mistaken. "What was she to-ing and fro-ing about — her abilities? Surely not; her representational abilities were a known constant throughout." There would be nothing to agonize about if ability were all she gained. See [[Concepts/Ability Analysis of Phenomenal Knowledge (Lewis-Nemirow)]].

Jackson then concedes the epistemic status of his own case: "I grant that I have no *proof* that Mary acquires on her release, as well as abilities, factual knowledge about the experiences of others." The claim is that the knowledge argument is "a valid argument from highly plausible, though admittedly not demonstrable, premises to the conclusion that physicalism is false. And that, after all, is about as good an objection as one could expect in this area of philosophy."

*(ii) It proves too much.* Churchland: if Mary received black-and-white televised lectures from a full-blown dualist about the "laws" governing "ectoplasm" and about qualia, she would still learn something on release — so the argument refutes dualism as well, and there is a parity of reasons. Jackson's reply: lectures about qualia over black-and-white television do not tell Mary *all* there is to know about qualia. They may tell her some things — that qualia do not appear in the physicalist's story, that "the quale we use 'yellow' for is nearly as different from the one we use 'blue' for as is white from black" — but there is no reason to think they tell her everything. By contrast it is plausible that such lectures could in principle convey **everything in the physicalist's story**: "You do not need color television to learn physics or functionalist psychology." To run the argument against dualism one would have to replace the premise that Mary has the full physicalist story with the premise that she has the full dualist story; the former is plausible, the latter is not. (The relevant dualism is *attribute* dualism — "ectoplasm is a bit of fun.")

*(iii) Imagination again.* Churchland reads the argument as claiming Mary could not even *imagine* the relevant experience and hence must be missing information. Jackson repeats clarification 1: the argument says she **would not know** what the experience is like. "If her knowledge is defective, despite being all there is to know according to physicalism, then physicalism is false, whatever her powers of imagination."

## Key Claims / Positions

- Physicalism is the thesis that the world is *entirely* physical; therefore complete physical knowledge must be complete knowledge simpliciter, or physicalism fails at our world.
- The knowledge argument's load-bearing premises concern **facts about other people**, not Mary's imagination, her logical acumen, or her own new experiences.
- Churchland's Leibniz's-Law reconstruction attacks a version Jackson does not hold; the accurate version never asserts that Mary's knowledge of brain states is complete.
- The Lewis–Nemirow ability analysis is the serious physicalist reply, and it is answered by the other-minds-skepticism case: abilities were constant throughout the episode Mary agonizes over.
- The argument is valid from plausible but non-demonstrable premises — Jackson explicitly disclaims proof.
- No parity of reasons against dualism: black-and-white lectures can plausibly deliver the whole physicalist story, but not the whole story about qualia.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Head-on collision with [[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]] — [[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]] denies premise (2)′: if Mary really has *all* physical information she already knows every reactive disposition, so the blue-banana trick fails to catch her out. Jackson's §I reply to Churchland is the same structural move Dennett attacks (Jackson insists the "physical" is genuinely complete and *still* leaves a fact out), which is why the two arguments cannot be reconciled by adding detail. Related: [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]].
- > [!warning] Jackson vs [[Thinkers/David Chalmers]] on strategy: Chalmers accepts the knowledge argument's conclusion but embeds it in a modal framework ([[Concepts/Logical Supervenience and Reductive Explanation (Chalmers)]]) and calls the Lewis–Nemirow ability analysis "deeply implausible" for the same reason Jackson does. Chalmers also files Jackson as Type-C property dualist in [[Concepts/Naturalistic Dualism (Chalmers)]]. Jackson himself, in this paper, argues only that physicalism is false — the positive metaphysics (epiphenomenal qualia) belongs to the 1982 paper, not this one.
- > [!warning] Fork with [[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]]: Jackson cites Nagel's bat as a fellow manifestation of the argument, but Nagel refuses the conclusion Jackson draws — for Nagel physicalism is *unintelligible*, not refuted ([[Concepts/Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)]]). Jackson's Mary is the version Dennett's four-feature qualia critique fits best.
- Open in this source: the metaphysics of the leftover facts. Jackson establishes only that some truths escape the physicalist story; whether they are epiphenomenal properties, and how they could then be known or spoken about, is not addressed here. That is the business of [[Sources/Epiphenomenal Qualia - Frank Jackson (1982)]].
- Not in this source, flagged for accuracy: Jackson later abandoned the knowledge argument's conclusion and became a physicalist. No vault page should present the 1986 position as his final view without a source establishing the reversal.
