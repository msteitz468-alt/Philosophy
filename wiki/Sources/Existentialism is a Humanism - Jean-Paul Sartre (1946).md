# Existentialism is a Humanism (1946)
> Sartre's public defence of atheistic existentialism: existence precedes essence, man is condemned to be free, and that freedom is a humanism of self-surpassing, not a cult of Man.

## Bibliographic Note

*L'existentialisme est un humanisme*. This edition: Philip Mairet translation in Walter Kaufmann, ed., *Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre* (Meridian, 1989; first published World Publishing Company, 1956); HTML markup Andy Blunden 1998, proofed 2005. The EPUB header dates the lecture 1946; [[Sources/Sartre - Ruud Welten (2020)]] dates it 1945. The usual resolution — delivered 1945, published 1946 — is recorded here as a dating note, not as a claim argued in either source.

This is the vault's first Sartre *primary*. The 2026-06-29 Welten biography already named the lecture and paraphrased three slogans; a companion PDF then sitting in `raw/` was a mis-saved university webpage and was not ingested. The present EPUB is the lecture text itself.

## Argument Spine

Existentialism is not quietism, pessimism, or caprice. Christian (Jaspers, Marcel) and atheistic (Heidegger, the French, Sartre) existentialists share one thesis: **existence precedes essence** — or, begin from the subjective. Atheistic existentialism draws the consequence that eighteenth-century atheism refused: if God does not exist there is no human nature, no *a priori* values, and man is nothing else but what he makes of himself. Choosing himself, he chooses an image of man for all; hence **anguish** (legislating for mankind), **abandonment** (God gone, no excuses: "condemned to be free"), and **despair** (count only on what is in one's will or the probabilities that make action feasible). The *cogito* is the starting point, but — contrary to Descartes and Kant — it discloses others as the condition of my existence (**inter-subjectivity**). Values are invented, not found; the only end consistent with freedom is freedom itself, including the freedom of others. The humanism at stake is not the cult of Man (Cocteau, Comte, Fascism) but the humanism of **self-surpassing**.

## Section-by-Section Summary

**Reproaches.** Communists: quietism of despair; pure Cartesian subjectivity that cannot reach solidarity. Catholics (Mlle. Mercier): neglect of charm and beauty; isolation. Christians generally: without God and eternal values, anything goes and no one can be condemned. Fashion has emptied the word "existentialist." Sartre's title answers all three: existentialism "does render human life possible" and affirms that every truth and action imply both an environment and a human subjectivity. What alarms people is not pessimism but **optimism** — that man is confronted with a possibility of choice.

**Two existentialisms; existence precedes essence.** Common thesis: existence before essence / begin from the subjective. Paper-knife: artisan's conception and technique precede the object — essence precedes existence. God as "supernal artisan" (Descartes, Leibniz) makes each man the realisation of a divine conception. Eighteenth-century atheism (Diderot, Voltaire, Kant) drops God but keeps **human nature** as a universal essence prior to historic existence — in Kant even the wild man, the man of nature, and the bourgeois share the same definition. Atheistic existentialism is more consistent: if God does not exist, at least one being exists before it can be defined. "Man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world — and defines himself afterwards." "Man is nothing else but that which he makes of himself." Responsibility is for *all* men: every choice creates an image of man as one believes he ought to be (the Christian vs Communist union; marriage and monogamy). → [[Concepts/Existence Precedes Essence (Sartre)]], [[Schools/Existentialism]]

**Anguish, abandonment, despair.** Anguish: the legislator for mankind; Kierkegaard's Abraham (who must decide whether the voice is an angel's); the military leader; not a bar to action but its condition. Those who shrug "everyone does not do so" are in self-deception. Abandonment (Heidegger's word): God does not exist, and the consequences must be drawn to the end. Secular "radicalism" (~1880) tried to keep *a priori* values after discarding God; existentialism finds that "there can no longer be any good *a priori*." Dostoevsky: "If God did not exist, everything would be permitted" — the starting point. "Man is free, man *is* freedom." "Condemned to be free." The pupil: mother vs Free French — neither Christian charity, nor Kant's Formula of Humanity, nor feeling, nor an adviser can decide; "You are free, therefore choose, that is to say, invent." The Jesuit who read his setbacks as a sign of vocation still chose the reading. Despair: rely only on what is within one's will or the relevant probabilities; Descartes's "Conquer yourself rather than the world" = act without hope. Marxists cannot be promised the revolution after one's death; "Fascism will then be the truth of man" if men so decide. Not quietism: "one need not hope in order to undertake one's work." → [[Concepts/Anguish, Abandonment and Despair (Sartre)]], [[Concepts/Radical Freedom and Situation (Sartre)]]

**Man is the sum of his actions.** No unused potentialities waiting behind a failed life. No love apart from deeds of love; Proust is his works; Racine is his tragedies. A coward is not born with a cowardly temperament; he makes himself a coward by giving up. "The coward makes himself cowardly, the hero makes himself heroic." What horrifies readers of *Les Chemins de la liberté* is responsibility, not baseness. An ethic of action and self-commitment; the sternness of optimism.

**Cogito, inter-subjectivity, human condition.** Starting point is the Cartesian *cogito* as the one absolute truth of consciousness attaining itself — otherwise all is merely probable. Materialism treats man as an object (pre-determined reactions, a table or stone). But "contrary to the philosophy of Descartes, contrary to that of Kant," the *cogito* attains the self *in the presence of the other*. "The other is indispensable to my existence, and equally so to any knowledge I can have of myself." The world of decision is **inter-subjectivity**. There is no universal *human nature*, but there is a universal **human condition**: being in the world, having to labour and to die. Limitations are both objective (everywhere) and subjective (nothing if not lived). Every purpose is comprehensible across cultures because each is an attempt to surpass, widen, deny, or accommodate those limits. Universality is made, not given. → [[Concepts/The Look and the Other (Sartre)]], [[Concepts/Cogito (Descartes)]]

**Three objections.** (1) Anarchy / "it does not matter what you do": not to choose is still a choice; Gide's *acte gratuit* is caprice without a situation; the moral choice is like constructing a work of art (no *a priori* picture) without being an aesthetic morality. (2) Unable to judge: true of sincere commitments (and Sartre denies progress: man is always the same facing a changing situation); false in that one can judge *error* (self-deception: hiding behind passions, determinism, or values that "impose themselves") and can judge *morally*: freedom can have no end but itself, and willing one's freedom requires willing others'. Cowards hide behind solemnity or determinism; scum try to show their existence is necessary. Content of morality is variable; a certain *form* is universal. Kant is right that freedom wills itself and others' freedom, wrong that the formal and universal suffice — the student still has to invent. Maggie Tulliver and La Sanseverina enact opposed moralities equivalent as free commitments. (3) Values are not serious if we invent them: if God the Father is excluded, someone must invent values; there is no sense in life *a priori*. → [[Concepts/Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) (Sartre)]], [[Concepts/Formula of Humanity as End in Itself (Kant)]]

**Two humanisms.** Cult-of-Man humanism (Cocteau's "Man is magnificent!"; Comte) ascribes to all men the deeds of some, ends in a shut-in cult and — "this must be said" — in Fascism. Existentialism never takes man as the end, "since man is still to be determined." The other sense: man is always outside himself; he exists by projecting and losing himself beyond himself. Transcendence here is self-surpassing, not God. "There is no legislator but himself." → [[Concepts/Existential Humanism (Sartre)]]

**Close.** Existentialism draws the full conclusions of a consistently atheistic position. Even if God existed that would make no difference: nothing can save man from himself. Optimistic; a doctrine of action.

## Whole-Work Assessment

The lecture closes the hole Outstanding Sources named: Sartre in the vault was a Welten biography. It supplies the *public* argument the biography only paraphrased — the paper-knife, the two atheisms, anguish/abandonment/despair as a defined triad, the pupil, the two humanisms, "condemned to be free," and the explicit reply to Communists and Christians. It also *corrects* the biography on two points. First, Welten's Sartre is an anti-humanist who refuses base values; the lecture distinguishes two humanisms and claims the self-surpassing one. Second, Welten's *Being and Nothingness* reading treats authenticity as impossible and withholds moral verdicts from the waiter; the lecture pronounces logical and moral judgments (error, cowards, scum) and names **good faith** as strict consistency in willing freedom. The *cogito*-as-intersubjectivity is also warmer than BN's "Hell is other people." *Being and Nothingness* is now ingested ([[Sources/Being and Nothingness - Jean-Paul Sartre (Barnes trans.)]], 2026-08-12). The lecture remains the public ethics (cowards, scum, will freedom including others'); the treatise remains the ontology, including the Conclusion's withheld ethics and the equivalence of drunkard and leader of nations. This is still the cheap on-ramp, not a substitute.
