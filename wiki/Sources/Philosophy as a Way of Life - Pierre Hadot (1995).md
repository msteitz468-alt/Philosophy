# Philosophy as a Way of Life — Pierre Hadot (1995)
> Ancient philosophy was not a system of theoretical discourse but a way of life: a conversion of existence practiced through spiritual exercises. Philosophical discourse is not philosophy itself.

**Full citation**: Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life: Spiritual Exercises from Socrates to Foucault*, ed. Arnold I. Davidson, trans. Michael Chase (Blackwell, 1995). Based on the 2nd edition of *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique* (Études Augustiniennes, Paris 1987). ISBN 0-631-18033-8 (pbk). 321 pp. OCR scan.

**Author**: Pierre Hadot (1922–2010). École pratique des Hautes Études (chair in Latin Patristics, later "Theologies and Mysticisms of Hellenistic Greece"); Collège de France, History of Hellenistic and Roman Thought (from 1983). Translator/commentator of Marius Victorinus, Porphyry, Ambrose, Plotinus, and Marcus Aurelius. See [[Thinkers/Pierre Hadot]].

## Argument Spine

From Socrates to late antiquity, *philosophia* was a choice of life and a set of spiritual exercises (*askēsis*) aimed at transforming the self — not a body of theory discussed in classrooms. The schools (Platonic, Aristotelian, Stoic, Epicurean, Cynic, Skeptic) offered rival conversions but shared the same structure: discourse *about* philosophy (logic, physics, ethics as taught) is not philosophy itself, which is the unitary act of living logic, physics, and ethics. Literary genre, school setting, and oral practice determine what an ancient text *is*; read as modern treatises they look badly written. Christianity inherited the form (early Christianity *was* a philosophy). The medieval university, and then Suárez, made systematic discourse the whole of philosophy. Goethe, Nietzsche, Kierkegaard, Wittgenstein, and Foucault are partial modern recoveries; Foucault's "cultivation of the self" is too aesthetic. Recovering the ancient model is both a historical claim and a proposal for philosophy now.

## Key Claims / Positions

- Ancient *philosophia* is a choice of a form of life; discourse *about* philosophy is not philosophy.
- Spiritual exercises transform vision and being; *askēsis* ≠ asceticism; Ignatius is a Christian version of a Greco-Roman tradition.
- Logic / physics / ethics cut up *discourse*; lived philosophy is a unitary act (desire = physics, action = ethics, assent = logic).
- Ancient texts look badly written because they are school products meant to form, not inform.
- Creative mistakes (*contresens*) in the exegesis of authentic texts generate new philosophy.
- The *Meditations* are methodical *hypomnemata* on Epictetus’s three *topoi*, not a gloomy diary.
- Early Christianity *was* a philosophy (Apologist–Origenist–monastic line); the final synthesis is essentially Christian.
- Foucault recovered the art of living and aimed it at the self; Hadot aims it at the Whole.
- Modern university philosophy is usually a professor’s form of life. Ancient attitudes remain usable once stripped of outmoded cosmology.

## Whole-Work Assessment

This is the missing primary Holiday imported and the vault listed as an outstanding hole. Until this ingest, Hadot existed only as a name on Holiday pages: three disciplines, inner citadel, “philosophy as a way of life.” The book supplies the argument, the vocabulary, and the fight Holiday merged away.

Three structural consequences. **(1)** The Stoic-practice cluster is now three-layered: Epictetus (primary *topoi*) → Hadot (lived physics/ethics/logic; spiritual exercises) → Holiday (Perception/Action/Will as self-help). Holiday’s order tracks Marcus’s *present* triad, not Epictetus’s school order; “Will” flattens the discipline of desire. **(2)** Foucault’s late ethics, previously only from Macey, now has its named interlocutor. The new contradiction page states both sides: techniques of the self vs cosmic universalization. **(3)** The vault gains a Socrates page without inventing a biography — Hadot’s *figure* — and a metaphilosophical pair (way of life; creative mistakes) that also licenses how the wiki should read ancient sources.

The Argument Spine from Step 1 held. Corrections from the essays: “inner citadel” is the title of the 1992 Marcus book, not a slogan in this volume; Hadot does not use *amor fati*; he does not say academic philosophy *cannot* be a way of life, only that it usually is a professor’s; Wittgenstein appears in the interview, not in ch. 11; *The Inner Citadel* itself remains uningested.
