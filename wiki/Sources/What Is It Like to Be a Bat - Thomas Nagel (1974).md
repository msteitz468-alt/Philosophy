# What Is It Like to Be a Bat - Thomas Nagel (1974)

> Thomas Nagel's 1974 essay — ingested here in the 2024 Oxford reprint, with a new preface and the 2023 lecture "Further Thoughts: The Psychophysical Nexus" — argues that the **subjective character of experience** ("what it is like" for an organism) is the feature that makes the mind-body problem intractable, and that no available reduction of the mental to the physical even addresses it.

**Full citation**: Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", *The Philosophical Review* 83 (October 1974). Reprinted with a 2024 preface and the 2023 lecture "Further Thoughts: The Psychophysical Nexus" as *What Is It Like to Be a Bat?* (Oxford University Press, 2024).

## Summary

The 1974 essay opens: "Consciousness is what makes the mind-body problem really intractable." Reductionist analogies (water–H2O, lightning–electrical discharge, gene–DNA, Turing machine–IBM machine) fail because they leave out the one feature that makes this reduction unique. An organism has conscious mental states **if and only if there is something it is like to *be* that organism** — something it is like *for* the organism. Functional, causal, and intentional analyses are logically compatible with the absence of this subjective character; they could be ascribed to robots that "behaved like people though they experienced nothing."

The bat is chosen because it is a mammal we already credit with experience, yet its primary sense — Microchiropteran sonar — is so alien that imagination cannot carry us from our case to its. Imagining webbing, poor vision, and high-frequency shrieks tells us only what it would be like for *us* to behave as a bat behaves, not what it is like for a *bat* to be a bat. Extrapolation from our own case is therefore incompletable. We can ascribe general *types* of experience (pain, fear, hunger, three-dimensional forward perception) but not the specific subjective character. The same barrier holds, less vividly, between one person and another (the congenitally deaf-blind), and would hold between us and intelligent Martians who tried to conceive what it is like to be us. That we cannot describe bat phenomenology is no reason to deny that it is as richly specific as ours: "to deny the reality or logical significance of what we can never describe or understand is the crudest form of cognitive dissonance." Nagel's realism about the subjective domain implies **facts beyond the reach of human concepts**.

The "point of view" in question is a *type*, not a private inner object. Phenomenological facts can be ascribed objectively by someone similar enough to adopt the subject's point of view; they are subjective in that even this ascription requires that first-person uptake. Physical facts, by contrast, are the kind that can be observed from many points of view. Reduction in other sciences is a move toward greater objectivity — abandoning a species-specific appearance (the look of lightning, the sound of a wave) to reach a common reality. Experience does not fit the pattern: "What would be left of what it was like to be a bat if one removed the viewpoint of the bat?" Shifting toward objectivity takes us *farther* from the phenomenon, not nearer. The seeds of the objection are already in successful reductions: when sound is identified with waves, the auditory viewpoint is left behind, unreduced.

Nagel does **not** conclude that physicalism is false. "It would be a mistake to conclude that physicalism must be false. … It would be truer to say that physicalism is a position we cannot understand because we do not at present have any conception of how it might be true." The word "is" in "mental events are physical events" is deceptively clear; without a theoretical framework showing how the two referential paths could converge, the identification has "an air of mysticism." Physicalism today is like "matter is energy" uttered by a pre-Socratic. We may have evidence for something we cannot understand (the caterpillar-in-the-safe / butterfly analogy; Davidson's anomalous monism as a gesture in this direction). The more fundamental unanswered question is whether experiences have an **objective character** at all.

The essay closes with a speculative proposal: an **objective phenomenology** that would describe the subjective character of experiences in a form comprehensible to beings incapable of having them — beginning with human cases (explaining seeing to the congenitally blind) rather than loose intermodal analogies ("Red is like the sound of a trumpet"). Structural features of perception might be so describable even if something is left out. Until more thought is given to the subjective/objective problem, "we cannot even pose the mind-body problem without sidestepping it."

The 2024 preface corrects a standard misreading: Nagel is not saying consciousness is beyond scientific understanding, only that science must expand beyond the physical sciences' shared-observation model of an objective external reality. He records the essay's second life in the science of **animal consciousness** (conversations with Donald Griffin at Rockefeller, 1973–74, feeding Griffin's *The Question of Animal Awareness*, 1976). He acknowledges Timothy Sprigge ("something it is like to be," 1971) and B. A. Farrell ("What would it be like to be a bat?", 1950).

The 2023 lecture, "Further Thoughts: The Psychophysical Nexus," is the constructive sequel. Nagel now holds that **natural language is conceptually dualist** (mental vs physical kinds; zombie conceivability as Descartes's argument), that every reductionist analysis of mental concepts fails because none entails inner consciousness, and that he nonetheless **resists metaphysical dualism** — substance *and* property. The hoped-for truth is **monism**: mental and neurophysiological properties as inseparable aspects of a more fundamental kind for which we lack a concept (Spinoza, neutral monism, dual-aspect theory). Conceivability of dualism would then be an artifact of incomplete concepts, as the temperature–pressure correlation of a gas is necessary once both are grounded in molecular kinetic energy. The guess: conscious experiences have something analogous to **essential, unmediated behavioral manifestations in the CNS** — they *are* what the immediate causes of those manifestations feel like from the inside. Pain and thirst, at the organism level, have contingent peripheral causes and effects; at a central state those dispositions become intrinsic. Experience and physical state would be necessary and sufficient for each other without either causing the other. This uses functionalism's causal-role facts as *explananda*, not as a conceptual analysis. The view is **hostage** to the condition that consciousness (or something that entails it) is essential to the explanation of behavior; if a complete physical story of the behavioral sequence is available, property dualism plus epiphenomenal laws would be equally good. Start with affect (Solms: the core of consciousness is valence, not perception or cognition). This is "not equivalent to panpsychism. Panpsychism is, in effect, dualism all the way down. This is monism all the way down."

## Key Claims / Positions

- Conscious mental states exist iff there is something it is like for the organism — the subjective character of experience.
- No current reductive analysis (functional, causal, intentional, behavioral) captures that character; all are compatible with its absence.
- Facts about what it is like embody a point of view (a type, not a private atom). Physical objectivity is the wrong direction of travel for explaining them.
- Physicalism is not thereby shown false; it is a hypothesis we cannot yet understand.
- An objective phenomenology is the next methodological step (1974).
- Conceptual dualism is real; metaphysical dualism should still be resisted in favor of a not-yet-formulated psychophysical monism (2023).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Direct clash with [[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]] — see [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]]. Dennett treats the "what it's like" residue as a Cartesian Theater illusion; Nagel treats it as the datum any theory must explain. The 1974 essay already names Dennett's *Content and Consciousness* (1969) among the reductionist targets.
- > [!warning] Fork with [[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]: both take subjectivity as irreducible and both use zombie conceivability, but Nagel's 2023 lecture *refuses* the step from conceptual dualism to [[Concepts/Naturalistic Dualism (Chalmers)|property dualism]] and hopes for monism instead. Chalmers's [[Concepts/Double-Aspect Theory of Information (Chalmers)|double-aspect information theory]] is the nearest constructive cousin; Nagel's monism is closer still to Spinoza than to Maxwell-style extra laws.
- The 2023 program is avowedly speculative and "hostage" to consciousness doing causal work. If a closed physical story of behavior is available, Nagel grants that epiphenomenal property dualism becomes equally live — the same worry Chalmers prices as the [[Concepts/Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment (Chalmers)]].
