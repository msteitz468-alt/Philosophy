# Parerga and Paralipomena, Volume 1 — Arthur Schopenhauer (1851; Roehr/Janaway, Cambridge 2014)

> Subordinate works and things left aside: the popular companion to *The World as Will and Representation*, and the book that finally made Schopenhauer famous. Volume 1 is the *parerga* — intelligible without the system, though the system lights it from afar.

## Argument Spine

*Parerga und Paralipomena* (completed June 1850; published 1851 by A. W. Hayn of Berlin, 750 copies, no honorarium) is Schopenhauer’s *opera mixta*: thoughts that “could find no place in my systematic works,” “incomparably more popular than everything up till now.” The unpublished draft preface assigns Volume 1 to the *parerga* (comprehensible without the system) and most of Volume 2 to the *paralipomena* (supplements that presuppose *WWR*). This Cambridge volume is **Volume 1 only** (Hübscher *SW* 5; Roehr/Janaway 2014). Do not treat it as Volume 2, and do not create a Janaway or Roehr thinker page.

The six essays are not one argument. They share a stance: Descartes opened the problem of the **ideal and the real** (what in cognition is subjective vs independently existing); half a dozen European minds worked it; Kant made the cut; Schopenhauer claims to have **solved** it — the ideal is the world as representation, “the will alone is left as the real.” Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel are sophists, not philosophers, and university philosophy is philosophy for hire. Two speculative essays then apply the system sideways (transcendent fatalism; spirit-seeing as *idealistic*, not spiritualistic). The closing **Aphorisms on the wisdom of life** are an *accommodation*: eudaemonology from the ordinary empirical standpoint the higher metaphysics has already judged false. He does not call his own philosophy “pessimism.”

## Whole-Work Assessment

The book that made Schopenhauer famous is, by his own unpublished draft preface, the *parerga*: intelligible without the system, lit by it from afar. Four things reception had flattened are now sharp.

(1) **He claims to have solved Descartes’ problem, not to have written a miscellany.** The ideal is representation; “the will alone is left as the real.” Locke–Hume–Kant–Schopenhauer is the line; Fichte/Schelling/Hegel are sophists who abolished or identified the cut. History is written as anticipation of himself (Eleatics, Empedocles). File that as *Schopenhauer on X*.

(2) **Fragments §13 is the load-bearing Kant.** Transcendent ≠ transcendental. The thing-in-itself is true; Kant’s causal introduction of it is not (Schulze). Moral theology is a “sop.” Theism undermines morality and *aseity*. His own system is **immanent dogmatism**, “only the thinking-through-to-the-end” of Kant. The university essay is the same fight as a polemic: philosophy for hire, Hegel as philistinism, teach logic + Thales-to-Kant.

(3) **The two speculative essays apply the system sideways.** Demonstrable fatalism (necessity of acts) is already the 1841 freedom essay; transcendent fatalism (a life composed) is labeled fantasy; the will is the secret impresario, by analogy with dream. *Amor fati* is absent. Spirit-seeing treats Mesmer’s facts as facts and refuses spirits: the explanation is *idealistic*.

(4) **The Aphorisms are an accommodation.** Eudaemonology is a euphemism for instructions to a happy existence written from the error the system has judged. What one is outweighs what one has and represents. Stoic techniques are borrowed; the dichotomy of control is not named; Holiday is absent. He does not call his philosophy “pessimism.”

The Argument Spine of Step 1 held. Corrections of emphasis: Fragments §§1–12 are scholarly and sour, not a second history-of-philosophy page; “On Women” is Volume 2 (Janaway’s mention, not a chapter here); the 1841 *Prize Essay* and *WWR* II remain uningested and are cited as already shown.
