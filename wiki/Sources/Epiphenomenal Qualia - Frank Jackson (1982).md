# Epiphenomenal Qualia - Frank Jackson (1982)

> Frank Jackson's ten-page *Philosophical Quarterly* paper: a **qualia freak** who thinks no amount of physical information captures the hurtfulness of pain or the look of the sky; the **knowledge argument** (Fred's extra colour, then Mary) is the polemically strongest case that Physicalism is false; and the standard reasons for refusing **epiphenomenal** qualia have no real force.

**Full citation**: Frank Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia," *The Philosophical Quarterly* 32, no. 127 (April 1982): 127–136. Written at Monash University. Thanks Robert Pargetter; §IV is indebted to Alec Hyslop and John Lucas, and (despite dissent) to Paul E. Meehl, "The Compleat Autocerebroscopist."

## Summary

Jackson opens by tagging the output of the physical, chemical, and biological sciences — and whatever "automatically comes along with it," including the **functional role** of neural states — as **physical information**. He does not offer a precise definition; the problems of defining Physicalism "cut across the central problems I want to discuss." He is a "qualia freak": tell him everything physical about a living brain, "the kind of states, their functional role, their relation to what goes on at other times and in other brains," and "you won't have told me about the hurtfulness of pains, the itchiness of itches, pangs of jealousy, or about the characteristic experience of tasting a lemon, smelling a rose, hearing a loud noise or seeing the sky."

Some qualia freaks treat the rejection of Physicalism as an unargued intuition. Jackson thinks they already have an argument — nothing physical captures the smell of a rose, therefore Physicalism is false — but it is **weak polemically**, because many do not find the premise obvious. The task is to find premises obvious to as many as possible. That is §I, the [[Arguments/Knowledge Argument (Jackson)|Knowledge argument]]. §II contrasts it with the **Modal argument** (physical replicas with no conscious life). §III distinguishes it from Nagel's [[Concepts/Subjective Character of Experience (Nagel)|"What is it like to be"]] argument. §IV answers "the bogey of epiphenomenalism": the main reason people refuse qualia is the belief that they would have to cause things in the brain, "and it is hard to do this without sounding like someone who believes in fairies."

**§I. The Knowledge argument.** First **Fred**: he sorts ripe tomatoes into two consistent groups the rest of us cannot even begin to make, and reports two colours, red₁ and red₂, "as different from each other and all the other colours as yellow is from blue." Physiology can in principle explain the extra discrimination (two groups of wavelengths, or an extra cone). We should admit he sees at least one more colour than we can; we are to Fred as a totally red-green colour-blind person is to us. To refuse this is to make the mistake of the villagers in Wells's "The Country of the Blind." Yet "no amount of physical information about Fred's brain and optical system tells us" what the new colour is like. We may know everything about his body, behaviour, dispositions, internal physiology, history, and relations that can be given in physical accounts — "we have all the physical information. Therefore, knowing all this is not knowing everything about Fred." If we later transplanted his optical system, people would say "at last we will know what it is like to see the extra colour"; so it cannot be that we knew all along all about Fred that the physicalist scheme contains.

Fred is "essentially [a] rhetorical device." The same point can be made with **Mary**: a brilliant scientist forced to investigate the world from a black-and-white room via a black-and-white television monitor, who acquires "all the physical information there is to obtain" about seeing ripe tomatoes or the sky — "just which wave-length combinations from the sky stimulate the retina, and exactly how this produces via the central nervous system the contraction of the vocal chords and expulsion of air from the lungs that results in the uttering of the sentence 'The sky is blue.'" (If this information could not in principle be obtained from black-and-white television, "the Open University would of necessity need to use colour television.") When she is released or given a colour monitor, "it seems just obvious that she will learn something about the world and our visual experience of it." Her previous knowledge was incomplete; she had all the physical information; "Ergo there is more to have than that, and Physicalism is false." The same style of argument covers taste, hearing, bodily sensations, "raw feels, phenomenal features or qualia." The polemical strength is that "it is so hard to deny the central claim that one can have all the physical information without having all the information there is to have."

**§II. The Modal argument.** No amount of physical information about another *logically entails* that he or she is conscious. So there is a possible world of organisms exactly like us in every physical respect (including functional states and physical history) with "no conscious mental life at all." What we have and they lack is not physical; Physicalism is false. Jackson presents it inter-world rather than intra-world "to avoid inessential complications to do with supervenience, causal anomalies and the like." The objection that Physicalism is advanced only as a contingent truth does not help: the argument is aimed at that lesser claim. The real trouble is that the modal intuition is **disputed** (Kirk recanted; Shoemaker argues against it). Head-counting is a poor method, but "frequently we can do no better when modal intuitions are in question," and the goal was polemical utility. Protagonists of the Knowledge argument may accept the modal intuition, but as a *consequence* of already having an argument that qualia are left out, not as its ground. There is a further complication: the physical–qualia link might be like that sometimes held between aesthetic and natural qualities — worlds alike in all natural respects (including experiences) must agree aesthetically, yet the aesthetic is not reduced to the natural.

**§III. The "What is it like to be" argument.** Jackson is "much indebted" to Nagel (1974) and notes that by the end of that article Nagel is objecting "not so much to Physicalism as to all extant theories of mind for ignoring points of view, including those that admit (irreducible) qualia." The arguments must still be distinguished. When Jackson complained that physical knowledge about Fred did not tell us what his special colour experience was like, he was **not** complaining that we weren't finding out what it is like to *be* Fred. He was complaining of a **property of the experience**. Even if we come to know that property, "we still will not know what it is like to be Fred, but we will know more about him." No amount of knowledge about Fred, physical or not, is knowledge "from the inside" — the *de se* items expressed by "that it is I myself who is …" (Lewis). Physicalist and qualia freak alike should grant the second; Jackson's complaint concerned the first. Nagel speaks as if the problem is extrapolating from familiar to unfamiliar experience (Hume's missing shade of blue). "It is hard to see an objection to Physicalism here. Physicalism makes no special claims about the imaginative or extrapolative powers of human beings." If Physicalism were true, enough physical information about Fred "would obviate any need to extrapolate"; the information would already be in our possession. "But it clearly isn't. That was the nub of the argument." (Nemirow's review of *Mortal Questions* and a discussion with Lewis are cited.)

**§IV. The bogey of epiphenomenalism.** See [[Concepts/Epiphenomenal Qualia (Jackson)]]. Jackson defends only that *certain properties* of certain mental states — qualia — can be such that their possession or absence makes no difference to the physical world. He does not defend the classical claims that mental *states* are inefficacious, or that the mental is totally inefficacious; "it may be that you have to hold that the instantiation of qualia makes a difference to other mental states though not to anything physical," and awareness of qualia "suggest[s] such a position." Three standard reasons for insisting that (e.g.) the hurtfulness of pain must make a difference in the brain are answered: (i) the "just obvious" causal role can be a common-cause illusion (Lee Marvin's fist and John Wayne's head on the screen; the projector, not the first image, causes the second); (ii) Darwin requires only that an evolved trait be conducive to survival *or a by-product* of one that is (polar bears' heavy coats); (iii) knowledge of other minds can run from behaviour back to a common brain-cause and out again to qualia (*The Times* and *The Telegraph* both report that Spurs won). The leftover complaint — they are an "excrescence," we cannot understand the how and why of them — is granted and then turned: we are products of evolution; epiphenomenal qualia are "totally irrelevant to survival"; Physicalism is "an extremely optimistic view of our powers." The **sea-slugist** parable: intelligent deep-ocean slugs whose tough-minded philosophers insist their restricted terms suffice for everything, and who correctly note that no slugist has ever spelled out how the mysterious residue fits their successful sciences. Super-beings might stand to us as we stand to the slugs.

The 1986 sequel [[Sources/What Mary Didn't Know - Frank Jackson (1986)]] restates Mary, defines Physicalism by completeness, and answers Churchland. It does not re-argue epiphenomenalism.

## Key Claims / Positions

- Physical information includes the sciences *and* functional role; even so, it leaves out qualia.
- The Knowledge argument (Fred, then Mary) is the polemically strongest case that Physicalism is false: all the physical information is not all the information.
- The Modal argument (zombies) is accepted by Jackson but is weaker because the modal intuition is disputed.
- Nagel's argument is a different complaint (point of view / *de se* / imaginative extrapolation), not a better objection to Physicalism.
- Qualia can be causally impotent with respect to the physical world; they may still affect other mental states and are known to the subject.
- Failure to understand how they fit the scientific world-picture is what evolution predicts, not a refutation.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Head-on collision with [[Thinkers/Daniel Dennett]] — [[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]] denies that Mary learns a fact (she already knows every reactive disposition) and treats strong epiphenomenal qualia as incoherent (parity / gremlins). Jackson's §IV is *not* Dennett's "undetectable even by introspection" sense: the subject is aware of qualia, and they may cause other mental states. The live fight is whether a property that makes no physical difference can still be a fact Mary learns and reports. See [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]].
- > [!warning] Fork with [[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]]: Jackson is indebted to the 1974 essay and then *separates* his argument from it. Nagel refuses the conclusion that Physicalism is false; Jackson draws it. Nagel's 2023 monism is hostage to consciousness doing causal work — the option Jackson's §IV says we need not take.
- > [!warning] Near-ally, different cost-accounting, with [[Thinkers/David Chalmers]]: Chalmers adopts the knowledge argument as one of five and files Jackson as Type-C, then treats epiphenomenalism as a *worry* to be lived with or escaped via Russellian intrinsicality ([[Concepts/Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment (Chalmers)]], [[Concepts/Naturalistic Dualism (Chalmers)]]). Jackson treats epiphenomenalism as a "perfectly possible" view and the expected result of selection.
- Not in this source, flagged for accuracy: Jackson later abandoned the knowledge argument's conclusion and became a physicalist. No vault page should present the 1982 position as his final view without a source establishing the reversal.
