# The Extended Mind - Andy Clark and David Chalmers (1998)

> Clark and Chalmers's thirteen-page *Analysis* paper: the mind is not bounded by skin and skull. Where an external resource is reliably coupled and does the work a brain process would, it is part of the cognitive process — and, in the Otto case, part of the mind.

**Full citation**: Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind," *Analysis* 58, no. 1 (January 1998): 7–19. Authors listed "in order of degree of belief in the central thesis." Written at Washington University in St. Louis and the University of California, Santa Cruz.

## Summary

The opening question is "Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?" Two standard replies are refused. The first accepts the skin/skull boundary. The second takes Putnam/Burge externalism about *meaning* ("just ain't in the head") and carries it over into an externalism about mind. Clark and Chalmers propose a **third position**: [[Concepts/Active Externalism (Clark and Chalmers)|active externalism]], "based on the active role of the environment in driving cognitive processes."

**§2. Extended cognition.** Three problem-solving cases, later identified as Tetris options:

1. Mental rotation of on-screen shapes to judge fit with sockets.
2. The same task, but the subject may physically rotate the image with a button (faster).
3. A "cyberpunk future" implant that performs the rotation internally, still chosen against ordinary mental rotation.

Case (3) is "clearly" on a par with (1). Case (2) has "the same sort of computational structure as case (3), distributed across agent and computer instead of internalized." If the implant rotation is cognitive, "by what right do we count case (2) as fundamentally different?" Pointing to skin/skull "is precisely what is at issue."

The exotic framing is then withdrawn. Human reasoners already "lean heavily on environmental supports": pen-and-paper long multiplication (McClelland et al. 1986; Clark 1989), Scrabble-tile rearrangement (Kirsh 1995), the nautical slide rule (Hutchins 1995), "the general paraphernalia of language, books, diagrams, and culture." The brain does some operations; others are delegated. Kirsh and Maglio (1994) time Tetris: physical 90° rotation ~100 ms plus ~200 ms to select the button, versus ~1000 ms for mental rotation. Physical rotation is used not only to position a piece but to *determine* compatibility — an [[Concepts/Epistemic Action (Kirsh and Maglio)|epistemic action]], which "alter[s] the world so as to aid and augment cognitive processes such as recognition and search," as against pragmatic actions that change the world for their own sake (cement in a dam).

Epistemic action "demands spread of epistemic credit." That yields the [[Concepts/Parity Principle (Clark and Chalmers)|parity principle]]: if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would unhesitatingly count as cognitive, "then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." Slogan: "Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!"

**§3. Active externalism.** Organism plus external entity in two-way interaction is a **coupled system** that "can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right." All components play an active causal role; remove the external component and competence drops "just as it would if we removed part of its brain."

This is not Putnam (1975) / Burge (1979). Twin-Earth differences are **distal and historical**; features of the present do not drive the process; twins behave indistinguishably. Those external features are **passive**. In the cases here, external features are **in the loop**, "not dangling at the other end of a long causal chain." Change them while holding internal structure fixed and behaviour may change completely — so the standard complaint that Putnam/Burge content is explanatorily idle does not apply. Footnote 2: much of the *appeal* of content externalism may actually be the appeal of active externalism; the more relevant question is "is mind in the present?"

Explanatory payoff: Scrabble-tile rearrangement is "not part of action; it is part of thought." Internalist paraphrase as a long series of inputs and actions is "needlessly complex." The same methods once reserved for inner processes should now be used on the outer. Supporting research: situated cognition (Suchman 1987), real-world robotics (Beer 1989), dynamical child development (Thelen and Smith 1994), distributed cognition (Hutchins 1995); philosophical kin in Haugeland 1995, McClamrock 1995, Varela, Thompson and Rosch 1991, Wilson 1994.

Two objections:

- **Consciousness.** Identifying the cognitive with the conscious would keep cognition in the head, but "not every cognitive process, at least on standard usage, is a conscious process." Memory retrieval, linguistic processes, and skill acquisition already run beyond consciousness. That external processes are external where consciousness is internal is "no reason to deny that those processes are cognitive." (Experiences themselves may still be internally determined — see §4.)
- **Portability / the Naked Mind.** Real cognition, on this objection, is the portable package we always bring; coupled systems "are too easily decoupled." Reply: the brain-and-body package is of interest, and may include fingers as working memory, but "mere contingency of coupling does not rule out cognitive status." A future plug-in memory module would be cognitive while plugged in. The real moral is **reliable coupling**, not location. A slide rule on the belt, a pocket calculator, a Filofax that is "always there when I need [it]" is "coupling enough." Discrete damage, loss, sleep, intoxication, and emotion already afflict the biological brain. Evolution, they add, has "favoured on-board capacities which are especially geared to parasitizing the local environment" — visual systems that exploit scene structure (Ullman and Richards 1984) and bodily motion (Blake and Yuille 1992). Language may have evolved "in part, to enable such extensions." Individual learning moulds the brain to complement external structures (school artifacts, professional tools). Extended cognition is therefore "a core cognitive process, not an add-on extra."

**§4. From cognition to mind.** Processing in the environment might be conceded while "truly mental states — experiences, beliefs, desires, emotions" stay in the brain. "We propose to take things a step further." Experiences "may be determined internally"; **beliefs** need not be. See [[Arguments/Otto and Inga (Clark and Chalmers)]].

Inga hears of an exhibition at MoMA, recalls that it is on 53rd Street, and walks there. She believed this before consulting memory; it was standing, not occurrent. Otto, with Alzheimer's, carries a notebook everywhere, writes new information, looks up old. He hears of the same exhibition, consults the notebook ("53rd Street"), and walks there. The notebook "plays the role usually played by a biological memory." Otto believed the museum was on 53rd Street *before* consulting the notebook, just as Inga did. The alternative — Otto has no belief until he looks, or only the belief that the museum is at the address in the notebook — is "unnatural." To point to the notebook in every belief/desire explanation is "to take one step too many," "pointlessly complex" in the same way as explaining Inga via beliefs about her memory. "In an explanation, simplicity is power."

**Twin Otto** wrote 51st Street by mistake. He is a physical duplicate of Otto from the skin in; his notebook differs; he walks to 51st. "A belief is simply not in the head." Unlike Putnam/Burge twins, these twins produce *different* behaviour; the external difference is dynamically relevant. Footnote 4 (Chalmers, forthcoming): Putnam/Burge twins differ only in relational/secondary-intension content; Otto and Twin Otto differ in **notional** (primary-intension) content, "the sort of content that governs cognition," because the cognitive system itself now includes the notebook.

What makes information a belief is **the role it plays**, "and there is no reason why the relevant role can be played only from inside the body." They do not debate "standard usage"; the broader notion "picks out something more akin to a natural kind."

Objections treated as shallow:

- **Reliability.** Someone might steal the notebook; a surgeon might tamper with Inga's brain, or she might drink too much. One-off consultation of a guidebook would not yield a standing belief; Otto's constant use does. Occasional unavailability (shower, darkness) is like Inga asleep or intoxicated.
- **Bandwidth.** Inga's memory–central link is higher-bandwidth than Otto's visual access. Lucy, with a low-grade biological link from "nonstandard biology or past misadventures," still believes. Struggle or a psychotherapist's aid would start to undermine ascription; Otto is not in that range.
- **Perception vs introspection.** From the standpoint of the coupled system, notebook-to-brain flow "is not perceptual at all"; it is "more akin to information flow within the brain." The only deep difference is **phenomenology**. Why should that decide belief-status? The Terminator recalls by displaying information in his visual field; that "unusual" phenomenology "surely makes little difference" to standing-belief status.
- **Occurrent-only beliefs.** The most consistent way to deny Otto also denies Inga's standing memory and most everyday belief ascription. Once dispositional beliefs are in, "it is difficult to resist the conclusion that Otto's notebook has all the relevant dispositions."

**§5. Beyond the outer limits.** Puzzle cases (Márquez's labelled village; a Filofax; a tampered notebook; an unread page; the Internet) get no "categorical answers." Four features make the central case clear: (1) the notebook is a **constant** — Otto rarely takes relevant action without consulting it; (2) information is **directly available without difficulty**; (3) he **automatically endorses** what he retrieves; (4) the information was **consciously endorsed in the past** and is there as a consequence. Feature (4) is "arguable" (subliminal perception, memory tampering). The first three "certainly play a crucial role." The Internet "is likely to fail on multiple counts, unless I am unusually computer-reliant, facile with the technology, and trusting"; certain computer files may qualify. Intermediate cases may be indeterminate or context-sensitive; "any indeterminacy here does not mean that in the central cases, the answer is not clear."

**Socially extended cognition** is allowed "in principle": an unusually interdependent couple; a favourite waiter as repository of meal-beliefs (even "extended desire"); secretary, accountant, collaborator. What is central is "a high degree of trust, reliance, and accessibility." Language carries most of the coupling. Without it "we might be much more akin to discrete Cartesian 'inner' minds." Language is "not a mirror of our inner states but a complement to them" — a tool that extends cognition "in ways that on-board devices cannot." The recent "intellectual explosion" may be due as much to this linguistically-enabled extension as to inner development.

**Extended self.** Dispositional beliefs already outstrip consciousness; if so, "these boundaries may also fall beyond the skin." Otto himself is "an extended system, a coupling of biological organism and external resources." Resisting this shrinks the self to "a mere bundle of occurrent states, severely threatening its deep psychological continuity." Moral and social consequences: "interfering with someone's environment will have the same moral significance as interfering with their person"; some social activity is "less akin to communication and action, and as more akin to thought." Closing line: "once the hegemony of skin and skull is usurped, we may be able to see ourselves more truly as creatures of the world."

## Key Claims / Positions

- A third externalism: **active**, causal, here-and-now coupling, not Putnam/Burge historical content.
- The **parity principle** is the criterion; skin/skull cannot be primitive.
- **Epistemic actions** spread cognitive credit into the world.
- **Reliable coupling**, not portability or biological location, marks the core package.
- Experiences "may be determined internally"; **beliefs** (and the self) need not be.
- Otto's notebook entries are standing beliefs because they play the same explanatory role as Inga's memories.
- Twin Otto shows a belief "is simply not in the head" in a dynamically relevant sense.
- Four rough conditions (constancy, availability, automatic endorsement, past endorsement) constrain how far the thesis goes; the 1998 Internet typically fails them.
- Language is a complement to inner states, and socially extended minds are possible in principle.
- Consciousness is **not** claimed to extend.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Allies on cognition, opponents on consciousness: the same two authors later split. Clark's *Experience Machine* deflates the hard problem ([[Concepts/Predictive Processing and the Hard Problem (Clark)]]); Chalmers's *The Conscious Mind* treats phenomenal consciousness as non-supervenient ([[Concepts/Hard Problem of Consciousness (Chalmers)]], [[Contradictions/Dennett vs Nagel - Multiple Drafts vs Subjective Experience]]). This 1998 paper already *anticipates* the split: experiences "may be determined internally" while beliefs extend.
- > [!warning] Against Cartesian internalism ([[Thinkers/Descartes]], the "Naked Mind"): the sealed *res cogitans* is the explicit foil. Language, they say, is what keeps us from being "discrete Cartesian 'inner' minds."
- > [!warning] Near-ally, different grain, with [[Thinkers/Hubert Dreyfus]]: both refuse a world-free inner reasoner. Dreyfus's body is a *lived* condition of relevance ([[Concepts/Embodied Intelligence and the Role of the Body (Dreyfus)]]); Clark and Chalmers will count a notebook as part of the mind. Dreyfus's 1992 worry is whether the *learner* is enough like us; this paper does not address learning, only coupling. See [[Contradictions/Dreyfus vs Computationalism - Skilled Coping vs Formal Rules]].
- > [!warning] Structural cousin of [[Concepts/Readiness-to-Hand and Presence-at-Hand (Heidegger)|Heideggerian readiness-to-hand]]: Otto's notebook withdraws into use the way a hammer does. The 1998 paper never cites Heidegger; the convergence is drawn later by Chalmers in *Reality+* ch. 16 and by the vault's Dreyfus ingest.
- Open in this source: no predictive-processing mechanism. Clark later says that is what the 1998 paper "lacked" ([[Sources/The Experience Machine - Andy Clark (2023)]]). The recruitment of external resources is asserted, not explained.
- Open: feature (4) (past conscious endorsement) is marked arguable; tampering and unread pages are left unresolved.
- Reception not in this source: Ned Block's later quip that the thesis "was false when we wrote the article in the 1990s but has since become true" is Chalmers 2022, not 1998. In 1998 the Internet typically *fails* their own criteria.
