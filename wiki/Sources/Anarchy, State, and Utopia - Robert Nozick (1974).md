# Anarchy, State, and Utopia — Robert Nozick (1974)

> Individuals have rights so strong they raise the question of what, if anything, the state may do. A night-watchman state is justified; anything more is not; and that minimal state is the framework left standing from the utopian tradition.

## Argument Spine

The book opens with rights, not with the state. “Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights).” So strong are those rights that they raise the question of how much room they leave for a state. Three conclusions: (1) a **minimal state**, limited to protection against force, theft, fraud, and enforcement of contracts, is justified; (2) any more extensive state violates persons’ rights not to be forced to do certain things, and is unjustified; (3) the minimal state is inspiring as well as right. Two implications: the state may not coerce some citizens to aid others, and it may not prohibit activities for people’s *own* good.

Part I answers the individualist anarchist (stimulated by Rothbard): starting from something close to Locke’s state of nature, a dominant protective association would arise by an **invisible-hand** process that need not violate anyone’s rights, and — once it prohibits risky private enforcement and compensates independents — it *is* a state (first ultraminimal, then minimal). Moral philosophy sets the boundary: rights are **side constraints**, not goals to be maximized; the experience machine is offered as a reason we are not hedonists about what matters. Part II argues that no more extensive state can be justified. Against patterned and end-state theories of distributive justice (especially Rawls), Nozick develops the **entitlement theory** (justice in acquisition, transfer, and rectification). Wilt Chamberlain shows that liberty upsets patterns. Equality, envy, workers’ control, and Marxian exploitation are then rejected as grounds for a larger state; **Demoktesis** is a hypothetical history designed to make the more-than-minimal state unattractive. Part III, written independently, argues that what can be saved from the utopian tradition is precisely the **framework** of the minimal state: a filter of voluntary communities rather than one designed society. The last sentence of the book: “How *dare* any state or group of individuals do more. Or less.”

The author flags what the book does *not* contain: a precise theory of the moral basis of rights, of retributive punishment, or a fully worked statement of the three entitlement principles. Those are promised for later work.

## Whole-Work Assessment

The book does what the preface said it would. Part I justifies a night-watchman state from Lockean anarchy by an invisible hand that need not violate side constraints; Part II refuses every offered reason for going further, with entitlement theory and Wilt Chamberlain as the load-bearing engine and Rawls as the named opponent; Part III, written first and claimed independent, converges: what can be saved from utopia is the framework, and the framework *is* the minimal state. The last sentence — “How *dare* any state or group of individuals do more. Or less.” — is the Kantian form (inviolability, ends not means) given libertarian content.

What it adds to the vault: the first primary libertarian treatise; the first primary experience machine (Chalmers and Clark had it only as a target or a title); a third pole on Hobbes vs Locke (Lockean rights without a compact); the political form of the Formula of Humanity (side constraints, including a “utilitarianism of rights” that is still utilitarian in form); and a one-sided but carefully marked contradiction with Rawls. What it does *not* add, by the author’s own admission: a theory of the moral basis of rights, a theory of original acquisition (form only), or a theory of rectification. Those holes are now named. *A Theory of Justice* is the outstanding source this ingest opens.

The Argument Spine written from the preface survives the text. The one correction of emphasis: Ch. 10’s “independence” is real as a line of argument but the chapter still *assumes* no more-than-minimal state is justified. Demoktesis is illumination of essential nature (slavery with a many-headed master), not a historical claim that democracies formed that way.
