# Fear and Trembling (1843)
> A dialectical lyric by Johannes *de silentio*: if Abraham is the father of faith, then the ethical as the universal is not the highest — or Abraham is a murderer and faith has never existed.

## Bibliographic Note

Søren Kierkegaard, *Frygt og Bæven*. This edition: *Fear and Trembling: Dialectical Lyric by Johannes de silentio*, translated with an introduction by Alastair Hannay (Penguin Classics, 1985; reprinted with a new Chronology 2003). ISBN 978-0-141-90623-2. First published in Copenhagen bookshops on 16 October 1843, on the same day as the companion *Repetition* (Constantin Constantius) and a collection of edifying discourses. Subtitle: "Dialectical Lyric." Epigraph: Hamann on Tarquin the Proud and the poppies — the son understood what the messenger did not.

**Pseudonym.** The author on the title page is Johannes *de silentio*, not Kierkegaard. Hannay reports Kierkegaard's later *Point of View* rule: the aesthetic works deceive by going along with the reader's delusion. Johannes says he has not understood the System, is no philosopher, and does not have faith. Claims on this page are Johannes's unless marked as Hannay.

## Argument Spine

Contemporaries "go further" than faith as they "go further" than Cartesian doubt: they treat faith as a cheap beginning rather than a task for a lifetime. Johannes will not go further. He retells Genesis 22 until the anguish is restored. Abraham is great because he believed *for this life*, on the strength of the absurd, that he would get Isaac back even while drawing the knife. Infinite resignation gives up the finite and wins eternal consciousness; faith is the further movement that gets the finite back. If the ethical is the universal (*Sittlichkeit*: the individual has his *telos* in the social whole), then Abraham is a murderer. The three *problemata* run the same *modus tollens*: if Hegel is right, Hegelians have no right to praise Abraham. Faith is the paradox that the single individual, as the particular, is higher than the universal and stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. It cannot be mediated, cannot be spoken, cannot be judged by the outcome. Faith is a passion; no generation starts further than the last.

## Section-by-Section Summary

**Hannay Introduction (reception, not primary).** The book is not primarily about Abraham and Isaac; it uses the story to cheapen current talk of faith and to break Hegelian *Sittlichkeit*. Faith here is not belief that God exists (Abraham already has that) but belief that the heart's project is possible when humanly impossible — for *this* life, not the hereafter. The shopman knight and Abraham share the double movement; they do not share sacrifice or ethical breach. Hannay reads the Abraham story as allegory of a religious attitude to value, not a literal command to kill. He reads the *problemata* as *modus tollens* against Hegel's "The State in and by itself is the ethical whole." Biographical overlay (Regine Olsen; journal: if he had had faith for this life he would have stayed with her) is Hannay's, from the journals. Martensen's "go further" from Descartes to Hegel is the Preface's local target.

**Preface.** Clearance sale in the world of ideas. Everyone has doubted everything and everyone has faith, so everyone goes further. Descartes did not make doubt a public duty and would not doubt what God revealed. The Greeks took doubt as a lifetime's work. Faith too was once a lifetime's work; the old campaigner never forgot the fear and trembling. Johannes is no philosopher, has not understood the System, and would not have grasped faith even if he could render its content into concepts. "This is not the System." Signed Johannes *de silentio*.

**Attunement.** A man who never needed to go further than faith tries four times to understand Abraham. (I) Abraham pretends the command is his own desire so Isaac will not lose God. (II) He performs the act in silence and is darkened; he sees joy no more. (III) He rides back to beg forgiveness for having been willing to sacrifice the best he owned. (IV) Isaac sees the clenched left hand and loses his faith. Each version is followed by a weaning image. None of these Abrahams is the father of faith. "Yet no one was as great as Abraham; who is able to understand him?"

**Speech in Praise of Abraham.** If there were no eternal consciousness, life would be despair. Greatness is measured by what one loved, expected, and strove with: he who loved God, expected the impossible, and strove with God is greater than all. Abraham left worldly understanding and took faith; he had no song of lament. It is great to give up one's desire, greater to stick to it after giving it up; great to grasp the eternal, greater to stick to the temporal after giving it up. He believed for *this* life, not a future life (that would be easier). He believed the absurd. Had he doubted he would have stabbed himself and been admired; it is one thing to be admired, another to be a guiding star. He said nothing to Sarah or Eleazar. "We know it, all of us — it was only a trial" is the cheapening the speech refuses. In 130 years Abraham got no further than faith.

**Preamble from the Heart.** In the world of spirit only one who works gets bread; only one who draws the knife gets Isaac. The ethical expression is that Abraham was willing to murder Isaac; the religious expression is that he was willing to sacrifice him; the contradiction is the anguish. Without faith the raw fact is easy to imitate. Johannes can think himself into the tragic hero, not into Abraham. "I cannot close my eyes and hurl myself trustingly into the absurd." He would have made infinite resignation and then been at a loss if Isaac were returned. Abraham believed God would not demand Isaac *and* was willing to offer him; he believed on the strength of the absurd. Even if Isaac were actually sacrificed, faith was that God could give a new Isaac *here*. The knight of infinite resignation is recognizable (gliding gait); the knight of faith looks like a tax-gatherer, a shopman who at every moment resigns everything and takes it back. The young man and the princess: resignation transfigures the love into an eternal love of God; faith says "I nevertheless believe that I shall get her, on the strength of the absurd." The absurd is not the improbable; it is what the understanding has concluded to be humanly impossible. Resignation is a purely philosophical movement one can discipline oneself into; faith is the paradox that gets the finite back. "Faith begins precisely where thinking leaves off."

**Problema I — Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?** The ethical as such is the universal; the single individual's task is to abrogate particularity and become the universal. Hegel's "Good and Conscience" is then right: the individual who stays particular is in sin or temptation. Where Hegel goes wrong is in still talking about faith and letting Abraham be its father — he should be remitted as a murderer. Faith is the paradox that the single individual is higher than the universal, having been in the universal and then setting himself apart in an absolute relation to the absolute. It cannot be mediated. Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus stay inside the ethical: they let one ethical expression have its *telos* in a higher ethical expression (the nation, the vow, the laws of Rome). Abraham oversteps the ethical altogether for a private *telos*. The temptation here *is* the ethical, which would keep him from God's will. He cannot speak. Do not judge by the outcome; the outcome comes last, and if Isaac had not been returned Abraham would not be less justified. Mary is the parallel: favoured, unintelligible, greater because of the distress, not relieved of it. Either Abraham was a murderer every minute or we stay with the paradox. Faith is a passion.

**Problema II — Is there an absolute duty to God?** If all duty is duty to God only by being referred to the universal, then one has no duty *to* God; God is a vanishing point. Hegel makes the outer higher than the inner; faith is the paradox that interiority (a new interiority, not the child's immediacy) is higher than exteriority. Philosophy has substituted the immediate for faith; Socrates already went further than that, his ignorance being infinite resignation. The single individual determines his relation to the universal through his relation to the absolute. Luke 14.26 ("hate father and mother…") is to be taken literally, not haggled into "love less." Abraham must love Isaac more, not less; only then is the act a sacrifice and not Cain's. If the Church demanded the sacrifice one would have only a tragic hero. The true knight is absolute isolation; the false knight is sectarian. A witness, never a teacher. Either there is an absolute duty to God — or Abraham is done for.

**Problema III — Was it ethically defensible of Abraham to conceal his purpose?** The ethical is the disclosed. Hegel allows no justified concealment. Faith is not the first immediacy (the aesthetic) but a later one. Long aesthetic inquiry: Aristotle on *anagnorisis*; Agamemnon must disclose (ethics has no old servant); Delphi bridegroom, Agnete and the merman, Tobit, Gloucester, Faust. The merman Johannes understands: guilt exiles him from the universal and he returns via the paradox. Abraham is the reverse — already able to realize the universal, he uses the paradox to put that capacity into God's hands. Abraham *cannot* speak: speech translates into the universal; "it is a trial" is exactly what cannot be understood. The preserved word, "God will provide himself a lamb," is irony — saying something and yet not saying it — and it carries both movements (he is ready to sacrifice; on the absurd, God may do otherwise). No poet can reach Abraham. Either the particular stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, or Abraham is done for.

**Epilogue.** Dutch spice merchants dumped cargo to raise the price; the world of spirit needs the opposite of that trick — honest seriousness that keeps the tasks young. Whatever one generation learns from another, it cannot learn the genuinely human factor. That factor is passion; every generation begins at the beginning. The highest passion is faith; none comes further. "One must go further" is of ancient standing: Heraclitus's disciple "went further" and turned "you cannot step twice into the same river" into an Eleatic denial of movement.

## Whole-Work Assessment

Outstanding Sources listed *Fear and Trembling* as the missing existential-religious primary. The book supplies the Abraham Sartre used as a model of undecidable authority, and it supplies it *against* Sartre: Johannes's Abraham already believes God exists; the trial is not "is this an angel?" but whether he can believe he will get Isaac back for this life while drawing the knife. The ethical Johannes suspends is Hegelian *Sittlichkeit* (and, by the same stroke, any ethics that identifies duty with the universal — including Kant's). The vault's Hegel cluster now has its first dedicated ethical opponent; the Kant cluster now has a named claim that there is an absolute duty to God that can give love of neighbour the opposite expression to ethical duty. Heidegger's ancestor-note for *Angst* remains one-sided: this book is about faith's anguish, not the dizziness of freedom before possibility (*The Concept of Anxiety*, 1844, is still outstanding). Hadot's Kierkegaard (pseudonyms; deceive into the truth; the Individual; not-Christian ≈ not-sage) is confirmed from inside one aesthetic work. Holes this opens: *The Concept of Anxiety*; *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*; *The Sickness unto Death*; Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* (the *Sittlichkeit* Johannes is targeting).
