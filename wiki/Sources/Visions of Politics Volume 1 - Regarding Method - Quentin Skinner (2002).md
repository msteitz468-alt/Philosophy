# Visions of Politics Volume 1 - Regarding Method - Quentin Skinner (2002)

> The Cambridge School's methodological charter. The history of ideas must recover not what texts say but what their authors were **doing** in saying it — and reading the canon for "dateless wisdom" produces "not histories but more appropriately mythologies." "There are no perennial problems in philosophy. There are only individual answers to individual questions."

**Full citation**: Quentin Skinner, *Visions of Politics, Volume 1: Regarding Method* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002). ISBN 0 521 58105 2 (hardback) / 0 521 58926 6 (paperback). Ten chapters, pp. 1–187, plus bibliography and index. Volume 1 of three; volumes 2 (*Renaissance Virtues*) and 3 (*Hobbes and Civil Science*) are the applications. Chapter 4 is the revised version of the 1969 essay of the same name.

## Argument Spine

Three post-empiricist assaults on "the world of facts" frame the book — from epistemology, from the theory of meaning, and from post-modern accounts of rhetoric and power — and Skinner joins the sceptical camp in each, but "as a practising historian." The positive programme: because "whenever we use language for purposes of communication, we are always doing something as well as saying something," understanding a text requires recovering its **illocutionary force** — what its author was doing, within the conventions then available, in writing it. This dissolves the canon-of-great-books model, since what survives of an idea is only "a history of its various uses, and of the varying intentions with which it was used." Two consequences follow that give the method teeth beyond interpretation: the truth of a belief is irrelevant to explaining why it was held (ch. 3), and normative vocabulary constrains action even for agents who believe none of it, because conduct must be **legitimised** to be performed (ch. 8).

## Key Claims / Positions

- **The programme**: "to grasp their concepts, to follow their distinctions, to appreciate their beliefs and, so far as possible, to see things their way." Not entry into thought-processes — ordinary historical technique.
- **Against the cult of the fact** (ch. 2): Elton's "true facts" is a pleonasm on his own account, and "total acquaintance" with the evidence is unintelligible — "A complete study of all the facts about Chatsworth would be literally endless." Skinner's counter-precept, via Dunn: "the historian should be Whig as to subject matter, Tory as to truth." His parting charge is that Elton's retreat to technique is rooted in a fear that historical study might transform us — "a consummation devoutly to be stopped."
- **The mythologies** (ch. 4): doctrines (scattered remarks promoted to doctrines; Lovejoy-style reification; blame for absence — "History then indeed becomes a pack of tricks we play on the dead"); coherence (overriding authors' own statements; Strauss's esoteric "reading between the lines"); prolepsis ("the episode has to await the future to learn its meaning"); and two forms of parochialism (spurious influence, tested by three necessary conditions; and misdescribing sense so "alien elements dissolve into a misleading familiarity").
- **The constraint**: no agent "meant or achieved something which they could never be brought to accept as a correct description." Questions violating it are "void for lack of reference."
- **Meaning trichotomy and the motive/intention cut** (ch. 5): motives are antecedent and contingent, genuinely outside the work; intentions *in* writing are a feature of it. Illocutionary intentions are equivalent to meaning₃, irrelevant to meaning₁ and ₂.
- **Not an intentionalist about textual meaning**: Skinner rejects Hirsch and Juhl and accepts Ricoeur's **surplus meaning** — "with this formulation I am in complete agreement." The two questions, what a text means and what its author meant, are "separable but not separate."
- **No unintentional illocutionary acts**: force is "a resource of language"; acts are identified by intentions.
- **Truth bracketed** (ch. 3): "the concept of truth is irrelevant to the enterprise of explaining beliefs." Rationality is "an achievement" requiring its own causal explanation. Bloor's impartiality accepted, symmetry rejected. Charity identifies subject-matter; it does not require mostly-true beliefs.
- **Legitimation** (ch. 8): "All revolutionaries are to this extent obliged to march backwards into battle." Principles are "among the enabling conditions" of action; "any course of action will be inhibited to the degree that it cannot be legitimised."
- **Words are not concepts** (ch. 9): Milton had the concept of originality before the word; polysemy is the trace of ideological *failure*, since successful arguments yield new social perceptions rather than new meanings.
- **Paradiastole** (ch. 10): redescription by a neighbouring term — prodigality as liberality, recklessness as courage — read out of Quintilian and Cicero and found in Machiavelli, Castiglione, and (unrecognised) Nietzsche's *Genealogy*.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] **vs. [[Thinkers/Arthur O. Lovejoy]]** — the central methodological collision in the vault's History of Ideas leg. Unit-idea history is named as the paradigm of the mythology of doctrines; the final verdict is "there is nothing lying beneath or behind such uses." Callout placed on the Lovejoy page, with the strongest Lovejovian reply recorded (Skinner's constraint makes long-range intellectual history nearly impossible to write — a cost, not a refutation). The vault now holds three rival methods for this leg: Lovejoy (unit-ideas), [[Thinkers/Isaiah Berlin|Berlin]] (value-collision), Skinner (uses in argument).
- > [!warning] **vs. [[Concepts/Archaeology of Knowledge (Foucault)]]** — the two rival methods for the history of discourse. Extensive agreement (both historicise, both treat vocabulary as constitutive; Skinner quotes Foucault approvingly twice), one sharp disagreement: archaeology brackets the author, Skinner's method requires him. He calls the death of the author "exaggerated" because language is a resource as well as a constraint.
- > [!warning] **Near-ally, [[Thinkers/Herbert Butterfield]]** — the mythology of prolepsis restates the Whig-interpretation critique in the vocabulary of intention, which makes it diagnosable rather than merely deplorable. But Skinner also faults Butterfield's allies for conceding Namier's premise that principles matter only as motives.
- > [!warning] **Bears on [[Concepts/Negative and Positive Liberty (Berlin)]]** — Skinner's apparatus implies "liberty" has no stable meaning to be partitioned, only a history of contested uses. **Flagged for accuracy**: his developed engagement with Berlin is *Liberty before Liberalism*, not this volume. No vault page should attribute a worked critique of Berlin to this source.
- **Skinner's own self-corrections**, recorded on every affected page: chapter 4 wrongly assumed morals and politics have "distinct and relatively stable vocabularies"; he no longer thinks evaluative terms have "accepted denotations," and now holds that "all attempts to legislate about the 'correct' use of normative terms must be regarded as equally ideological in character." He concedes Palonen on Time ("I cannot make sense of it") and denies he ever attacked **Begriffsgeschichte** — he had "no knowledge of Koselleck's research programme."
- **Open**: whether "same concept, different applications" is stable. Skinner calls it "almost paradoxical" himself, since "if the disputants are genuinely arguing, they must have the same concept," yet claims the long-run result is real conceptual change.

## Whole-Work Assessment

This is the methodological spine the vault's **History of Ideas** leg has been missing. The leg opened with [[Sources/The Great Chain of Being - Arthur O. Lovejoy (1936)|Lovejoy]] and was extended by Berlin; both were absorbed as *methods* without anything in the vault contesting how intellectual history should be done. Skinner supplies the contest, and does so at the level of first principles rather than of results.

Three effects. **First**, it turns an unexamined assumption of the vault into a live question. Many pages here narrate a term's development across thinkers as a single story; Skinner's argument is that such a story is legitimate only as a history of *uses in argument*, and that anything more presupposes a stable concept underneath. The vault's "How Different Thinkers Use This" convention already leans his way, but the pressure is real and worth keeping visible. **Second**, it connects two clusters that had been sitting apart: the philosophy-of-science material (Kuhn on paradigms, Hacking on styles of reasoning — both of which Skinner cites approvingly) and the history-of-ideas material now share a claim about theory-laden perception and framework-relative rationality. **Third**, it gives the vault its first sustained treatment of speech-act theory put to non-linguistic work, and with it a properly sourced [[Thinkers/J.L. Austin]] page that the existing Searle material had been leaning on implicitly.

What it opens rather than closes: the Lovejoy/Skinner fork, which is a genuine contradiction and is flagged as such on both sides; and the Foucault fork about whether discourse is best studied through its rules or through agents' moves within it. Both are the kind of tension SCHEMA calls the highest-value pages in the wiki, and neither should be reconciled.

A caution for future use: this book is a *collection*, and chapter 10 explicitly withdraws a premise that chapters 4 and 8 rely on. Cite chapter 4's mythologies freely — they are diagnoses and they survive — but not its picture of a fixed normative lexicon being manipulated by sleights of hand. Skinner abandoned it.
