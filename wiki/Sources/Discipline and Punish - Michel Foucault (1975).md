# Discipline and Punish — Michel Foucault (1975; Sheridan trans., Vintage 1995)

> The vault's first primary Foucault text: a genealogy of the modern soul and of a new power to judge, written as the history of the present — how the disappearance of public torture installed a more efficient political technology of the body whose masterpiece is the prison and whose diagram is the Panopticon.

## Argument Spine

*Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison* (Gallimard, 1975; Alan Sheridan trans. 1977; this file is the Second Vintage Books Edition, May 1995) is not a history of penal humanization. The opening contrast — Damiens quartered in 1757 versus Faucher's 1838 prison timetable — marks a displacement of the *object* of punishment, from the tortured body to the "soul" (Mably: "Punishment … should strike the soul rather than the body"). That soul is not a theological remnant but the correlative of a new political technology of the body: power produces it, knowledge names it, and "the soul is the prison of the body." Four rules govern the study: treat punishment as a complex social function (not merely repressive); treat it as a political tactic (not a legal consequence); treat penal law and the human sciences as one epistemologico-juridical formation; and read the entry of the soul into justice as a transformation in how power invests the body. Power is exercised, not possessed; it produces knowledge rather than being suspended for knowledge to appear. The prison, decried from the outset, is nonetheless the natural and legitimate form of this new economy because it is only the concentrated node of a carceral continuum already operating in school, factory, barracks, hospital. The book ends as "a historical background to various studies of the power of normalization and the formation of knowledge in modern society," with Mettray (22 January 1840) as the date of the carceral system's completion.

## Whole-Work Assessment

The vault's first primary Foucault text converts the Macey-derived cluster from reported doctrine into quoted argument. Three things the biography blurred are now sharp. (1) *Reformers are not hypocrites and did not design the prison.* They invented a semio-technique of public obstacle-signs ("punish less, but punish better"; humanity is the "respectable name" of an economy). Three technologies competed c. 1760–1840 — sovereign mark, reformer sign, prison trace — and the third won because disciplinary procedures already constituted outside the law *colonized* the legal institution. (2) *The Panopticon is a diagram, not a building, and "disciplinary society" is dissemination, not capture.* Bentham is the engineer of a transferable mechanism, not a villain; Foucault rejects "the Fourier of a police society." Do not write "surveillance state." (3) *The prison's "failure" is its function.* It succeeds in producing a specified, usable **delinquency** (strategic antonym of the field of illegalities) and in remaining the concentrated node of a carceral continuum. Foucault does not call for abolition; the live issue is the rise of **normalization**.

The Argument Spine of Step 1 held, with one correction of emphasis: "the soul is the prison of the body" is not a slogan tacked on the opening contrast but the product of the four rules plus the Kantorowitz inversion (surplus royal power → coded lack → non-corporal soul). Power-knowledge's canonical sentence is in I.1, not a later interview. Nietzsche is unnamed in that chapter; Hobbes is unnamed throughout — the contract is called a "fiction" only at IV.3. *Discipline and Punish* does **not** yet distinguish individualizing discipline from later biopower; "population" here is the multiplicity discipline orders. Companion now ingested: [[Sources/The History of Sexuality Volume 1 - Michel Foucault (1976)]]. The 1976 book makes the distinction *Discipline and Punish* does not: anatomo-politics of the body vs bio-politics of the population, joined by the deployment of sexuality.

Contradictions opened or sharpened: capillary strategy vs Hobbesian appropriation/contract; discipline as the bodily condition of labour-power vs Marx's exploitation (force separated from product); productive power vs the vault's Enlightenment knowledge-by-escaping-power cluster; "no outside" vs the closing "distant roar of battle."

## Translation Notes (Sheridan)

Sheridan: *surveiller* has no adequate English equivalent (Bentham's "inspect," "supervise," "observe" all miss); Foucault himself suggested *Discipline and Punish*, which "relates closely to the book's structure." *Supplice* (Part One heading) is rendered "Torture" for brevity but covers public torture-execution as spectacle, prolonged mental/physical torment, and, as *la question*, judicial torture.
