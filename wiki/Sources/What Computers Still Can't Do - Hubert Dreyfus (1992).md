# What Computers Still Can't Do — Hubert Dreyfus (1972/1979/1992)
> A Heideggerian critique of artificial reason: digital computers programmed with facts and rules cannot reproduce human intelligence, because intelligence is embodied, situational skilled coping rather than formal calculation.

**Full citation**: Hubert L. Dreyfus, *What Computers Still Can't Do: A Critique of Artificial Reason* (MIT Press, 1992). Revised edition of *What Computers Can't Do* (Harper & Row, 1972; revised 1979). Sixth printing 1999. ISBN 0-262-54067-3.

## Argument Spine
Artificial intelligence as facts-plus-rules on a digital computer is the technological fulfillment of a 2,500-year Western rationalist project: Socrates and Plato demand that knowledge be an explicit effective procedure; Hobbes makes reasoning reckoning; Leibniz promises a universal characteristic; Turing supplies the universal discrete-state machine. That project rests on four assumptions — biological (the brain is a digital computer), psychological (the mind processes information by formal rules), epistemological (all knowledge can be formalized), and ontological (the world consists of independent determinate facts). Phenomenology (Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Wittgenstein, Polanyi) shows each assumption is optional and, for human intelligence, false. Know-how is not know-that; skilled coping is ready-to-hand, body-involving, and organized by needs and situations, not by atomic facts plus rules. GOFAI is therefore a degenerating research program (Lakatos). Expert systems fail because experts do not follow rules. Connectionism is more promising but still faces the commonsense-knowledge and appropriate-generalization problems unless a net shares our body, architecture, and form of life. The 1992 MIT introduction treats the original book as history of a bygone research program, not as a still-open debate.

## Summary

The 1992 MIT edition is three books in one: a new introduction that declares GOFAI historically over, the 1979 introduction that adds Phases III–IV (micro-worlds, frames, story understanding), and the intact 1972 treatise.

The 1972 book is a **critique of artificial reason** in Kant's sense. Since the Greeks, Western thought has wanted all reasoning reduced to calculation — Socrates asking Euthyphro for an "effective procedure," Plato converting knowing-*how* into knowing-*that*, Hobbes declaring that "REASON … is nothing but reckoning," Leibniz promising "Let us calculate, Sir," Boole and Babbage building the calculus and the engine. Turing's 1950 paper supplies the machine and the test. Simon (1957) and Minsky then announce that machines already think and that general intelligence is a generation away. Dreyfus's historical claim: this is not a new science but the last metaphysics, "staking everything on man's ability to formalize his behavior."

Part I autopsies 1957–67. **Phase I** (Cognitive Simulation: GPS, machine translation, chess, pattern recognition) produces Bar-Hillel's "fallacy of the successful first step" — early legal chess and mechanical dictionaries, then stall. **Phase II** (Minsky's semantic-information-processing students: STUDENT, ANALOGY, Quillian nets) replaces general methods with *ad hoc* tricks that do not generalize. Four human capacities resist formalization: fringe consciousness, ambiguity tolerance, essential/inessential discrimination, and perspicuous grouping.

Part II isolates the **four assumptions** that keep optimism alive after the record. The biological assumption (neurons as on/off switches) no longer fits neurophysiology and would not underwrite heuristic AI even if true (a random net is not a heuristic program). The psychological assumption (the mind as a rule-following information processor) is a philosophical posit squeezed between physics and phenomenology, confirmed circularly by the programs it postulates. The epistemological assumption (behavior formalizable even if humans do not follow the rules) borrows failed analogies from physics (planets are not solving DEs) and linguistics (Chomsky formalizes competence, not performance). The ontological assumption (the world as determinate independent facts) has "no reason to suppose that such data about the human world are available… and several reasons to suggest that no such data exist."

Part III supplies the alternative. The body is not an obstacle to intelligence (Plato–Descartes) but its condition: gestalt pattern recognition, motor schemata, inner horizon, cross-modal transfer. Situation — Heideggerian being-in-the-world — makes orderly behavior possible *without rules* and halts the regress of rules-for-applying-rules. Needs and concerns structure the field so that relevance is seen rather than computed; a utility function is one more determinate object and cannot occupy that role. AI "must begin at the level of objectivity and rationality where the facts have already been produced."

The 1972 conclusion splits two questions (do humans follow formal rules? can behavior be formalized?) and answers both no. Burden of proof shifts to the optimists. Four activity areas: associationistic and simple-formal are digitally simulable; complex-formal is formalizable in principle, intractable in practice; **nonformal** (*esprit de finesse*) is "totally intractable" and is prior to the formal, not a further increment of complexity. What remains is man–computer **symbiosis** in already-formalized domains, not unaided minds. Recent AI is "a crucial experiment disconfirming the traditional assumption that human reason can be analyzed into rule-governed operations on situation-free discrete elements." The alchemy analogy: early distillation of mercury from dirt does not license transmutation.

The 1979 introduction adds Phase III (micro-worlds, SHRDLU: a "fairyland" whose statements would be "literally false" of the real world) and Phase IV (frames, scripts, KRL). Knowledge engineering can succeed where relevance is pre-fixed; that is not general intelligence. Everyday understanding cannot be represented as facts. "Man can embody the truth, but he cannot know it" (Yeats).

The 1992 introduction is the load-bearing update. GOFAI is a Lakatosian **degenerating research program**. The commonsense-knowledge problem is three problems: organization of everyday knowledge, representation of know-how as knowing-that, and situational relevance. Stuart Dreyfus's **five-stage skill model** (novice → advanced beginner → competent → proficient → expert) shows that acquisition-rules need not operate in later application. Expert systems and Japan's Fifth Generation failed as predicted. Lenat's CYC is GOFAI's last defense and is doomed by body, deixis, *savoir faire*, and analogy. Connectionism completed GOFAI's degeneration but still fails human-like generalization (NETtalk; army tank/shadows net) unless a net shares our body, architecture, and form of life. Three successor programs — Heideggerian/interactionist AI (Winograd, Agre–Chapman: "the world is its own best representation"), supervised nets, reinforcement learning — all still fail relevance. Deep dilemma: GOFAI must represent all that humans understand *by being embodied*; learning systems must share enough human structure to generalize *our* way. Both are "at least as unlikely."

## Key Claims / Positions

- GOFAI (facts + formal rules on a physical symbol system) has failed as a research program; this is not a proof of impossibility.
- The failure exposes four optional metaphysical assumptions, not temporary engineering gaps.
- Know-how is not knowing-that; experts do not apply the rules beginners follow.
- Commonsense is holist and situational, not a large database of facts (Minsky's 10^5–10^6 "elements").
- The body and being-in-the-world are conditions of intelligence, not implementation details.
- Computers can do associationistic and simple-formal work; they cannot reproduce adult nonformal intelligence.
- Connectionism is the right kind of successor and still inherits the generalization/relevance problem.

## Whole-Work Assessment

This is the missing collision of the Heidegger cluster with the AI & Cognitive Science leg. Until this ingest, Dreyfus appeared only as a one-line application of ready-to-hand to expert systems. The book supplies the argument: GOFAI is Western metaphysics implemented, the four assumptions are optional, and the alternative is being-in-the-world rather than a better knowledge-representation language. It also opens a three-way split the vault did not have. Against Turing, Simon, and Minsky, Dreyfus denies that formal symbol manipulation on discrete facts is the right model of mind. Against Searle, he locates the failure in embodiment and situation rather than in biological causal powers or the syntax/semantics gap. Against Nagel, his target is skill and worldhood, not the subjective character of experience — they are allies against functional reduction and talking past each other. The 1992 introduction already names the next problem Clark will try to solve (how a prediction machine recruits a world) and the next problem Mitchell will document (brittleness, common sense, hype cycles). The Argument Spine from Step 1 held. One correction: Dreyfus never claims a negative proof that AI is impossible; the 1992 text is explicit that GOFAI has reached a dead end "for the time being at least." The five-stage skill model is Stuart Dreyfus's contribution, fully stated only in 1992, proto-stated in Part III (1972).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] Direct clash with computationalism — see [[Contradictions/Dreyfus vs Computationalism - Skilled Coping vs Formal Rules]].
- The 1992 dilemma (represent all that embodiment understands, or build a device like us) is still open against connectionism and later deep learning. Mitchell's brittleness pages document the empirical half; they do not settle the philosophical half.
- Dreyfus leaves wet/analogue or sufficiently human-like embodied agents open. That is a different claim from Searle's "never, because syntax."
