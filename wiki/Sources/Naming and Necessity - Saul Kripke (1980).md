# Naming and Necessity - Saul Kripke (1980)

> Three Princeton lectures (20, 22, 29 January 1970) that detach metaphysical necessity from the a priori, treat ordinary names as rigid designators, replace the Frege–Russell description theory with a historical picture of reference, and take the same apparatus through natural-kind terms to a modal argument against the mind–body identity thesis.

## Argument Spine

Identity is an internal relation: if *a* and *b* are rigid designators, then `a = b`, if true, is necessary (Preface, pp. 3–4). Ordinary names are rigid — they designate the same object in every counterfactual situation in which it exists — so true identity statements between names are necessary even when they are empirical discoveries (Hesperus/Phosphorus). The Frege–Russell description theory, and its cluster weakening, fails both as a theory of meaning and as an account of how reference is fixed. A name's reference is typically fixed by an initial baptism and transmitted along a historical chain; the descriptions a speaker associates with the name need not uniquely identify the referent and are not synonymous with it. The same picture extends to natural-kind terms. Because necessity and a priority come apart, there are necessary a posteriori truths and contingent a priori ones (the standard metre). Possible worlds are stipulated states or histories, not distant planets requiring a criterion of transworld identification. The identity thesis in the philosophy of mind cannot treat "pain = C-fiber firing" as a contingent scientific identification of the Hesperus/Phosphorus type: if the identification is true, it is necessary, and the conceivability of pain without that physical state is evidence against the identification.

The 1980 preface refuses an official doctrine on the propositions expressed by codesignative names in epistemic contexts, refuses to reduce rigidity to scope, and refuses Lewis-style counterpart "contingent identity." It does not use Chalmers's later two-dimensional vocabulary (primary/secondary intension). That apparatus is a reception of this book, not its content.

## Core Thesis (as executed)

Identity is internal. Names are rigid. The Frege–Russell / cluster theory fails as meaning and, except at rare baptisms, as reference-fixing. A better *picture*: baptism plus a chain of communication; descriptions used in baptism fix reference and are not synonymous. A priori and necessary come apart, so Hesperus is Phosphorus and gold is atomic number 79 are necessary a posteriori, and the standard metre is contingent a priori. Natural-kind terms work like names (fool's gold / fool's water). If pain = C-fiber stimulation, the identity is necessary; the heat analogy fails because pain is picked out by being pain. Materialism then needs mental facts to follow from physical facts by necessity. Dualism is not thereby entailed. Possible worlds are stipulated states, not distant planets. There is no official doctrine on names in belief contexts.

He calls the positive account a **picture**, not a theory. He never says "causal theory of reference." He never says "zombie."

## Whole-Work Assessment

The vault already had Kripke as a slogan: Chalmers's 2-D semantics, Nagel's footnote, Jacquette's metre reply, McDowell's WRPL quarrel. Those pages had not read the lectures. What this ingest supplies is the primary split of the epistemological from the metaphysical, the rigidity test on simple sentences, the cluster theses and Gödel/Schmidt, the *picture* (not theory) of baptism and chains, the contingent a priori in Kripke's own metre wording, necessity of origin, natural-kind identifications as necessary a posteriori, and the pain argument that blocks the heat analogy. The 1980 preface is a conservative errata-plus-genesis note: rigidity ≠ scope, worlds are dice-states, no official doctrine on belief. Addenda add unicorns/Holmes, Madagascar shifts, and the claim that much "physical necessity" is necessary tout court — and leave how far that goes open. Two Kripkes stay two Kripkes. Two-dimensional vocabulary stays Chalmers's. Quine is the live contradiction, not a predecessor Kripke "overcomes."
