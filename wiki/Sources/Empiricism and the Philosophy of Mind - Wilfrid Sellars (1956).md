# Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind - Wilfrid Sellars (1956)

> The essay that killed the **Given**: the empiricist demand for episodes both epistemically independent of all other knowledge and capable of justifying it. Sellars shows nothing can be both, replaces the foundation metaphor with "a self-correcting enterprise which can put *any* claim in jeopardy, though not *all* at once," and then — with the **Myth of Jones** — shows that giving up the Given costs us neither inner episodes, nor privileged access, nor naturalism.

**Full citation**: Wilfrid Sellars, *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*, with an Introduction by [[Thinkers/Richard Rorty|Richard Rorty]] and a Study Guide by [[Thinkers/Robert Brandom|Robert Brandom]] (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1997), 181 pp. Originally delivered as the University of London Special Lectures on Philosophy for 1955–56, in March 1956, under the title "The Myth of the Given: Three Lectures on Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind"; first published in Herbert Feigl and Michael Scriven, eds., *Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science*, vol. 1 (Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 1956). Sixteen parts, §§1–63.

## Argument Spine

The empiricist tradition rests on a **Given**: a stratum of non-inferential, self-authenticating awareness that is both *epistemically independent* of everything else known and yet capable of *justifying* the rest of empirical knowledge. Nothing can play both roles. To be a knowing at all, an episode must stand in inferential relations and be recognized as authoritative, which presupposes a whole framework of concepts and a public practice. Hence "all awareness … is a linguistic affair" (psychological nominalism), and "in characterizing an episode or a state as that of *knowing*, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the logical space of reasons" (§36). The destructive half is only half: the Myth of Jones reconstructs thoughts and sense impressions as *theoretical postulates* of an intersubjective explanatory framework which then *acquires* a reporting use — "what began as a language with a purely theoretical use has gained a reporting role" (§59).

*Checked against the text (SCHEMA Step 3): the spine drawn from Rorty's Introduction survives, with one correction of emphasis. Rorty's framing makes the essay look primarily like an episode in the history of anti-foundationalism. Sellars's own §§39–44 make clear that the target is equally **instrumentalism in philosophy of science**: he argues the positivistic demotion of theoretical entities rests on the *same* mistake as foundationalism — the Given. The vault page [[Concepts/Scientia Mensura (Sellars)]] carries this, and it should not be treated as a side theme.*

## Key Claims / Positions

- **What the Myth is not.** Not the denial that seeing differs from inferring; not the denial of inner episodes; not identical with foundationalism, though that is "the most widespread form." It is a *framework* — "sense contents, material objects, universals, propositions, real connections, first principles, even givenness itself" — common to "both 'dogmatic rationalism' and 'skeptical empiricism.'"
- **The inconsistent triad** (§7). (A) sensing a red sense content entails non-inferentially knowing it is red; (B) the ability to sense is unacquired; (C) the ability to know facts of the form *x is φ* is acquired. Any two entail the denial of the third. The classical sense datum is "a mongrel resulting from a crossbreeding of two ideas" — an explanatory posit and an epistemic foundation.
- **Looks is not a relation** (§§12–17). "*X* looks green to Jones" ascribes the propositional claim to Jones's experience but *withholds endorsement*; "X merely looks green" rejects it. This handles qualitative, existential, and merely generic looking with one mechanism.
- **The presupposition thesis** (§18). "The concept of *looking* green… presupposes the concept of *being* green." The biconditional with "standard conditions" is a necessary truth but not a definition, because "standard conditions" *means* "conditions in which things look what they are." One has a concept "only by having a whole battery of concepts of which it is one element" — the break with logical atomism.
- **Psychological nominalism** (§29). "All awareness of *sorts, resemblances, facts*… indeed, all awareness even of particulars — is a linguistic affair." Locke, Berkeley and Hume shared an unargued assumption of innate awareness of determinate repeatables.
- **'Means' is not a relation** (§31). "'*Rot*' means red" says that "rot" plays in German the role "red" plays here; nothing follows about the simplicity of that role. So one may "know what redness is" only by possessing a great deal of knowledge classical empiricism thought merely contingent.
- **Epistemic facts are irreducible** (§5). Analyzing them into non-epistemic facts is "a mistake of a piece with the so-called 'naturalistic fallacy' in ethics."
- **The space of reasons** (§36), and the dissolution of the regress: what is required is that it be correct *now* to say the reporter knows the reliability, not that anyone knew it then.
- **No tortoise** (§38). "Empirical knowledge, like its sophisticated extension, science, is rational, not because it has a *foundation* but because it is a self-correcting enterprise which can put *any* claim in jeopardy, though not *all* at once." He rejects both the elephant-on-tortoise and the "great Hegelian serpent of knowledge with its tail in its mouth."
- ***Scientia mensura*** (§41). "In the dimension of describing and explaining the world, science is the measure of all things, of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not." Instrumentalism about theoretical entities rests on the Given plus a reified methodological distinction.
- **The Myth of Jones** (§§48–63). Theoretical postulation, modelled on overt speech (thoughts) and on inner replicas (impressions), each with a *commentary* restricting the analogy; then the training of a reporting use. Impressions are states of the perceiver, not particulars; not literally red; but not "purely formal" either — that too "is but another expression of the Myth of the Given."
- **The diagnosis** (§62). The sense-datum philosopher "has taught himself to notice impressions" and misreads this as a discovery that such things were there all along — speaking of the given "in the very act of taking."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- > [!warning] **vs. [[Thinkers/Bertrand Russell]]** — the primary casualty. [[Concepts/Sense-Data vs. Physical Objects (Russell)]] and [[Concepts/Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description (Russell)]] are dismantled as an epistemological foundation, though the act/object distinction Russell used against Berkeley survives intact. Callouts placed on the Russell concept page.
- > [!warning] **vs. [[Thinkers/Hume]], [[Thinkers/Locke]], [[Thinkers/George Berkeley]]** — [[Concepts/Impressions and Ideas - Copy Principle (Hume)]] loses its unstated premise. Sellars's historically pointed claim is that the three disagree about abstract ideas while *sharing* the assumption that awareness of determinate repeatables is innate.
- > [!warning] **Fork with [[Thinkers/W.V.O. Quine]]** — complementary demolitions (Rorty's division of labor), incompatible reconstructions. Quine naturalizes epistemology; Sellars's naturalistic-fallacy claim says the epistemic cannot be analyzed without remainder into the descriptive. Whether normativity survives naturalization is left open and now flagged on [[Concepts/Naturalized Epistemology and the Web of Belief (Quine)]].
- > [!warning] **Convergence with [[Thinkers/Karl Popper]]** — [[Concepts/Basic Statements and the Empirical Basis (Popper)]] and [[Concepts/No Authoritative Sources of Knowledge (Popper)]] reach the same anti-foundationalist verdict from the logic of testing rather than the logic of "knows." They differ on where rationality is secured: methodological rules (Popper) vs. the normative structure of knowledge-attribution (Sellars).
- > [!warning] **vs. [[Thinkers/Gilbert Ryle]] and [[Thinkers/Descartes]] simultaneously** — Sellars keeps inner episodes and privileged access (against Ryle) while denying they are self-intimating, incorrigible, or conceptually prior to public discourse (against Descartes). Both sides deny this position is available.
- > [!warning] **vs. [[Schools/Phenomenology]]** — the *scientia mensura* ("there are no such things" as the coloured objects of the common-sense world) is precisely what [[Thinkers/Edmund Husserl]] and [[Thinkers/Martin Heidegger]] refuse. The vault now holds both claims; this tension is unresolved and worth a dedicated `Contradictions/` page if a phenomenology source engages scientific realism directly.
- **Open, and inherited by the successors**: does a theoretical language "gaining a reporting role" really escape the Given, or defer the question of what makes a non-inferential report authoritative? [[Thinkers/John McDowell]] answers by making experience conceptual; [[Thinkers/Robert Brandom]] by building outward from social-inferential practice. Both accept the destructive argument.
- **Flagged for accuracy**: the "manifest image / scientific image" vocabulary belongs to Sellars's 1962 "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man," **not** to EPM. This volume contains §41's *scientia mensura* and the §§39–44 critique of positivism, and no vault page should read the later essay's detail back into it.

## Whole-Work Assessment

EPM is the keystone the vault's post-positivist cluster was missing. It had Ayer's positivism, Quine's attack on the two dogmas, Popper's falsificationism, Kuhn on paradigms, and Hacking on experiment; what it lacked was the argument that dismantles the *empiricist theory of perception* underwriting all of them, and that had been visible only at second hand — [[Thinkers/John McDowell]]'s page named the Myth of the Given with no page behind it. That gap is now closed at the source.

Three things shift. **First**, the vault's account of the analytic tradition is no longer bipolar. Rorty's three-work thesis (Quine 1951, Wittgenstein 1953, Sellars 1956) gives a structure into which the existing Ayer/Quine/Popper/Kuhn pages now fit, and Sellars supplies the missing bridge from philosophy of science to philosophy of mind — the same volume that critiques instrumentalism also reconstructs thoughts and sensations. **Second**, the vault's empiricism pages acquire a live opponent. Locke, Berkeley, and Hume were previously in tension only with rationalism; psychological nominalism attacks a premise all three share with Russell, and does so historically rather than by fiat. **Third**, it re-opens the qualia cluster from a new angle: against [[Concepts/Qualia Disqualified (Dennett)]], Sellars is the ancestor of the attack on incorrigible immediate experience yet *retains* impressions as real posits with a genuine reporting use — he relocates the explanandum where Dennett dissolves it.

What it closes: the question of whether anti-foundationalism entails behaviorism or eliminativism. Sellars's answer is no, and the Myth of Jones is the constructive demonstration. What it opens: the Quine fork on whether normativity survives naturalization, and the phenomenology/scientific-realism tension left standing by §41. Both are now flagged rather than silently reconciled.

A note on the edition: the Rorty Introduction and Brandom Study Guide are substantial secondary matter, and this ingest treats them as such — Rorty's historical thesis and Brandom's two-ply account are attributed to them by name, not to Sellars.
