# Putnam on BIVs and Radical Skepticism - Pritchard and Ranalli (2016)

> A fifteen-page reconstruction of Putnam's semantic argument that one cannot truly think "I am a brain in a vat," and of the four main lines of attack: Brueckner (question-begging), Wright (third-person / self-knowledge of content), Stroud (transcendental arguments generally), and Nagel (reductio plus ineffable hypotheses).

**Full citation**: Duncan Pritchard and Chris Ranalli, "Putnam on BIVs and radical skepticism," ch. 6 of Sanford C. Goldberg (ed.), *The Brain in a Vat*, Classic Philosophical Arguments (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2016), 75–89. DOI: [10.1017/CBO9781107706965.006](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107706965.006). ISBN-13 of the volume: 9781107706965 (ebook); print 9781107069671.

## Summary

**§6.1 Putnam’s BIV argument.** The ordinary skeptical use of the [[Concepts/Brain in a Vat|brain-in-a-vat]] hypothesis is epistemological: the hypothesis is indistinguishable from normal experience, so one cannot know it is false; by a closure principle, one then cannot know everyday propositions known to be inconsistent with it (e.g. that one has hands). Responses include denying closure (Dretske 1970; [[Thinkers/Robert Nozick|Nozick]] 1981) or claiming one *can* know the denials of skeptical hypotheses (Pritchard's own work, cited but not developed here).

Putnam (1981b) looks like the second strategy and is not. His goal is **semantic**, not epistemological: "one cannot truly think the thought that one is a BIV." The two claims "are logically distinct, in both directions." Knowing that one is not a BIV is consistent with being able to think the thought; being unable to think the thought truly is consistent with being unable to know that one is not a BIV.

The engine is Putnam's [[Concepts/Semantic Externalism (Putnam)|content externalism]]. To think thoughts with a given content, "certain external conditions — in particular, causal conditions — need to be met." A Martian who has never encountered trees, and is not part of a linguistic community with a practice of referring to trees, "cannot even think thoughts about trees." A lifelong BIV stimulated by supercomputers is in the same boat: no direct or indirect causal route to trees. So if one *can* think about trees, one is not a BIV. More generally, Putnam denies the skeptic's picture on which envatted and unenvatted counterparts "are thinking exactly the same thoughts," the former massively false, the latter mostly true.

The conclusion is stronger still: the thought "I am a BIV" is "in a sense necessarily false." If one is not a BIV, the thought is trivially false. If one *is* a BIV, one cannot think that thought at all and must think a thought with different content instead — so the sentence still comes out false. This is supposed to be a priori. The analogy to the Martian **requires** Putnam's stipulated scenario: the brain has **always** been envatted, and stimulation is (and always has been) by supercomputers. Recent envattment would leave prior causal connections to trees intact. The anti-skeptical result is therefore "essentially limited" to that rendering — "even so, if Putnam’s argument goes through it is still a startling result."

**§6.2 Brueckner’s critique.** Criticisms divide into question-begging and self-refuting. Anthony Brueckner (1986) is the first. His reconstruction:

- (P1) Either I am a BIV (speaking vat-English) or I am not (an English-speaking human).
- (P2) If I am a BIV, my utterances of "I'm a BIV" are true iff I'm a BIV*.
- (P3) If I am a BIV, I'm not a BIV*.
- (C1) So if I am a BIV, those utterances are false.
- (P4) If I am not a BIV, those utterances are true iff I'm a BIV.
- (C3) So if I am not a BIV, those utterances are false.
- (C) My utterances of "I'm a BIV" are false.

(C) plus closure under competent deduction does **not** yield (*) *I know that I'm not a BIV*. To get (*) one needs (A) my utterances of "I'm not a BIV" are true, and (B) those utterances are true iff I'm not a BIV. (B) assumes **disquotation**. Vat-English is not disquotational; English is. BIVs cannot speak English. So the argument "presupposes what it seeks to prove: that I'm not a BIV." Brueckner: "Can I claim to know this without assuming that I am speaking English?"

**§6.3 Wright’s reconstruction.** Crispin Wright (1992) keeps the two core assumptions (the arguer's language is disquotational; in the BIV's language "BIV" does not refer to BIVs) but treats **disquotation as the less problematic** one. Putnam (1994) said Wright put "more clearly than I myself did … the premises and the deductive steps involved in my argument." Wright's first-person version:

1. In my language, "BIV" is meaningful.
2. So (with disquotation) "BIV" refers to BIVs.
3. In the BIV's language, "BIV" does not refer to BIVs; so my language is not the BIV's.
4. If I am a BIV, my language is the BIV's.
5. Therefore I am not a BIV.

The third-person version (replace "I" with "Putnam") is, Wright says, "altogether incredible" as an a priori proof that *Putnam* is not a BIV. From the fact that Putnam's language is disquotational and that "BIV" is meaningful in it, an assessor can infer only that a specification *in Putnam's language* would be **homophonic**. That is the same as "in Putnam's language, 'BIV' refers to BIVs" only if the assessor's language *is* Putnam's. Homophony does not yield sameness of meaning — "this point flows from semantic externalism." For all the assessor knows, "BIV" in Putnam's language means *Frenchman*. The needed additional information (AI) — that the argument was formulated in Putnam's language and that Putnam's language coincides with the assessor's — is just the kind of **identifying knowledge of content** that externalism "itself proscribes." Wright: "fully exorcising the sceptical doubt … would not require precisely the kind of identifying knowledge of content which semantic externalism itself proscribes."

**§6.4 The transcendental reading.** Wright (1992: 85) and Putnam (1981b: 16) both call the argument **transcendental**. Putnam: "my procedure has a close relation to what Kant called a ‘transcendental’ investigation; for it is an investigation … of the pre-conditions of reference and hence of thought — preconditions built in to the nature of our minds themselves, though not (as Kant hoped) wholly independent of empirical assumptions." Reconstruction:

- (T1) A necessary condition of the possibility of thinking that I'm not a BIV is that I'm not a BIV.
- (T2) I can think that I'm not a BIV.
- (TC) I'm not a BIV.

The sentence "I'm not a BIV" is **ambiguous**: in vat-English it is true iff I'm not a BIV-in-the-image; in English iff I'm not a BIV. Either way the sentence is true. "The pressing question is which true proposition are we expressing?" Brueckner (2010: 95): the argument is **epistemically circular**. If I do not yet know which world I am in, I do not know which truth-conditions and contents are mine. "In order to know, or have justification for believing, the argument’s self-knowledge premise … I need to know, or have justification for believing, its conclusion." That undercuts the advertised benefit of a transcendental argument: a self-knowledge premise even the skeptic should accept.

**§6.5 Stroud’s dilemma.** Barry Stroud (1968, 2000) argues that transcendental arguments generally are anti-skeptically ineffective. Three moves:

1. **Hedging.** A mind-to-world thesis (having psychological states with content S requires that S is true) can always be hedged to a mind-to-mind thesis: it is enough if we *believe* S, or if it looks for all the world as if S. Applied here: a necessary condition of thinking one is not a BIV is only that one must *not seem* to be a BIV. The skeptic grants that and still denies knowledge.
2. **Bridge of necessity.** To get from seeming-not-a-BIV to not-being-a-BIV one needs idealism, verificationism, or some anti-realist principle. The BIV hypothesis is built so that appearances can come apart from a mind-independent world.
3. **Sufficiency.** Those anti-realist principles already block skepticism, so the transcendental argument is superfluous.

Pritchard and Ranalli's reply: [[Concepts/Semantic Externalism (Putnam)|content externalism]] can cross the bridge **without** idealism. Externalist contents are world-dependent; idealism makes the world mind-dependent. Brueckner (1999: 230) is quoted: "the existence of content-bearing mental states requires that non-psychological reality be a certain way. But its being that way is not constituted by some sustaining psychological reality." A residual worry: if "I'm not a BIV" is always verifiable (true in English, true in vat-English), is that a collapse into verificationism? They say no: no *general* verificationist principle is invoked; only some special sentences always come out true. Parallel drawn to Davidson's later transcendental anti-skepticism (not developed).

**§6.6 Ineffable skeptical hypotheses.** [[Thinkers/Thomas Nagel]] (*The View from Nowhere*, 1986) gives two criticisms.

1. **Reductio of semantic externalism.** "The evident possibility and intelligibility of skepticism" refutes theories on which "tree" must mean whatever is causally responsible for tree-impressions. "Since those things could conceivably not be trees, any theory that says they have to be is wrong." Worse, the argument **exacerbates** skepticism: "If I accept the argument, I must conclude that a brain in a vat can’t think truly that it is a brain in a vat, even though others can think this about it. … Instead I must say ‘Perhaps I can’t even think the truth about what I am, because I lack the necessary concepts and my circumstances make it impossible for me to acquire them!’ If this doesn’t qualify as skepticism, I don’t know what does."
2. **Ineffable hypotheses.** The BIV scenario is a **template** for "limitless possibilities that we can’t imagine." Traditional skeptical possibilities "stand for" ones we cannot conceive. Even a successful reply to the stipulated lifelong-computer BIV leaves those untouched.

Nagel also attacks Putnam's causal theory of reference directly (the skeptic need not explain how terms refer without the referents existing; the skeptic is not refuted "unless reason has been given to believe such an account impossible"). Pritchard and Ranalli note Zagzebski (2009) as a defence of that line. They do not adjudicate.

## Key Claims / Positions

- Putnam's result is semantic (one cannot *truly think* one is a BIV), not the epistemological claim that one can *know* one is not.
- The argument requires lifelong envattment by supercomputers; it does not touch recent envattment.
- Brueckner: to get from "the sentence is false" to "I know I am not a BIV" one must assume disquotation, which assumes one speaks English.
- Wright: the worse assumption is the anti-disquotation of vat-English; the third-person version exposes a demand for identifying knowledge of content that externalism itself problematizes.
- The transcendental reading still faces Brueckner's circularity: one does not know *which* true proposition "I am not a BIV" expresses.
- Stroud's dilemma can be resisted if content externalism supplies a mind-to-world bridge without idealism; the authors take that to be open.
- Nagel: if the argument works it is a reductio; if it works only against this hypothesis, ineffable skeptical hypotheses remain; and the residual thought ("perhaps I cannot even think what I am") *is* skepticism.
